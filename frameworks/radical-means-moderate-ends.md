# Radical Means, Moderate Ends

### A structural reform agenda for the 2029 trifecta

---

## I. The thesis

The sentence that organizes everything is one Pete Buttigieg said once, in passing, on a bench in Traverse City, and then never returned to:

> To get to any kind of common-sensical — some would say moderate — economic policy, we have to have dramatic political change.

That is an inversion, and it is the whole argument. The *ends* here are unremarkable. Paid leave. Lower drug prices. A carbon price. Child care. Each of these polls at or above sixty percent and none of them can clear the machine. The American problem is not that the public wants the wrong things. It is that the mechanism cannot convert what the public wants into law.

Which means the radicalism has to live in the mechanism. This is not a program of bold policy. It is a program of boring policy and radical procedure — and it is *conservative* in the only sense that matters, because it aims to make the government capable of doing the ordinary things governments do.

The corollary is Buttigieg's other insight, the one he stated more forcefully: **there is no going back.** Not to 2021, not to 2012, not to 1992. The institutions that were demolished should not be reconstructed as they looked, because the versions that were demolished were already failing — that is substantially why they were vulnerable. The wrecking crew burned the wrong things down in the wrong ways, and the resulting state is one where you can build something new in a way that has not been possible in a very long time. What gets built in the next four years is what the country lives with for fifty.

His instinct here is Birch Bayh's, and the lineage matters: a pragmatic Midwestern temperament that arrives at radical structural change not despite its pragmatism but because of it. Two amendments ratified, a third within two states, and an Electoral College abolition that cleared the House overwhelmingly in 1970 and died to a Senate filibuster. Sometimes a pragmatic mindset takes you to the place of dramatic change.

---

## II. What is actually broken

**Conversion, not preference.** Majorities exist. They do not convert. The Senate is malapportioned on a worsening demographic trend line and cannot be reapportioned. The House is capped by a 1929 statute at a size that makes districts three times the OECD norm and gerrymandering maximally efficient. The Court has functionally unreviewable authority over the rules of politics itself. Campaign finance is constitutionalized against regulation. Each of these is a conversion failure, and each compounds the others.

**Depletion, not accumulation.** Political capital does not bank. It drains — through year one, monotonically, regardless of performance. The window for structural change is at its widest on inauguration day and narrows continuously. Any strategy premised on earning capital early to spend later has the physics backwards.

**And the geometry that governs everything: speed and durability are inversely related, and every clever workaround sits on the fast-fragile end.**

| Instrument | Speed | Durability |
|---|---|---|
| Executive order | Days | Reversed in a day |
| Reconciliation | Weeks | Sunsets by design |
| Interstate compact | Sessions | Any member withdraws |
| Regular-order statute | Months | Repealed by the next trifecta |
| Constitutional amendment | A generation | Effectively permanent |

There is no fast-durable quadrant. This is the strongest available defense of Buttigieg's claim that the restoration model was disproven rather than merely underambitious. Every major win of the last cycle sat on the fast-fragile end — the rescue plan expired on schedule, the climate credits are being unwound, the executive actions reversed on day one. The failure was not insufficient policy content. It was instrument selection that guaranteed nothing would outlive the next election.

This is also why the base-hit track cannot be the terminal answer. The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact stands at 222 electoral votes after Virginia's April 2026 enactment — genuinely close, and undoable by any one legislature. It is a deposit on an amendment, not a substitute for one.

## III. The architecture

Four rules govern what goes in and in what order.

**1. Every base hit is a deposit on a big swing, never a substitute.**
Buttigieg's own framing, sharpened by a test: does the incremental version *build toward* the structural version, or relieve the pressure for it? Ranked-choice voting builds the administrative capacity and public familiarity that single transferable vote requires — a deposit. Montana-style charter forfeiture builds the legal theory that artificial persons are not citizens, in live posture — a deposit. Small-dollar public financing is enormously valuable and builds toward nothing; it is a parallel track, and calling it progress toward the amendment is how the incremental track quietly becomes the terminal one.

**2. The reciprocity test.** Every reform must be one you would accept while in the minority. Term limits pass. House enlargement passes. Public financing passes. Court expansion by bare partisan majority fails — it deepens the legitimacy collapse it claims to answer and installs a retaliation ratchet. Expansion therefore belongs in the agenda only as a *pre-committed consequence*: statutory term limits first, expansion triggered solely by judicial invalidation of the term-limits statute. That puts the trigger in the Court's own hands and converts an aggression into a deterrent.

**3. The pairing rule.** No structural reform travels alone. Enlargement ships alongside the constituent service it makes possible; campaign finance alongside drug pricing. The engine reform and the output it enables get announced in the same sentence, every time. Otherwise the agenda confirms the suspicion that it is proceduralism wearing populism's clothes — which is the single most likely way this fails.

**4. Constitutionalize procedure and capacity. Never substantive policy.**
Amendments that entrench outcomes invite the opposition to entrench theirs. Amendments that fix the rules of conversion are the ones that survive changing majorities, because both sides can eventually live with them.

## IV. The agenda

### Tier 0 — Statutory, first Congress

*The enabling move.* **Standing Rules reform.** Frame as restoration, not abolition: the 1975 reduction from 67 to 60 is the usable precedent, and it happened in Bayh's Senate. Cleanest form is a restored talking filibuster with the burden shifted to 41 senators present and voting. Second-best is a democracy-and-elections carve-out on the 2013 and 2017 nominations model. Say plainly, on the record, that you would accept the same rules in the minority.

*Representation.*
- **Apportionment Act.** House to roughly 585 on the Wyoming rule, phased over three cycles. Pure statute — the 1929 cap is not constitutional. Four floor days for the largest structural change available, and it cuts Electoral College malapportionment by roughly half as a side effect. The single most underpriced item on the board.
- **Fair Representation Act.** Repeal 2 U.S.C. §2c; multi-member districts of three to five seats elected by *single transferable vote* — stated precisely, because plurality bloc voting in multi-member districts is worse than the status quo. Commission the §2 compliance modeling in advance rather than litigating it after.
- **Freedom to Vote Act.** Independent redistricting commissions under Elections Clause authority, which *Rucho* expressly invited and *Moore v. Harper* protected. Small-dollar matching at 6:1 with voluntary spending caps, DISCLOSE, FEC restructuring to break the 3–3 deadlock.
- **DC Admission** and a binding **Puerto Rico** plebiscite with pre-commitment to honor the result.

*The judiciary.*
- **Federal Judgeships Act** — the sixty-six district seats the Judicial Conference already requested. Two floor days, bipartisan cover.
- **Judicial Term Limits Act** — eighteen-year active terms implemented through senior status, one appointment per Congress, Article III tenure and salary preserved, rotation to circuit duty.
- **Ethics and recusal** with an enforceable review panel.
- Random district-wide case assignment; codified standards for nationwide relief.

*Capacity — the load-bearing item nobody puts on these lists.*
- **Civil Service Modernization.** Schedule F's damage is not answered by restoring a merit system that had neither real performance management nor real political insulation. This is a prerequisite, not a good-government footnote: every ramp time in the delivery agenda assumes it passed.
- **Agency Successor Act.** Development and education functions rebuilt in new form. The enforcement functions are separable from the policy functions and probably should not be reassembled in the same box.

### Tier 1 — The state track, which is the primary track

- **NPVIC to 270.** Forty-eight votes remain. The path runs through Pennsylvania, Michigan, Arizona, and one of Wisconsin or Nevada — and every one of those legislatures is on this November's ballot. Two things must ship with it: proactive congressional consent sought *before* activation rather than litigated during a crisis, and a tightened withdrawal blackout, which is the compact's softest joint.
- **Ranked-choice and open primaries, rebranded.** The 2024 losses in Nevada, Colorado, Oregon, and Idaho were framing losses. Alaska's system survived because it ran as anti-corruption and veteran-adjacent, not as good government. Democracy-reform framing consistently underperforms anti-corruption framing with exactly the voters this coalition needs.
- **Anti-dark-money initiatives** in every state with a viable initiative process.

### Tier 2 — Amendments, ordered by ratification coalition rather than importance

The first ratification is the proof of concept that makes the rest thinkable, so lead with whichever coalition is *least* correlated with partisan control.

1. **Anti-corruption / campaign finance.** Roughly 75–80% support, genuinely cross-partisan, no small-state disadvantage. If this one cannot reach thirty-eight, none can — which makes it the diagnostic as well as the pathfinder.
2. **Judicial term limits**, constitutionalizing the statute and mooting the Article III objection permanently.
3. **National popular vote**, claiming the Bayh-Celler lineage explicitly. The real opposition is seven swing states, not twenty-five small ones — small states are already ignored, and saying so is the argument.
4. **Senate reform, done legally.** Article V's equal suffrage proviso makes reapportionment impossible without unanimous consent, and the two-step workaround is bad faith that would poison everything else. The available big swing is a **suspensive veto** on the Parliament Act model: each state keeps its two senators and its equal suffrage, and the Senate simply stops being an absolute veto — the House overrides rejection by supermajority after a defined delay. This is the largest structural gain legally available and also the most contested; the proviso arguably protects effective voice rather than seat count, and that question would be resolved by a Court the opposition appointed. High variance, and it belongs on the list anyway.
5. **An affirmative individual right to vote.** There isn't one. *Bush v. Gore* said so out loud.

*On mechanics:* hold the Article V convention path as leverage even if you never intend to use it. The Seventeenth Amendment moved through Congress largely because a convention call sat a state or two from triggering. Subject-limited applications with pre-committed rescission manage the runaway risk while preserving the pressure.

## V. Sequencing: what the floor calendar actually permits

**Two rails, not one.** The most common error in reform agendas is assuming structural work and material delivery compete. They do not. Reconciliation is a privileged vehicle with capped debate — it costs the same fifteen floor days whether it carries one provision or forty, and everything Byrd-compliant inside it is free at the margin. Structural reform rides the *regular* floor, which rules reform is what makes affordable. **Delivery rides reconciliation. Structure rides the floor that rules reform frees.** Announce them as one program; schedule them on separate rails.

**The inherited slot is the largest single lever.** Budget resolutions are one per fiscal year, not two per Congress. A divided Congress cannot adopt one at all — it requires concurrent adoption in both chambers. So flipping either chamber in November 2026 does not merely seat the members who would vote on rules reform in 2029; it **freezes the FY2029 slot** for whoever wins in 2028. An inherited resolution can move a reconciliation bill by week eight, as it did in February and March of 2021. An originated one takes until week thirty. That difference is worth more than any other scheduling choice available.

**Byrd eligibility, not ideology, sorts the agenda** — and this deserves to be said out loud, because it explains a great deal about American policy that is usually attributed to preference or lobbying. What can ride reconciliation is whatever moves money. What cannot is whatever changes a rule. The procedural architecture therefore privileges *transfers over regulations*, invisibly, at the level of what is schedulable rather than what is popular. It is a large part of why this country does tax credits instead of mandates and a submerged welfare state instead of a legible one.

**Stock beats flow beats nothing.** Voters weight the recent past heavily. A one-time payment in month five is nearly spent as a political asset by November 2030. A monthly payment that began in month five and is *still arriving* on election day collects both the establishment effect and the recency effect. Paid leave is episodic by nature and decays; the child credit and the dividends do not. The scheduling rule is not "go early" — it is **recurring beats one-time**, and the reason to go early is ramp time and capital depletion, not accumulation.

**Legibility is a drafting choice and it is free.** A junk fee ban reaches a hundred million households and almost nobody notices a fee they were not charged. Drug pricing is felt at the counter and rarely attributed to anyone. The IRA's out-of-pocket cap took effect days before the administration that passed it left office. Naming the program, routing it through one visible channel, writing attribution into the statute, mailing something physical — none of this costs floor days, and all of it is routinely traded away in conference for administrative convenience.

**What is underpriced.** Sorted by result per floor day, the usual ranking inverts. Two days for a junk fee ban. Four days for House enlargement. Both routinely queued behind marquee bills costing four times the floor time that land after the election.

## VI. What delivery can and cannot buy

Two scoreboards, in different currencies, resolving on different dates.

**Persuasion return.** A full, well-scheduled delivery agenda is worth roughly *five House seats* against a twenty-six-seat postwar midterm headwind. It reduces a loss; it does not produce a win. And it is asymmetric: backlash tracks what you pass, while reward tracks what people feel and attribute — so an agenda passed at full volume and delivered too late scores **worse than passing nothing**. That is the IRA's shape precisely, and the 2010 shape: the largest social legislation in forty years, sixty-three seats lost.

**Conversion return.** Structural reform is worth zero in 2030 and roughly twenty-seven permanent House seats at an unchanged national vote share from 2032 onward — redistricting commissions, a larger House, proportional districts, two new states.

Neither half justifies itself alone. Structure without delivery loses the majority that would defend it. Delivery without structure wins a few seats inside a conversion system that keeps eating them. **You need the delivery to survive long enough to keep the structure.** That is the whole case for doing both, and it is stronger than either argument by itself.

One uncomfortable geometry: rules reform is the mandatory first act and has no constituency. It is the prerequisite for everything and the one item no voter will ever feel. The only resolution the calendar offers is that it does not have to be *alone* — the rules resolution and the first reconciliation bill move simultaneously on separate rails, so the opening looks like delivery while the structural work happens underneath. That is not spin; it is the actual schedule. But it means the program's first public act has to be the checks, not the reform, and reform advocates instinctively want it the other way around.

## VII. Why 2026 is the pivot

The seats contested this November are Class 2 — seated through January 2033. **The senators elected in three months are the exact body that would vote on rules reform, House enlargement, the Fair Representation Act, and statehood in 2029.** And 2028 is Class 3, last contested in 2022, which means the presidential cycle is spent defending Arizona, Georgia, Nevada, New Hampshire, and Pennsylvania. This is the building year. That one is the holding year.

Three implications:

**The marginal senators may be the best ones on this agenda.** The seats at the margin run through Alaska, Nebraska, Montana, and Iowa. Alaska already uses top-four open primaries and ranked-choice general elections; Nebraska and Montana would seat independents elected against both party machines. Those members are plausibly *better* on electoral-system reform than the median Democrat, even if more cautious on everything else. Build the whip strategy around that rather than treating them as the obstacle.

**The compact and the majority are the same operation.** Pennsylvania, Michigan, Arizona, Wisconsin, and Nevada are simultaneously the NPVIC's remaining path and states with legislative chambers on this ballot. The marginal cost of adding compact commitments to state legislative candidate questionnaires this fall is approximately zero — and the window matters, because a candidate will sign in August while the compact is an abstraction and will not sign in March 2027 when it is two states from activation and the national press has arrived.

**The 2027–28 Senate is preparatory, not legislative.** It faces a veto until January 2029. Its three jobs: build the hearing record that House enlargement and STV will need in 2029 rather than improvising it under time pressure; extract public rules-reform commitments while they are still free, because every reform that dies in 2029 dies because someone was never asked in 2027; and state the reciprocal standard on nominations out loud, which costs nothing then and determines whether judicial term limits read as reform or as revenge later.

---

## VIII. What we do not know

The conversion seat estimates for proportional districts and commissions are the shakiest numbers here and want real modeling against district geography. The Senate suspensive veto rests on a contested reading of Article V. The thermostatic backlash term is real in the literature and loosely pinned in magnitude. And every model behind this document treats a dynamic bargaining process as a static allocation — no capital depletion, no opposition response, no exogenous shock.

The binding constraint remains the 2028 presidential election, and nothing here changes that.

But the ordering is defensible, the legal load-bearing is honest about where it is weak, and the thesis survives contact with the arithmetic: **the ends are moderate, the means have to be radical, and the reason is not ideology but conversion.** Things that cannot go on forever end in either reform or catastrophe. The only variable is which.
