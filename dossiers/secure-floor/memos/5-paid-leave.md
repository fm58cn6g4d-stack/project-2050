# Memo 5: Paid family and medical leave

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 7 of the secure floor (`../README.md`, "Forks for S3"; `../3-design.md`). Status: draft for decision (board row S3); nothing here is decided. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **B, twelve weeks of national paid leave run by SSA, with the 2025 FAMILY Act's progressive replacement (85%, 69% and 50% in bands) and job restoration, financed by the legacy contribution (0.6% of wages, stepping up toward the 1.10% ceiling you approved), with states that already do as well keeping their programs.** Cost: about **$0.9 trillion of benefits over FY2030-39 (range $0.6-1.4 trillion), about $130 billion in FY2040 (range $90-200 billion)**, paid from the leave fund; the general fund bears the income-tax offset and any temporary Treasury loan. Evidence: quasi-experimental studies of state programs; microsimulation for cost.

**This memo decides paid leave's weeks, benefit, job protection, financing and structure.** You have already approved two pieces on October 26: SSA as administrator and the 1.10% ceiling (`legacy/governing/project-2050-governing-decisions.md`) [EV-SEC-693]. They are working choices here, not reopened unless you wish.

## The question

The legacy design (FAM-3A) is twelve weeks of social insurance at "about two-thirds" of wages, a 0.6% contribution stepping up toward 1.10%, and a repayable Treasury loan if claims outrun the contribution [EV-SEC-693]. Since then the 2025 FAMILY Act set a more progressive benefit and job restoration [EV-SEC-340] [EV-SEC-342]. The question is which benefit, which protections and which structure: a federal program, or a floor that states meet.

## Why it matters

- **Most workers have no paid family leave.** 27% of private-sector workers had it in 2023, 13% in the bottom wage quarter (BLS) [EV-SEC-300] [EV-SEC-301].
- **Low earners cannot afford to take unpaid leave, and lose jobs when they do.** 66% of workers who needed leave and did not take it could not afford to; 45% feared losing their job; 18% of low-wage leave-takers lost a job (Abt for DOL, 2020) [EV-SEC-307] [EV-SEC-308].
- **Thirteen states run programs, covering about a third of private workers** (CRS, 2026; National Partnership for Women & Families) [EV-SEC-309] [EV-SEC-322].
- **The United States is the only OECD country with no national paid maternity leave** (OECD) [EV-SEC-375].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Paid leave raises leave-taking, most of all for fathers** (quasi-experimental): California doubled mothers' leave (Rossin-Slater, Ruhm and Waldfogel) [EV-SEC-355] and raised fathers' by 46% [EV-SEC-362]; reserved weeks raised Norway's fathers' take-up from 3% to 35% [EV-SEC-379].
2. **It improves health** (quasi-experimental): fewer infant hospital admissions (Pihl and Basso) [EV-SEC-366] and 11% less nursing-home use by older people (Arora and Wolf) [EV-SEC-368].
3. **Its long-run effect on mothers' careers is contested** (both sides, section 2, part C.1): the largest tax-record study finds no gain and some harm for first-time mothers (Bailey and others) [EV-SEC-357].
4. **Low earners claim least** (administrative data): California's lowest-earning quarter and small-firm workers are the least likely to take leave [EV-SEC-372]. A progressive formula is the main lever, but no study yet shows it raises their take-up [EV-SEC-666].
5. **A capped contribution can fall behind claims** (program record): Washington's rate rose from 0.40% to its 1.2% cap, and its actuary finds the cap too low [EV-SEC-323] [EV-SEC-325] [EV-SEC-327].

## Options

**A. The legacy design (FAM-3A) as written.**
- **What it does.** Twelve weeks; replacement starting at about two-thirds of wages up to a cap; SSA; 0.6% contribution split evenly, stepping up 0.05 points a year when the reserve runs low, to 1.10%; a repayable Treasury loan beyond that [EV-SEC-693].
- *Cost:* about $0.9 trillion of benefits over FY2030-39, range $0.6-1.4 trillion, by take-up [EV-SEC-693]. At high take-up, the loan peaks near $185 billion and is repaid in 2058 (legacy recovery budget) [EV-SEC-694].
- *Gap:* "about two-thirds" is the 2019 bill's rate [EV-SEC-344], less generous to low earners than the 2025 text, and the legacy file sets no job protection.

**B. A as updated to the 2025 FAMILY Act (recommended).**
- **What it does.**
  - *Benefit:* 85% of monthly earnings up to the first band, 69% up to the second and 50% up to the third, roughly $580 to $4,000 a month in 2026 (S. 2823) [EV-SEC-340].
  - *Weeks:* twelve a year, for a new child, a family member's serious illness or one's own [EV-SEC-340].
  - *Job restoration* at any employer after 90 days of work, and health coverage kept during leave [EV-SEC-342]. Oregon uses a 90-day line [EV-SEC-313].
  - *Financing:* the legacy contribution and step-up toward 1.10% [EV-SEC-693]. The household cost: $180 a year for a $60,000 worker at 0.6%, up to $330 at the ceiling (legacy household test) [EV-SEC-676].
  - *States:* a state whose program pays at least as well for at least twelve weeks keeps it and receives the federal contribution for its workers, as the 2025 bill's "legacy States" grants provide [EV-SEC-343]. Workers elsewhere are in the federal program from day one.
  - *SSA administers*, with an office for paid leave [EV-SEC-341].
- *Where tried:* thirteen state programs, most with progressive formulas (CRS, 2026) [EV-SEC-311]; Washington's replaces up to 90% for low earners [EV-SEC-311].
- *For people:* a home health aide earning $2,400 a month after a birth gets about $1,850 a month for twelve weeks and her job back (project arithmetic on the 2026 bands [EV-SEC-340]); under A she would get about $1,600.
- *Cost:* about $0.9 trillion over FY2030-39 (range $0.6-1.4 trillion) and about $130 billion in FY2040 (range $90-200 billion) (judgment: the legacy take-up cases, with the progressive bands roughly cost-neutral on average because they pay less at the top).
  - *Benchmarks:* Urban's microsimulation of the progressive FAMILY Act puts benefits at about 0.46% of taxable earnings, about $43 billion a year (Urban Institute for DOL's Women's Bureau, 2024) [EV-SEC-660] [EV-SEC-661]; IWPR at under half a percent of payroll (2018) [EV-SEC-664]. CBO and JCT put the 2019 bill at $547 billion over 2020-30 [EV-SEC-344]. These sit at the legacy range's low and middle; the high case, a mature 1.01% of payroll, is what the 1.10% ceiling is built for.
  - *Who gains:* Urban projects 8.8% of the lowest earners take benefits against 2.5% of the highest [EV-SEC-662], and poverty falls over 16% among families receiving them [EV-SEC-663].
- *Political risk:* a new payroll contribution, which opponents will call a tax on every worker. Republican alternatives fund leave from families' own future credits or Social Security [EV-SEC-353] [EV-SEC-354].
- *Burden:* one claim to SSA, with a medical or birth record.

**C. A floor that states meet (the House bipartisan approach).**
- **What it does.** Grants of $1.5-7 million to states to build their own programs and interstate networks; no federal benefit (H.R. 3089) [EV-SEC-351].
- *Cost:* small, under $1 billion a year (judgment).
- *Consequence:* workers in the states without a program, most of them, keep none until their state acts. The grants are far below what a program costs.

**D. Four weeks, from general revenue (FAM-3C).**
- **What it does.** Build Back Better's design: up to four weeks [EV-SEC-347].
- *Cost:* CBO about $205 billion over ten years for the 2021 bill (via CRFB) [EV-SEC-346]; about $0.3 trillion over FY2030-39 (legacy) [EV-SEC-693].
- *Consequence:* four weeks is a fraction of the OECD's 18.5-week maternity average [EV-SEC-375] and of most state programs; it leaves leave on the general fund.

**Fiscal rule** (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`): the leave fund pays its benefits; the general fund bears the income-tax offset (the legacy allowance is 12.5% of receipts) and lends at the ceiling. Both are reported to the financing settlement.

## Recommendation: B

- **It pays most to those who can least afford unpaid leave,** with the bands the 2025 bill sets, and it brings their job back, which 45% of workers with unmet need feared losing [EV-SEC-307].
- **It keeps the financing you approved,** with headroom above Washington's mistake [EV-SEC-327].
- **It keeps states that already do as well,** so no worker in California or New Jersey loses a program that works.
- **Its case rests on leave-taking, fathers' share and health,** where the evidence is consistent, not on mothers' long-run earnings, where it is contested.

**What would change this:** a financing settlement that wants leave on the general fund (toward the President's FY2025 shape); evidence that SSA cannot staff it by 2031 (a delayed start, not a smaller program); a study showing progressive bands do not raise low earners' take-up (keep them for fairness, revisit outreach).

## If you decide

- **Under B:** section 4 writes a new Social Security Act title in the 2029 reconciliation bill: benefits from 2031 (the legacy ramp), the contribution from 2030, the legacy-state rule, job restoration. Job restoration is a labor standard; the work flagship is told. The budget model's paid leave line (now at the 0.85% cap in its inputs, `../legacy-research.md`, 6.5) is updated in tier 3 by the lead.
- **Under C or D:** as described; D needs general-fund financing.
- **Not proposed:** weeks reserved for each parent, the clearest lever on fathers' take-up abroad [EV-SEC-379] [EV-SEC-380], because with twelve weeks a person it would not add leave; the household model can test it in section 7.
