# Memo 6: Social Security: solvency, the minimum benefit, cost-of-living and caregivers

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 8 of the secure floor (`../README.md`, "Forks for S3"; `../3-design.md`). Social Security travels in its own bill: section 310(g) of the Budget Act keeps it out of reconciliation [EV-SEC-476], and it is financed by its own trust funds. Status: draft for decision (board row S3); nothing here is decided. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Effects are in percent of taxable payroll over 75 years, the actuaries' measure; a plus closes the gap.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **part 1 C, the D8 package with SS-5, closed with a partial investment of the reserves in a broad stock index (SS-8), and a broad one-point rate rise (SS-7) named as the fallback; part 2, a minimum benefit at 125% of poverty (yes), CPI-E for the cost-of-living adjustment (yes) and a caregiver earnings credit for up to five years that tops up actual earnings, paid care hours counting as earnings (yes, pending SSA's score); part 3, pay full benefits on time if depletion arrives before the law (yes).** Cost: trust-fund effects of about **+4.2 to +4.7% of payroll from part 1 against a 4.42% gap, and about −0.5 to −0.6 from part 2** (legacy estimates on SSA's 2026 single-provision scores; no whole-package score exists). Evidence: SSA's actuaries for the pieces; official data on what Social Security does.

**This memo decides how the plan closes Social Security's gap and which benefit improvements it carries.** Revenue pieces SS-1, SS-2 and SS-5 are Social Security's own financing; the financing settlement weighs them with the plan's other taxes.

## The question

The retirement fund can pay full benefits until the fourth quarter of 2032, then 78% (2026 Trustees) [EV-SEC-400]. The 75-year gap is 4.42% of payroll [EV-SEC-402]. Before October 28 you chose D8: solvency first with a targeted increase, as its own bill in spring 2030 [EV-SEC-677], and on October 26 added SS-5, the tax on S-corporation and partnership income [EV-SEC-696]. Those are working choices here (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`). The questions are whether the package closes the gap on the 2026 report, what closes the rest, which benefit improvements to carry, and what happens if depletion comes first.

## Why it matters

- **It is the largest anti-poverty program.** It moved 28.8 million people out of poverty in 2025 (Census) [EV-SEC-420]. 39% of men and 44% of women 65 and over get half or more of their income from it (SSA) [EV-SEC-418].
- **The cut at depletion is large.** About $16,900 a year for a typical couple retiring in 2033 (CRFB) [EV-SEC-406]; CBO projects a larger cut, 26%, and the retirement fund's exhaustion in fiscal 2032 [EV-SEC-407] [EV-SEC-409].
- **The plan's first Congress is the last with time to act.** Depletion falls in its second Congress, near the 2032 election.

The gap grew 0.60 points in a year, mostly from lower assumed fertility [EV-SEC-403], so the 2027 and 2028 reports may move it again (section 6).

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Benefits cut elderly poverty about one for one** (quasi-experimental): Social Security explains the whole fall in elderly poverty from 1967 to 2000 (Engelhardt and Gruber) [EV-SEC-442] [EV-SEC-443].
2. **Every serious plan closes most of the gap from high earnings** (actuarial scores): removing the cap without a benefit credit closes 58% (SSA) [EV-SEC-448]; the 2023 Social Security 2100 Act moved depletion to 2066 (SSA's chief actuary) [EV-SEC-454].
3. **Revenue-only and benefit-cut packages differ in their economic effects in one model** (simulation): Penn Wharton's seven packages each close about 4.78% of payroll; 2060 GDP is 0.4% above baseline with tax-only and 12.1% above with benefit-only (Penn Wharton Budget Model, 2026) [EV-SEC-671]. That model's view is one side; it assumes large saving responses.
4. **Today's special minimum benefit reaches almost no one** (program data): about 32,000 of 64 million beneficiaries in 2019 (CRS) [EV-SEC-439].
5. **Caregiving years cut pensions, and peer countries credit them** (international practice): a five-year break cuts pensions 5% at the average wage (OECD) [EV-SEC-479]; Germany credits three years per child at the average wage [EV-SEC-478].

## Part 1. Solvency

**A. D8 with SS-5 (the legacy package).**
- *Effect:* about +3.7, 84% of the gap; depletion about 2081; about 0.7% of payroll left [EV-SEC-696].
- *Who pays:* earnings above $250,000, investment income above $400,000 and pass-through business income above $200,000 or $250,000 [EV-SEC-452]; no one below.
- *Consequence:* the trust fund is solvent for about fifty years, not seventy-five, and the actuaries will still report a gap.

**B. A, with a broad rate rise (SS-7).**
- *Effect:* SS-7, one point phased over 10-20 years, adds +0.9 to +1.0 (legacy estimate) [EV-SEC-697], enough to close the 75-year gap.
- *Who pays:* every covered worker: about $300 a year at maturity for a $60,000 worker, half the point (project arithmetic).
- *Consequence:* full 75-year solvency, at the cost of a middle-class tax your October 26 revenue direction steers away from.

**C. A, with a partial investment of the reserves in stocks (SS-8; recommended).**
- *Effect:* +0.5 to +1.0 (legacy estimate) [EV-SEC-697]: at or near 75-year balance with A.
- *What it does:* part of the trust fund's reserves, which A builds up in the 2030s and 2040s, goes into a broad, passively managed index fund under an independent board, as the bipartisan Cassidy-Kaine fund proposes in a different form [EV-SEC-466].
- *Who pays:* no one directly; it carries market risk, which the fallback covers.
- *Evidence:* an estimate, not an SSA score; the Cassidy-Kaine sponsors claim their fund covers 65-75% of the shortfall, extrapolating stock returns from 2003 to 2021, which a MarketWatch columnist argues overstates likely returns (Hulbert, 2026) [EV-SEC-467] [EV-SEC-469].
- *Fallback:* if the next Trustees report shows a gap after A and C, SS-7's rate rise starts, phased (point 11). That is the legacy file's own suggestion [EV-SEC-696].

**D. The bipartisan track first (SS-10A), D8 as the fallback.**
- *What it does:* negotiate on the Warren-Moreno framework of lifting the cap [EV-SEC-461], with Republicans' asks (a donut to $400,000, a lower accrual for top earners, no investment-income tax) in exchange for 67 votes.
- *Evidence:* no bill had been introduced by November 2026 [EV-SEC-462]; three of 20 Republican Senate candidates endorsed raising or removing the cap [EV-SEC-464]. The legacy scenario's "solvent to 2081" is unscored [EV-SEC-696].
- *Consequence:* a more durable law if it passes, but it drops SS-2 (about +1.2) and spends the time before late 2032 on a negotiation.
- *Also on the table:* a commission (the PROMISE Act, S. 4979, bipartisan) [EV-SEC-470], the 1983 route.

## Part 2. Benefits

| Piece | Options | Effect | Recommended |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Minimum benefit** (SS-3) | 125% of poverty for 30-year workers, wage-indexed, against none | −0.11 [EV-SEC-445] | **Yes** |
| **Cost-of-living index** (SS-4) | CPI-E [EV-SEC-455]; the higher of CPI-W and CPI-E, as the 2100 Act does [EV-SEC-458]; current CPI-W | CPI-E −0.33; "higher of" somewhat more (not scored in the register) | **CPI-E.** It has grown about 0.2 points a year faster [EV-SEC-440], though it is experimental, with a larger sampling error (CRS) [EV-SEC-667] |
| **Caregiver credit** (care's memo 9 left it here) | (a) none; (b) dropout years for parents of children under 6, −0.06 per year dropped (SSA, B4.4) [EV-SEC-445], as Canada does [EV-SEC-480]; (c) an earnings credit at half the average wage for up to five years of care for a child under 6 or a dependent relative, on the 2100 Act's model of 960 hours a year [EV-SEC-460] | (c): about $1,172 more a year in retirement for a typical caregiver (Boston College's Center for Retirement Research, 2024) [EV-SEC-673]; not scored by SSA; judgment −0.1 to −0.2 | **(c), pending SSA's score**; (b) if the score is high |

- **The caregiver credit and paid family care.** Care decided that relatives can be paid for care: in Medicaid now, and through the long-term care benefit from 2035 at least at the care wage floor (HLT-9.05, `DEC-2026-11-11-02`). Those wages already earn Social Security credit. So under (c) the credited earnings only **top up a caregiver's actual earnings to half the average wage** in a year, and hours paid by the plan or Medicaid count as earnings, never toward the credit. The same care is never credited twice. That follows the 2100 Act, which credits *unpaid* caregivers [EV-SEC-460]. The judgment cost of −0.1 to −0.2 assumes this rule: it counts unpaid caregivers and the shortfall of low-paid ones, not relatives whose paid care hours already reach half the average wage. The score requested in point 12 should model the same population.
- **Together:** about −0.5 to −0.6 of payroll, inside part 1's margin under C.
- **Not proposed:** an across-the-board increase, −1.56 on the 2026 score [EV-SEC-446]; with it, part 1's options no longer close the gap.

## Part 3. If depletion comes before the law

The law does not say whether SSA pays full benefits late or reduced benefits on time [EV-SEC-411] [EV-SEC-412]. **Recommended (point 11):** a standing rule that if the retirement fund runs short before the solvency bill takes effect, the general fund lends it enough to pay full benefits on time, repaid from the package's revenue. *Cost:* none if the bill passes in 2030; otherwise a repayable loan of roughly the gap's size, about $400-450 billion a year at depletion (legacy estimate, `../legacy-research.md`, 4.1).

## Recommendation: part 1 C; part 2 as in the table; part 3 yes

- **A holds up on the 2026 report.** Each piece matches SSA's 2026 score, and SS-5 brings the package to about 84% of the gap with no tax below $250,000.
- **C closes the rest without a middle-class tax,** using the reserves A builds, with SS-7 as an automatic fallback so solvency never depends on markets alone.
- **Part 2's improvements are targeted** at the poorest long-career workers, at older people's prices and at caregivers, at about a seventh of part 1's gain.
- **D's 67-vote premise is untested,** and the clock is short. The bill can still be offered for bipartisan votes; D8 does not need them if the Senate's rules change, as legacy D8 assumed [EV-SEC-677].
- **Ask SSA's actuaries for a whole-package score in 2027-28** (preparation; point 12). Every share above is an estimate on single-provision scores.

**What would change this:** an SSA package score far below the estimates (toward B); a Republican bill with real cosponsors before 2029 (toward D); a later Trustees report that widens the gap again (more of SS-7).

## If you decide

- **Under C:** section 4 writes the Social Security Solvency and Protection Act of 2030 as a regular-order bill: SS-1, SS-2, SS-5, an investment board for part of the reserves with SS-7 as its trigger, SS-3, SS-4, the caregiver credit and part 3's standing loan rule.
- **Under A, B or D:** the same bill with or without the closers; D starts with a 2029 negotiation.
- **The agenda.** SS-1 to SS-5 and SS-8 stay in this flagship; SS-B4 (caregiver credits, `agenda/social-security`) is carried here as part 2.
