# A secure floor for every household: the problem

Role: section 1 of the secure floor dossier. It covers what is wrong, for whom and how large, and how the law will stand in January 2029, for the four subjects of flagship 1 (`charter/PLAN.md` 3.1): a monthly child benefit (part A), income support and participation help (part B), paid leave (part C) and Social Security protection (part D). Part E covers how they fit together: income definitions, phase-outs and income lines (shared rules 1, 2 and 4). Status: draft (stage D, board row S2). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`, or another dossier's register where it already holds the fact). Claims still missing evidence are listed at the end and are not made here. The legacy model results are restated with their file and line in `legacy-research.md`.

Outcomes this section frames (`charter/outcomes.md`):
- OUT-01, child poverty comes as close to eradicated as possible (parts A and B);
- OUT-02, households can plan beyond the next paycheck (parts B, C and D);
- OUT-06, fair pay and real power at work, through its paid-leave access measure (part C);
- OUT-08, time and practical freedom (part C).

## In brief

- **Child poverty is back to where it was before 2021.** On the Supplemental Poverty Measure, 13.4% of children were poor in 2025, about 9.66 million [EV-SEC-019] [EV-SEC-020], and 3.4% were in deep poverty [EV-SEC-022]. The rate was 5.2% in 2021, the year of the expanded monthly child credit, and 12.4% in 2022 after it lapsed [EV-HOU-073].
- **The 2025 law raised the child credit to $2,200 but left out the poorest children.** About 17 million to 19 million children, one in four or more, get less than the full credit because their parents earn too little [EV-SEC-011] [EV-SEC-013] [EV-SEC-014]. A new Social Security number rule shuts out citizen children whose parents lack one: about 500,000 children on a secondhand estimate [EV-SEC-016].
- **The rest of the floor is thin and shrinking.** The 2025 law cuts SNAP by almost $187 billion over ten years [EV-SEC-035]. CBO expects its work rules alone to remove about 2.4 million people in an average month [EV-SEC-036]. Participation fell from about 42.1 million in fiscal 2025 to about 38.0 million in fiscal 2026 [EV-SEC-039]. SSI pays $994 a month [EV-SEC-041], and its $2,000 asset limit has not moved since 1989 [EV-SEC-042]. Fewer than three in ten unemployed people get unemployment insurance [EV-SEC-045] [EV-SEC-046], and 21 of every 100 poor families get TANF [EV-SEC-050].
- **Most workers have no paid family leave.** 27% of private-sector workers had it through their employer in 2023, 13% in the bottom wage quarter [EV-SEC-300] [EV-SEC-301]. Thirteen states run their own programs [EV-SEC-309]. Two-thirds of workers who needed leave and did not take it could not afford to go unpaid [EV-SEC-307].
- **Social Security's retirement fund runs short in late 2032.** The 2026 Trustees project the OASI fund can pay full benefits until the fourth quarter of 2032, then 78% [EV-SEC-400]. That is a cut of about $16,900 a year for a typical couple retiring in 2033 [EV-SEC-406]. CBO puts the cut larger and the combined funds' date a year earlier [EV-SEC-407] [EV-SEC-409]. The plan starts in January 2029, so its first Congress is the last one with time to act before depletion.
- **The layers stack.** A single parent of two on a voucher and SNAP, in the earned income credit's phase-out range, keeps about 25 cents of each added dollar before payroll tax (table in part E). Few families take every program at once, and CBO's average rate for low- and moderate-income workers is 31% [EV-HOU-401] [EV-SEC-539].

## Part A. The child benefit

### A.1 Child poverty

| Measure, 2025 | Rate | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Official child poverty | 13.4%, a record low, down 1.0 point | [EV-SEC-018] |
| Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM), children | 13.4% (13.5% in 2024); about 9.66 million children | [EV-SEC-019] [EV-SEC-020] |
| SPM deep poverty, children (below half the threshold) | 3.4% (3.2% in 2024) | [EV-SEC-022] |
| SPM, all people | 13.1% | [EV-SEC-019] |

- **The 2021 test.** The SPM child rate fell from 9.7% in 2020 to 5.2% in 2021 and rose to 12.4% in 2022, as first published [EV-SEC-202] [EV-SEC-205] [EV-HOU-073]. Census's 2026 tables show the series as revised, 5.1% and 12% [EV-SEC-021], and BLS corrected the thresholds in July 2026 [EV-SEC-207]; this dossier uses the original figures with their original release. Section 2, part A weighs how much of the fall the credit caused.
- **What already works.** In 2025 the refundable child credit moved about 1.3 million children out of SPM poverty, the earned income credit about 2.1 million, SNAP about 1.2 million and Social Security about 1.3 million [EV-SEC-024]. These are Census's accounting, not causal estimates.
- **Who stays poor.** The legacy model's breakdown, on its historical 2034 package, finds that children in families with no earnings have a poverty rate of about 63%. Renters make up about 65% of the poor children left (`legacy-research.md`, 1.6). These are model results, not Census figures, and need a re-run on the 2029 baseline.

### A.2 The credit under the 2025 law

The 2025 reconciliation law (Public Law 119-21) made these changes, all permanent:

| Rule | 2026 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credit per child under 17 | $2,200, indexed from 2026 | [EV-SEC-001] [EV-SEC-002] [EV-SEC-003] |
| Refundable maximum | $1,700, indexed | [EV-SEC-003] [EV-SEC-004] |
| Earnings phase-in | 15% of earnings above $2,500 | [EV-SEC-006] |
| Phase-out | $50 per $1,000 above $200,000 ($400,000 joint), not indexed | [EV-SEC-007] |
| Social Security numbers | The taxpayer, or one spouse on a joint return, and the child must have one issued for work | [EV-SEC-005] |
| Other dependents | $500, nonrefundable | [EV-SEC-008] |

- **Who gets less.** The phase-in means a family must earn enough to get the full credit. The Tax Policy Center counts about 17 million children, one in four, getting less than the full credit, and about 2 million getting nothing [EV-SEC-011] [EV-SEC-012]. Columbia's Center on Poverty and Social Policy counts 19 million (28%) under the enacted law [EV-SEC-013], and CBPP 19 million in 2026 [EV-SEC-014]. The estimates use the same method and differ by base year and the $2,200 maximum. TPC later put the share at "almost 1 in 3" [EV-SEC-074]. The children shut out are the ones in the deepest poverty: the legacy model finds deep-poor children receive a mean existing credit of $527 against $4,185 for children who are not poor (`legacy-research.md`, 1.5; a model result).
- **The number rule.** The enacted rule bars the credit for a citizen child unless at least one parent has a work-eligible Social Security number. About 500,000 children lose it, on a Tax Policy Center estimate relayed by the Bipartisan Policy Center [EV-SEC-016]. The House's stricter text would have cut about 2 million (the Joint Committee on Taxation, via TPC) [EV-SEC-015] [EV-SEC-017]. No official estimate of the enacted rule is in the register. Immigrant eligibility is shared rule 3, owned by the immigration flagship.
- **What else the law added.** A $1,000 deposit to a "Trump account" for children born from 2025 through 2028, at a parent's election [EV-SEC-009]. It is a deposit to the child's account, not family income, and its pilot ends with births in 2028. A $6,000 deduction for people 65 and over, which expires after 2028 and so is not in the January 2029 baseline unless extended [EV-SEC-010] [EV-SEC-426].

### A.3 The legacy design and its model

- **FAM-1D, the American Family Act.** It pays $300 a month for children 6 to 17, $360 under 6 and $2,400 in the first month of life, fully refundable, monthly, indexed (`legacy/domains/domain-family.md`, FAM-1). Espen adopted it on October 24, 2026, with one million new vouchers (legacy governing decisions, "Family package adopted"). It is a working choice under `DEC-2026-10-30-02`. Its working cost is $1.7-2.1 trillion over 2030-39, a rough planning estimate anchored to the Tax Policy Center's $1.4 trillion for FY2026-35 (`legacy-research.md`, 1.9).
- **The legacy model's result.** The child-poverty microsimulation puts the adopted package at 8.73%, 8.34% or 8.09% child poverty against a 13.40% baseline, under moderate, enhanced or full delivery (`legacy-research.md`, 1.4). Its baseline reproduces Census's 13.4% and its refundable-credit removal matches Census's counts. **What is not validated**: the delivery cases are analyst assumptions. The model's American Family Act formula keeps $2,000 a child, not current law's $2,200. It applies no Social Security number test. Its data are 2025 incomes, so they include none of the 2025 law's SNAP or Medicaid changes (`legacy-research.md`, 0 and 2). S3 needs a re-run before relying on these figures.

## Part B. Income support and participation help

The flagship's "income support" has no legacy design (`README.md`, "Scope"). This part maps what a family stands on today.

### B.1 The earned income credit

- In 2026 it pays up to $664 with no children, $4,427 with one, $7,316 with two and $8,231 with three or more [EV-SEC-025]. It phases in at 34%, 40% or 45% of earnings with children and phases out at 15.98% (one child) or 21.06% (two or more) [EV-SEC-501] [EV-SEC-500].
- Workers without children get at most $664, phased out by $19,540 [EV-SEC-026], and only from age 25 to 64 [EV-SEC-027].
- 78% of eligible taxpayers claimed it in tax year 2021, 81% of the dollars [EV-SEC-111].

### B.2 SNAP after the 2025 law

| Change | Starts | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work time limit widened to ages 18-64 and to parents whose youngest child is 14 or older; exemptions for veterans, homeless people and former foster youth removed | On enactment, with USDA guidance | [EV-SEC-028] [EV-SEC-029] |
| States pay 0% to 15% of benefit costs, by payment error rate | Fiscal 2028 (later for the highest-error states) | [EV-SEC-030] |
| States pay 75% of administrative costs, up from 50% | Fiscal 2027 | [EV-SEC-031] [EV-SEC-070] |
| Thrifty Food Plan: inflation only; a re-evaluation may not raise its cost | From October 2025 | [EV-SEC-032] |
| Eligibility limited to citizens, permanent residents, Cuban and Haitian entrants and Compact residents | On enactment, with guidance | [EV-SEC-033] |
| The energy-assistance utility allowance ended for households without an elderly or disabled member; internet costs no longer deductible | On enactment | [EV-SEC-508] |
| SNAP-Ed's mandatory funding ended after fiscal 2025 | Fiscal 2026 | [EV-SEC-071] |

- **What CBO expects.** The nutrition subtitle saves almost $187 billion over FY2025-34 [EV-SEC-035]. The work rules alone cut participation by about 2.4 million people in an average month [EV-SEC-036] [EV-SEC-037]. The cost share leads states to cut benefits for about 300,000 more [EV-SEC-038]. The food plan change lowers the average benefit to $213 in 2034, against $227 [EV-SEC-034]. CBO scored each provision alone.
- **What has happened.** USDA counts an average 38.0 million participants in fiscal 2026 so far, against 42.1 million in fiscal 2025 [EV-SEC-039]. CBPP finds the March 2026 drop larger than CBO forecast, and reads it as states moving early. That reading is CBPP's (an advocacy group), set against CBO's estimate [EV-SEC-040] [EV-SEC-038].

### B.3 SSI, unemployment insurance and TANF

- **SSI** pays $994 a month for an individual in 2026 [EV-SEC-041], about three-quarters of the $15,960 poverty guideline [EV-SEC-044] (project arithmetic). Its $2,000 resource limit has been fixed since 1989 [EV-SEC-042] [EV-SEC-072], and its $20 and $65 income exclusions are not indexed [EV-SEC-043]. Care's part B recommends higher limits where Medicaid long-term care uses them [EV-CAR-478].
- **Unemployment insurance** reached 29% of unemployed people in 2023 (Minneapolis Fed) [EV-SEC-045] and 27% in 2024 (NELP and EPI, from Labor Department data) [EV-SEC-046] [EV-SEC-047]. Rates run from about 10% in Kentucky to 55% in Minnesota [EV-SEC-045]. Sixteen states pay fewer than 26 weeks, and Florida and North Carolina pay 12 [EV-SEC-048]. States set benefits and weeks within broad federal rules [EV-SEC-049]. This dossier maps it; its design belongs to the work flagship (`README.md`).
- **TANF** reached 21 of every 100 poor families with children in 2023, against 68 in 1996 [EV-SEC-050] (CBPP's other table gives 20 [EV-SEC-051]). Its $16.5 billion block grant has not risen since 1996 and has lost 49% of its value [EV-SEC-052].

### B.4 What families say, and how volatile income is

- **Emergencies.** In the Federal Reserve's 2025 survey, 63% of adults would cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent [EV-SEC-053]. 30% had income that varied at least occasionally, and 11% struggled to pay bills because of it [EV-SEC-054]. 73% said they were doing at least okay financially, though low-income, young and Black adults saw meaningful declines [EV-SEC-122].
- **Worries.** These are polls: opinion data, graded C, without causal weight.
  - Pew, April 2026: 73% call the affordability of health care a very big problem for the country, and 66% inflation [EV-SEC-135].
  - Pew, August 2026: 60% say they lack the income to lead the life they want, and 53% have no emergency fund covering three months [EV-SEC-136].
  - Gallup, April 2026: the cost of living is the most-named family financial problem, at 31% [EV-SEC-137].
  - West Health-Gallup: 49% can afford health care, down from 61% in 2022 [EV-SEC-138]. About 40% doubt Social Security will be there in ten years [EV-SEC-139].
  - AP-NORC: about three-quarters call child care costs a major problem [EV-SEC-140].
- **Food.** USDA's last household food security report found 13.7% of households food insecure in 2024 [EV-SEC-055]. USDA then ended the report [EV-SEC-056], and AEI researchers report the survey supplement behind it was dropped [EV-SEC-057]. So OUT-01's and OUT-02's food-hardship measure has no federal source after 2024. That is a gap for section 7.

### B.5 Delivery: the floor reaches people only if it is delivered

- **Non-filers.** About 86% of people who could appear on a tax return do (CBO, via CRS) [EV-SEC-060]. Non-filers are concentrated among low-income households, the people a tax-delivered benefit most needs to reach. In 2021, estimates of children at risk of missing the advance credit ran from 4 million to 7 million (advocacy estimates) [EV-SEC-058] [EV-SEC-110]. No official count of eligible children the IRS could not reach is in the register.
- **Free filing.** The IRS's own Direct File will not be offered in 2026, with no launch date set [EV-SEC-063] [EV-SEC-064]. The 2025 law funds a report on public-private alternatives [EV-SEC-062].
- **SSA's capacity.** SSA lost about 6,500 staff in fiscal 2025, to 52,100, on its inspector general's count [EV-SEC-066], against a target of 50,000 from about 57,000 [EV-SEC-432]. The inspector general found callers waited 15 minutes on hold in fiscal 2025, and 108.6 minutes for a callback; about 25 million calls ended without service [EV-SEC-434]. SSA reports much shorter waits for 2026 [EV-SEC-068] [EV-SEC-435]. Those are the second Trump administration's own figures, which exclude callback waits, and SSA stopped publishing several measures [EV-SEC-069]. They do not stand alone. Paid leave (FAM-3) and any non-tax benefit would run through this agency.

### B.6 Participation help (LAB-10)

- The legacy direction is a $100 billion envelope over FY2030-39. The Labor Department leads it through existing employment and training institutions, paying for transport, child care during training, accommodations, navigators and paid placements. It is "an offer, not a work condition" (`legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`, LAB-10; [EV-SEC-131]). It has no design, eligibility rule or placement count, and none can be read from the envelope (`legacy-research.md`, 5).
- **Why it matters now.** The plan creates jobs in care at a $20 floor (`DEC-2026-11-11-04`), in construction and repair (housing, transportation) and in clean power. BLS projects health care and social assistance to add the most jobs of any sector over 2025-35, over 2.2 million. Solar installers and wind technicians grow fastest but add fewer than 15,000 jobs [EV-SEC-133]. An industry group projects construction needs 349,000 new workers in 2026 [EV-SEC-145]. Lack of child care keeps low-income parents out of training [EV-SEC-149]. Section 2 weighs what gets people into these jobs.

## Part C. Paid leave

- **Access.** 27% of private-sector workers had employer-paid family leave in March 2023, the latest year BLS published [EV-SEC-300]. By wage quarter it was 13% at the bottom and 44% at the top [EV-SEC-301]. 80% had paid sick leave in March 2025, but only 41% of the lowest-paid tenth [EV-SEC-302].
- **Unpaid leave and its limits.** In 2018, 56% of employees were eligible for the Family and Medical Leave Act's unpaid, job-protected leave [EV-SEC-304]. 7% needed leave and did not take it, 9% among low-wage workers [EV-SEC-306]. 66% of those could not afford unpaid leave and 45% feared losing their job [EV-SEC-307]. 18% of low-wage workers who took leave lost a job, against 5% of others [EV-SEC-308]. These come from a federal survey with low response rates.
- **The states.** Thirteen jurisdictions, including DC, run mandatory programs as of September 2026 [EV-SEC-309]. The programs began with California (2004), New Jersey (2009), Rhode Island (2014) and New York (2018) [EV-SEC-310]; Minnesota and Delaware started paying in 2026 [EV-SEC-317] [EV-SEC-318] and Maine in May 2026 [EV-SEC-319]. Maryland and Virginia begin in 2028 [EV-SEC-309] [EV-SEC-320].
  - All are paid for by payroll contributions, mostly or wholly from employees [EV-SEC-312]. They replace 50% to 100% of wages up to caps from $170 to $1,765 a week [EV-SEC-311].
  - An advocacy estimate puts about 34% of private-sector workers in a state with a program, counting the two not yet paying [EV-SEC-322].
  - Job protection is uneven: California's benefit carries none of its own [EV-SEC-313].
- **Federal law in 2029.** The 2025 law made the section 45S employer credit permanent and widened it [EV-SEC-330] [EV-SEC-331] [EV-SEC-332]. It reached 1,230 firms and about $101 million in 2020, 88% of the dollars to firms with revenue over $1 billion [EV-SEC-334]. Federal employees have had 12 weeks of paid parental leave since 2019 [EV-SEC-337].
- **The cost of a state's program running short.** Washington's contribution rose from 0.40% in 2021 to 1.13% in 2026 and reaches the 1.2% statutory cap in 2027 [EV-SEC-323] [EV-SEC-324] [EV-SEC-325]. Its actuary and the legislature's auditors both find the capped rate too low [EV-SEC-327] [EV-SEC-329]. This is the closest evidence for FAM-3A's step-up design.
- **Abroad.** The United States is the only OECD country with no national paid maternity leave; the OECD average was 18.5 weeks in 2022 [EV-SEC-375] [EV-SEC-376].
- **The legacy design** (FAM-3A): 12 weeks of social insurance at 0.6% of wages, stepping toward a 1.10% ceiling, run by SSA. Its working cost of about $0.9 trillion is the middle take-up case. The budget model uses the high case: $1.4 trillion of benefits over 2030-39, a contribution reaching 1.10% in 2041, and a Treasury loan peaking near $185 billion and repaid in 2058, not 2048 as the legacy domain file says (`legacy-research.md`, 3). The legacy file's "CBO $547B" is CBO's 2020 score of the 2019 FAMILY Act [EV-SEC-344]. Its "JCT break-even 0.85%" appears only in an American Action Forum article and is unverified [EV-SEC-345].

## Part D. Social Security

### D.1 The trust funds

| | 2026 Trustees (June 2026) | CBO (September 2026) | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retirement and survivors (OASI) fund depleted | Fourth quarter of 2032; then 78% of benefits payable | Fiscal 2032 | [EV-SEC-400] [EV-SEC-409] |
| Disability (DI) fund | Pays full benefits through at least 2100 | After 2100 | [EV-SEC-400] [EV-SEC-409] |
| Combined funds (hypothetical) | Third quarter of 2034; then 83% | Fiscal 2033 | [EV-SEC-400] [EV-SEC-413] [EV-SEC-409] |
| 75-year deficit | 4.42% of taxable payroll (3.82% a year earlier) | 4.57% | [EV-SEC-402] [EV-SEC-403] [EV-SEC-410] |
| Cut at depletion | 22% | 26% (an average of 28% a year in 2033 onward) | [EV-SEC-406] [EV-SEC-407] [EV-SEC-408] |

- **The legacy figures check out.** The legacy file's 4.42%, 2034 and 83% match the 2026 report. Its OASI date "in as little as six years" fits the fourth quarter of 2032 (`legacy-research.md`, 4).
- **Why the gap grew.** It rose by 0.60 points of payroll in a year; changes in law, methods and assumptions added 0.53, mainly lower assumed fertility [EV-SEC-403]. The Social Security Fairness Act's repeal of the Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset (January 2025) [EV-SEC-423] [EV-SEC-424] and the 2025 law's tax changes [EV-SEC-427] [EV-SEC-428] brought the dates earlier (CRFB) [EV-SEC-425].
- **The gap is larger than in 1983.** Before the 1983 amendments the deficit was 1.82% of payroll; today's is more than twice that [EV-SEC-414].
- These are projections. The Trustees report is a second Trump administration document; CBO's independent projection agrees on OASI and is less favorable on the rest.

### D.2 What happens at depletion

- People remain legally entitled to full benefits. But SSA may not pay more than the fund holds, borrow, or move money between the funds, so it would pay full benefits late or reduced benefits on time. The law does not say which [EV-SEC-411] [EV-SEC-412].
- CRFB puts the 22% cut at about $16,900 a year for a typical dual-earning couple retiring in January 2033, $12,700 for a single-income couple and $10,200 for a low-income dual-earning couple [EV-SEC-406]. CBO's larger cut would make these figures larger [EV-SEC-407].
- **For the plan's calendar.** Depletion in late 2032 falls in the plan's second Congress, near the 2032 election. Section 310(g) of the Budget Act bars Social Security changes from reconciliation [EV-SEC-476], so the fix needs regular order: 60 votes or a rules change, as legacy D8 assumed.

### D.3 Who depends on it

- 71.6 million people drew benefits in September 2026, averaging $1,943 a month [EV-SEC-417].
- It is the largest anti-poverty program: it moved 28.8 million people out of SPM poverty in 2025 [EV-SEC-420]. Among people 65 and over, poverty was 9.8% on the official measure and 15.4% on the SPM, which counts medical costs (reported by Spotlight on Poverty from Census's tables) [EV-SEC-422].
- 39% of men and 44% of women 65 and over get half or more of their income from it, and 12% and 15% get 90% or more [EV-SEC-418]. Older survey-based tables put these shares higher [EV-SEC-419].
- **Adequacy gaps.**
  - The special minimum benefit reaches almost no one: about 32,000 of 64 million beneficiaries in 2019, and no new retirees [EV-SEC-439].
  - The cost-of-living adjustment uses a price index that has grown about 0.2 points a year more slowly than an index of older people's spending [EV-SEC-440] [EV-SEC-441].
  - Years out of paid work to care for children or relatives count as zeros in the benefit formula; abroad, many systems credit them [EV-SEC-478] [EV-SEC-480].
- **In force in 2029.** The 2026 taxable maximum is $184,500 ($192,300 in 2027) [EV-SEC-431]. The December 2025 cost-of-living adjustment was 2.8% [EV-SEC-429].

## Part E. How the floor fits together: income, phase-outs and income lines

### E.1 Every program defines income its own way

| Program | Income test | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax credits (child credit, earned income credit) | Adjusted gross income or earnings, for the tax-filing unit, over the year | [EV-SEC-504] [EV-SEC-500] |
| Premium tax credits and Medicaid | Modified adjusted gross income, which itself has no single definition | [EV-SEC-518] [EV-SEC-520] [EV-SEC-535] |
| SNAP | Gross and net monthly household income, after its own deductions | [EV-SEC-506] [EV-HOU-417] [EV-HOU-418] |
| Housing | HUD annual and adjusted income, all sources not excluded | [EV-SEC-509] [EV-HOU-409] |
| SSI | Countable monthly income, most sources, taxable or not | [EV-SEC-522] [EV-SEC-523] [EV-SEC-536] |

- **One rule already protects the tax credits.** A tax refund or an advance payment of a refundable credit is not income, and not a resource for 12 months, for any federally funded program [EV-SEC-534]. So a child benefit paid as a refundable credit leaves rent and SNAP untouched; paid any other way, it would count unless a statute excludes it (shared rule 1).
- **Some programs already borrow each other's findings.** HUD may use another program's income finding [EV-SEC-510]. Medicaid and CHIP may use SNAP's findings through express lane eligibility [EV-SEC-562].

### E.2 Income lines

The programs set their lines against different measures:
- **Poverty guideline:** SNAP's 130% gross test, with state options to 200% [EV-SEC-506] [EV-SEC-507]; Medicaid's 138% [EV-SEC-519]; school meals' 130% and 185% [EV-SEC-527]. The 2026 guideline is $15,960 for one person, $27,320 for three and $33,000 for four [EV-SEC-532] [EV-SEC-533].
- **Area median income:** housing's 30% and 50% lines [EV-SEC-512].
- **State median income:** child care's 85% [EV-SEC-529] [EV-SEC-530], and care's decided free line at 85% (`DEC-2026-11-11-03`).
- **Fixed dollar amounts:** the tax credits.

A family moving between programs faces several tests at once, and the models must translate between them (shared rule 4).

## What this means for the design

1. **The children the 2025 law leaves out are the children in deepest poverty.** Any child benefit design that keeps an earnings phase-in or the parent Social Security number rule leaves them out too. Full refundability is the cheapest single change against deep poverty (legacy FAM-1C), but it is not the only design. Section 2 weighs the evidence and the employment debate.
2. **Delivery decides reach.** A tax-delivered benefit misses non-filers, and the 2025 law removed the IRS's free filing tool. SSA, the natural administrator for paid leave and any non-tax benefit, has lost about one staff member in nine (B.5).
3. **The rest of the floor is being cut while the plan is designed.** The SNAP cuts phase in through 2028, so in 2029 the plan inherits a smaller SNAP and states facing new costs. Whether the flagship restores any of it is a fork for S3.
4. **Social Security's date is the hard deadline in the plan.** The plan's first Congress is the last one with time to act before late 2032. The legacy package's scores mostly hold against SSA's 2026 figures, but no whole-package score exists.
5. **The stack is real but narrow.** Rates of 70-80 cents hit families taking several programs at once. Most families take one or none. The plan's own additions (the 7% child care cap, rent counting new benefits) have to fit under the ceiling shared rule 2 asks for.
