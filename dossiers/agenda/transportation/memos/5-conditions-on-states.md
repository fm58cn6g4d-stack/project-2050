# Memo 5: How hard to push states toward repair

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 5 of the transportation design (`../3-design.md`). Status: for decision (one of six in this batch, `README.md`). Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

## In short

About nine of every ten federal highway dollars go to states by formula, and states pick the projects. Many add new lanes while existing roads and bridges fall behind. The plan as filed adds three strings to push states toward repair.

**Recommendation: keep the simplest string, a lower federal share for new lanes, and turn the other two into public reports with a review (B).**

## The question

How much should federal law steer what states do with their formula highway money?

**What you already chose.** Option A is the plan as filed (Draft 2's call A-24). The safety and local-access shares (A-25) stay as chosen in every option: they set shares of money, not reviews.

## The options

**A. Three conditions (as chosen).**
1. **Preservation shift.** States below their own pavement and bridge targets move National Highway Performance Program money to preservation.
2. **Lifecycle test.** Projects over $100 million that add lanes must pass a lifecycle cost test.
3. **Lower federal share for new lanes.** New general-purpose lanes get a 70% federal share (80% on the Interstate) instead of the usual 80% or 90%.

- None costs the federal budget anything (Moving America Draft 2, 2026) [EV-MOV-214].
- *Risk:* state transportation departments and road builders will resist strings on formula money [EV-MOV-214]. The first two add a review process for each state, and the second gives the agency a gate to hold.

**B. Keep the price signal; report the rest (recommended).**
- Keep condition 3, the lower federal share for new lanes. It works through the money and needs no review.
- Turn conditions 1 and 2 into published reports:
  - each state publishes its condition trend and the lifecycle analysis of its large lane-adding projects;
  - FHWA reviews a state whose condition falls for two years running, and agrees a repair plan with it.
- *Burden it removes:* a federal approval step on large projects.
- *What it gives up:* a binding requirement to spend on repair first.

**C. Review only (H.R. 8870's approach).**
- The House committee's 2026 bill, as the legacy design reads it, moves state asset-management plan review to every two years and adds no conditions (Moving America Draft 2, 2026; not yet traced to the bill text).
- *Risk:* it leaves repair to state choice, as today.

## Recommendation

**B.** It follows the design lens (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`): prefer published counts with a review trigger over strict structures enforced up front. The lower federal share alone makes a new lane cost the state more. The reports make the trade-off visible to the public and to Congress without adding a gate.

The evidence behind condition 3 is strong: driving rises about one-for-one with new interstate lane-kilometers (Duranton and Turner, American Economic Review, 2011) [EV-MOV-114], and a review for California's air board put the long-run effect near one as well (Handy and Boarnet, 2014) [EV-MOV-115]. The register holds no evaluation of fix-it-first conditions themselves, so the choice between A and B rests on judgment.

**What it does not settle.** The set-asides for resilience, clean fuels and truck parking (point 5 in section 3). Those move about $2.6 billion a year of formula money out of state discretion (Moving America Draft 2, 2026) [EV-MOV-215], and are a yes-or-no point of their own.
