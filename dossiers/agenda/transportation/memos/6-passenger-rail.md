# Memo 6: How much passenger rail to build, and how money waits for readiness

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 6 of the transportation design (`../3-design.md`). Status: for decision (one of six in this batch, `README.md`). Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). The rail figures are the legacy rail design's own (the American Rail Competitiveness Act draft and Moving America Draft 2, 2026), project estimates.

## In short

The plan as filed is the largest federal passenger rail program the country has had: $254 billion over FY2032-36. It covers the Northeast Corridor and Amtrak's network, state-supported corridors, high-speed lines and big-city terminals. U.S. rail projects are slow and costly, and few corridors are ready to build. The design already releases its largest money only as projects pass public gates.

**Recommendation: keep it (A)**, gates included.

## The question

How large should the intercity rail program be in 2032-36, and how much of it should be firm money rather than money released as projects show they are ready?

**What you already chose.** Option A is the plan as filed (division D; Draft 2's calls A-6, A-19, A-21 and A-22).

## What the program is

- **Size.** $254.03 billion over FY2032-36: $160.83 billion of renewal from the rail account and $93.20 billion of expansion from the general fund, plus up to $25 billion of reserve released only at gates (Moving America Draft 2, 2026) [EV-MOV-222].
- **The parts:**
  - the core network (Northeast Corridor, the national network, long-distance trains, a national fleet, stations and accessibility): $59.95 billion;
  - corridor development: $160.45 billion;
  - accountability, regional commissions, access rights, the industrial base and the rest: $33.63 billion (Moving America Draft 2, 2026) [EV-MOV-223].
- **High-speed lines.** They get $55 billion. No corridor may take more than $14 billion, no project under $1 billion qualifies and the federal share is at most 50% (Moving America Draft 2, 2026) [EV-MOV-224]. A project needs four things first:
  - an environmental decision;
  - trains under contract;
  - independently checked ridership;
  - committed state or private money.
- **Payment in tranches.** Large projects are paid in four tranches, each after an independent reviewer certifies progress.

## The options

**A. As chosen (recommended).**
- *For people:* new and faster service on state corridors in the 2030s, and the first high-speed lines beyond the private Las Vegas line.
- *Risk:* the money arrives faster than corridors are ready. The legacy design's own projection had only three to five corridors with a finished environmental record by 2031, and none had reached the last planning step in March 2026 (the rail division's draft, 2026) [EV-MOV-225]. Gates and the dollar caps limit the damage: money waits rather than being spent badly.

**B. Firmer: make high-speed money mostly contingent.**
- Keep the core network and state corridors. Move most of the $55 billion high-speed program into the gated reserve.
- *For the budget:* less firm money in FY2032-36.
- *Risk:* sponsors raise state and private shares against firm federal money; contingent money weakens that signal.

**C. Slower: state corridors and the Northeast first, high speed in the next bill.**
- *For the budget:* the smallest rail cost in FY2032-36.
- *Risk:* it defers the projects with the longest lead times by five years.

## Recommendation

**A.** It rests on the design's own structure and on judgment:
- **Readiness.** The gates already do what B would do by moving money into a reserve. Money that is not ready waits, and the dollar caps keep one corridor from absorbing the program.
- **Firm money.** It is what lets states and private partners commit their shares.
- **Choosing B or C.** Either is reasonable if you weigh cost risk above speed.

Three cautions:
- **The pipeline is under review.** The current administration is "looking at all of the corridors" in the federal pipeline, and FRA's staff is down 22% (Trains, 2026) [EV-MOV-109]. A 2029 plan may inherit fewer ready corridors than the legacy design assumed.
- **Costs are high, here and abroad.** U.S. rail transit is the sixth-costliest of 59 countries to build (Transit Costs Project) [EV-MOV-125]. European high-speed lines averaged €25 million a kilometer, with large overruns and several lines carrying too few passengers (European Court of Auditors, 2018) [EV-MOV-130]. Choosing corridors by demand and paying by stage is what the gates are for.
- **Named allocations.** The design names likely corridors (Dallas-Houston, California, Seattle-Portland, Charlotte-Atlanta, Brightline West's extensions). These are illustrations of how $55 billion could fall, not allocations in the bill text, which allocates by gate clearance.
