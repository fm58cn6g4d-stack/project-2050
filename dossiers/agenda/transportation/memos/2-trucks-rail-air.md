# Memo 2: What trucks, railroads and flyers pay

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 2 of the transportation design (`../3-design.md`). Status: for decision (one of six in this batch, `README.md`). Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). "The plan's model" is the legacy Moving America design's own arithmetic (2026), a project estimate.

## In short

Heavy trucks do most of the damage to pavement, but they pay almost the same fuel tax per mile as a car, plus a flat yearly tax that hasn't changed since 1982. Intercity rail has no money of its own. The plan as filed charges heavy trucks by weight and distance, and adds small taxes on rail diesel and air trips for the rail account.

**Recommendation: keep it (A).**

## The question

Should heavy trucks pay by weight and miles driven, and should freight railroads and air travelers pay toward passenger rail?

**What you already chose.** Option A is the plan as filed (MR4, MR5; Draft 2's calls A-12 and A-13).

## The options

**A. Weight-distance charge, rail diesel and an aviation surcharge (as chosen; recommended).**
- **Trucks over 26,000 lb** pay per mile by weight class, averaging 3 cents on the heaviest classes. It replaces the heavy-vehicle use tax, which is flat, capped at $550 and unindexed since 1982 (Moving America Draft 2, 2026) [EV-MOV-209].
- **The data** are the mileage records carriers already file each quarter under the interstate fuel-tax agreement, so there is no new reporting system.
- **Truck excises.** The truck and tire excises become permanent, at 4% for zero-emission trucks.
- **Rail diesel** is taxed at 15 cents a gallon for the largest railroads and 7.5 cents for regional ones; short lines are exempt.
- **Air.** The aviation segment surcharge rises from $4 to $16.
- Together, rail diesel and the surcharge raise $36.2 billion over FY2032-36. They are rail's only dedicated money outside the shares of fuel taxes (the plan's model) [EV-MOV-208].

**B. A diesel increase instead of the weight-distance charge.**
- *For the budget:* simpler, but it charges a light delivery truck nearly the same per mile as an 80,000-lb rig.
- *Risk:* it leaves the pavement cost of the heaviest trucks underpriced. That judgment rests on the engineering rule that wear rises steeply with axle weight; the register does not yet hold an outside source for it.

**C. Keep the use tax; add only the rail and air taxes.**
- *For the budget:* the smallest change for trucking.
- *Risk:* the use tax keeps eroding, and the charge at a 2-cent average shows what a lighter truck charge costs: the general-fund draw rises from 0.180% to 0.185% of GDP over FY2032-36 (the plan's model) [EV-MOV-207].

**Dropped already: the corridor surcharge.** The legacy design had a separate surcharge on air trips that compete with rail corridors. It raised about $1 billion, with its own machinery and a fight with airlines (the plan's model) [EV-MOV-208].

## Recommendation

**A.** It prices the cost that matters for pavement and uses records carriers already keep. That is the design lens's "records the government already holds". It also gives rail a modest stream of its own. Without these rail taxes, rail's share paid by users roughly halves (the plan's model) [EV-MOV-208].

**Risks to watch.** Trucking groups will oppose it. A charge collected through quarterly filings invites under-reporting, so section 5 sets the audit rate. The final rate schedule is set in the 2030 act from a cost-allocation study, so the 3-cent average is a working assumption with a range of 2 to 3 cents.

**What the evidence says.** Oregon taxes trucks over 26,000 lb by weight and miles. Its tax raised about $252 million in FY2013 from about 277,000 trucks (Oregon DOT) [EV-MOV-121]. It costs about 11% to collect (Oregon DOT staff) [EV-MOV-118], and a state-commissioned study put evasion at 5.09% to 11.33% [EV-MOV-122]. So the federal charge should ride on records carriers already file rather than a separate return, and section 5 sets the audit rate.
