# Transportation: design choices

Role: section 3 of the transportation dossier: the real forks in the Moving America design, the options at each, what the evidence says, how they fit together, and a recommendation. Status: draft (stage C, step 7, PR 2). It converts the legacy design (`legacy/domains/transportation-moving-america-act-2031-draft-2.md`, "Draft 2", which governs where the legacy sources disagree). Under `DEC-2026-10-30-02` every legacy call, including the seven Draft 2 records as decided by Espen, is a working choice (`chosen`): it is weighed here as an option, not assumed. The plan begins in January 2029 (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`); anything meant for 2027-28 is labeled as an exception.

Outcomes the choices serve:
- OUT-05, clean, affordable power and a prepared country (transportation is the largest source of U.S. greenhouse emissions; section 1);
- OUT-11, the fiscal and fairness tests.

`charter/outcomes.md` has no outcome for getting around safely and affordably. Point 7 below asks Espen whether to add one.

## How to read this section

- **Six forks, one memo set.** Each fork that is Espen's to decide has a memo in `memos/`, with two to four options, a recommendation and its evidence. The six are sent together as one batch, so they can be decided in one round (`memos/README.md`). Approval is per memo, never as a package.
- **Recommended without a memo.** Eight narrower points, each a yes-or-no question. None is adopted by silence.
- **Everything else stays a working choice.** The table at the end lists all twenty-eight legacy calls (A-1 to A-28) and what this section does with each.
- Numbers from the plan's own model are project estimates (grade D): the legacy model's arithmetic, with no demand response. Sections 6 and 7 rerun and test them.

## Starting points (chosen, open to revision)

The legacy design rests on one principle and four laws.
- **Users pay for repair; the nation pays to build.** Renewal (keeping roads, bridges, transit and rail in good repair) is paid from user fees credited to a trust fund. Expansion (new lines and capacity) is paid from the general fund [EV-MOV-200].
- **One fund, three accounts** (highways, transit, intercity rail), with new receipts split 35 / 50 / 15 through FY2036 and 45 / 40 / 15 from FY2037 (A-7, A-8).
- **Four vehicles**, each carrying only what it can legally carry:
  1. A clean extension of fuel taxes and fund credits by September 30, 2028, plus a transfer keeping both accounts solvent through FY2031 (A-9). This is a **2027-28 exception**: it needs a bipartisan vote under divided government.
  2. The 2029 reconciliation act (MOV-29, on the plan's first budget bill, R1): fuel taxes made permanent and indexed with ten 3-cent steps (MR1); the clean-vehicle per-mile charge (MR2); permanent truck excises (MR3); the first rail and transit package; renewal top-ups for FY2032-35.
  3. The 2030 reconciliation act (MOV-30, on R3): the weight-distance charge (MR4), rail diesel and the aviation surcharge (MR5), package two, top-ups for FY2035-36.
  4. The Moving America Act of 2031 (MOV-31, 60 votes or regular order): programs, contract authority within receipts, expansion appropriations, account shares from FY2037, continuation, the solvency backstop and privacy.
- **Size as chosen:** $936.57B over FY2032-36; dedicated receipts $545.84B; the general fund $390.73B, or 0.180% of GDP a year, falling to 0.026% by FY2042-46 [EV-MOV-200].

## The baseline in 2029

What current law will be in 2029 is not settled, and the plan should not assume it.
- **Programs.** The 2021 authorization expired on September 30, 2026 and was extended to December 11, 2026. The House committee's five-year bill (H.R. 8870, BUILD America 250, about $580 billion over FY2027-31) was reported 62-2 on May 22, 2026, but has not passed the House, and the Senate has no bill (section 1).
- **Taxes.** Whatever happens to programs, current law stops crediting fuel and truck taxes to the trust fund after September 30, 2028, when most of the rates fall (section 1). That date is a fixed cliff.
- **The legacy assumption.** Draft 2 assumed H.R. 8870 is enacted in the 2026 lame duck and called it "this project's forecast, not law". The scenario dossier puts a multi-year bill by the end of 2028 at 40-55% (`dossiers/scenario/2027-28-playbook.md`, surface transportation row).
- **Working assumption.** Three cases are plausible in 2029:
  1. a five-year law through FY2031 (Draft 2's case);
  2. short extensions through 2028, with programs at roughly current levels;
  3. a lapse on October 1, 2028.
- **What changes across them.**
  - The two reconciliation acts work in every case: they set revenue and top up existing programs.
  - The 2031 bill's timing does change. In cases 2 and 3 the plan has to authorize programs itself in 2029-30, by regular order (point 1 below).
- **Fiscal rule baseline.** Each case starts from CBO's baseline convention, which carries the last year's contract authority forward. Section 6 states the case it prices.

## The fiscal rule here: report, don't gate

The legacy design sized itself to a self-imposed benchmark: 0.206% of GDP, FY2026's peak general-fund draw on surface transportation, which no statute or CBO rule sets [EV-MOV-213]. Several legacy calls follow from it:
- the benchmark itself (A-4);
- a report-and-recommend trigger and a capped backstop in division A section 108 (A-17);
- counting the general-fund grants against the benchmark (A-18);
- Interstate and Bridge Renewal's new money starting at zero in FY2032, because that year "has no room" [EV-MOV-212].

Under `DEC-2026-11-10-05` the dossier reports instead: yearly cost, permanent cost from FY2040 and the share of the plan-wide margin, passed to the financing settlement. A gap is not a defect. So the benchmark stops being a design rule here. Memo 3 asks the one question this raises for Espen: keep the envelope as sized, or resize by need.

## The design lens

`DEC-2026-11-09-13` (guide, don't police) bears on four places:
- **The clean-vehicle charge** reads the odometer at registration or inspection and bars location data [EV-MOV-204]. That is one plain test, using records the government already holds.
- **The weight-distance charge** uses the mileage records carriers already file each quarter under the fuel-tax agreement (memo 2).
- **Conditions on states' formula money** are the main case where the legacy design polices up front. Memo 5 weighs a lighter version: published condition reports and a review trigger, with one price signal kept.
- **The transit operating help** has conditions (maintenance of effort, service floors, a use rule). Memo 4 says what each costs agencies in burden as well as what it buys.

## Shared rules

Transportation relies on few of `dossiers/shared-rules.md`'s rules, because it has no income-tested benefit.
- **Rule 5 (who verifies, pays and answers for mistakes).** The per-mile charges are taxes. The Treasury administers them, and the privacy rule in Draft 2's division A section 111 (odometer only, return-information protection, records destroyed after three years) is this dossier's own. Working answer: keep it in one provision (section 4).
- **Rule 8 (workforce).** Construction, transit operations and rail manufacturing draw on the same workforce as housing and clean power. Working answer: no transportation-specific rule; section 5 states the capacity need.
- **Fairness (D3, owned by the financing settlement).** Fuel taxes take a larger share of income from lower-income households. The plan-wide test that lower- and middle-income households come out ahead is settled policy (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`). Memo 1 shows the household cost; the household model checks the plan-wide result.

## Fork 1. Who pays for road repair (memo 1)

- **The question.** Fuel taxes have not risen since 1993, and electric cars pay no fuel tax. What fills the gap: higher fuel taxes, a per-mile charge on clean vehicles, the general fund, or a mix?
- **As chosen (option A).**
  - Fuel taxes are made permanent and indexed, plus ten 3-cent steps from October 2029, each deferred in a year of high gas prices.
  - Clean vehicles pay per mile at the rate a 40-mpg car pays in fuel tax.
  - After the steps, a "fossil transition premium" adds five 2-cent steps in FY2040-44 that clean vehicles never pay.
- **What it costs drivers.** For a 25-mpg car, the federal fuel tax goes from $88 a year to $225 in FY2036 [EV-MOV-201]. In 2029 dollars the gasoline rate ends near the 1993 rate's real value [EV-MOV-202]. An electric car pays $77 a year in FY2031 and $141 in FY2036 [EV-MOV-204].
- **The alternatives.**
  - Indexing alone raises the general-fund draw from 0.180% to 0.267% of GDP [EV-MOV-203].
  - Dropping the premium more than doubles the FY2042-46 draw [EV-MOV-206].
- **Recommendation: A.** The premium is the long-run signal and the cheapest way to retire the general-fund draw, and clean vehicles never pay it. Its receipts are an upper bound, because the model has no demand response [EV-MOV-206].

## Fork 2. Trucks, rail and air (memo 2)

- **The question.** Fuel taxes cannot price axle weight, which drives most pavement wear, and the heavy-vehicle use tax is flat at $550 and unindexed since 1982 [EV-MOV-209]. Rail has no dedicated receipts.
- **As chosen (option A).**
  - Trucks over 26,000 lb pay a weight-distance charge (a 3-cent average for the heaviest classes) in place of the use tax, with zero-emission trucks at a 4% excise.
  - Rail diesel is taxed at 15 or 7.5 cents by railroad class, with short lines exempt.
  - The aviation segment surcharge rises from $4 to $16.
- **Recommendation: A**, with section 4 keeping the charge on records carriers already file. A 2-cent average raises the draw slightly [EV-MOV-207]. Without the rail and air taxes, rail's coverage roughly halves [EV-MOV-208].

## Fork 3. How large, and how split (memo 3)

- **The question.** With the benchmark no longer a design rule, is $936.57B the right size, and is the split right?
- **The options.**
  - Keep the chosen envelope, with the benchmark kept only as a published reference.
  - Resize by need, so Interstate and Bridge Renewal starts in FY2032 rather than ramping from zero [EV-MOV-212].
  - Take the first fallback tier.
- **The split.** It does not change the total draw. It decides which account reaches self-sufficiency, and moving it in FY2037 strands nothing [EV-MOV-211].
- **Recommendation: A.** Keep the envelope and the split; publish the benchmark as a comparison; keep section 108's capped backstop. Resizing by need is the alternative the settlement can choose if the plan-wide margin allows.

## Fork 4. Transit operating help (memo 4)

- **The question.** Since 1998, federal money for large transit systems has paid for capital, not operations. The pandemic aid that bridged fare losses is spent.
- **The options.**
  - A: none.
  - B (as chosen): $35.00B over FY2032-36, a conditional tier apportioned by service, trips and low-income residents.
  - C: $70.00B [EV-MOV-210].
  - D: B's money with lighter conditions.
- **Recommendation: D**, under the design lens. Keep the money, the maintenance of effort and the service floor, which protect riders. Replace the narrower use rules with published reporting. Memo 4 lists each condition with its burden.

## Fork 5. Conditions on states (memo 5)

- **The question.** Formula money is about 90% of highway aid, and states choose the projects. The legacy design adds three strings, at no federal cost [EV-MOV-214]:
  - states below their condition targets shift money to preservation;
  - a lifecycle test applies to projects over $100M that add lanes;
  - the federal share falls to 70% for new general-purpose lanes.
- **Recommendation: B.** Keep the lower federal share for new lanes, a simple price signal that needs no review. Turn the preservation shift and the lifecycle test into published reports, with a review trigger where a state's condition falls. The design lens favors published counts and review triggers over gates enforced up front. Safety and local access (A-25) stay as chosen: they set shares, not reviews.

## Fork 6. Intercity passenger rail (memo 6)

- **The question.** Division D is $254.03B over FY2032-36, the largest new federal rail commitment, in a country with high construction costs and a thin pipeline of corridors ready to build.
- **As chosen (option A).** It already ties money to readiness:
  - Tier 1 corridors are capped in dollars;
  - a contingent reserve of up to $25B is released only as projects clear their gates;
  - terminal money is a single program.
- **Recommendation: A.** Memo 6 weighs firmer gates (B) and a slower start for high-speed lines (C).

## Recommended without a memo

Each point is a yes-or-no question for Espen. None is adopted by silence.

1. **If no multi-year law is in place by the end of 2028, authorize programs in 2029-30.** Under baseline cases 2 and 3, the plan writes a regular-order authorization for FY2029-31 or FY2030-34 alongside the 2029 act, and the 2031 bill becomes its renewal. Recommended: yes. The reconciliation acts cannot authorize programs (the Byrd rule), so without this the money has nowhere to go.
2. **The 2028 extension (A-9), as a labeled 2027-28 exception.** It is a one-year extension of the expiring rates and fund credits, plus a transfer through FY2031, with no new revenue in FY2029. Recommended: yes. It keeps 2029's forcing event: an expiring tax base to reform.
3. **Money paths and timing (A-14, A-15, A-16).**
   - Contract authority is capped at receipts.
   - Renewal top-ups go to programs in the two reconciliation acts.
   - Expansion is appropriated in the 2031 bill.
   - The packages obligate over FY2029-31.

   Recommended: yes. Obligating each package in its enactment year leaves a $26.7B rail cliff [EV-MOV-217], and placing top-ups by fiscal year would make the 2030 act fail the Byrd out-year test [EV-MOV-218].
4. **The Charging Gap Program replaces NEVI (A-27).** National reverse auctions on a published gap map: half paid at energization, half over five years for uptime, a 24-month lapse rule, unspent NEVI balances folded in. It costs $2.63B of formula money, with no new money, but the method is untested at federal scale [EV-MOV-216]. Recommended: yes, with the auction treated as something to study in implementation (section 8).
5. **Resilience, clean fuels and freight (A-26).**
   - A 4% resilience set-aside.
   - The Carbon Reduction Program restored inside formula money.
   - The alternative-fuel set-aside held at 10%.
   - The 91,000-lb truck pilot ends in 2031.
   - 15% of freight formula money goes to truck parking.

   The set-asides move about $2.6B a year of formula money out of state discretion [EV-MOV-215]. Recommended: yes, kept as set-asides of existing money rather than new programs.
6. **One renewal definition for all three accounts (A-19); drafted dates shifted one year, except the transit base years (A-20).** Recommended: yes.
7. **A transportation outcome.** Add to `charter/outcomes.md`: "People can get where they need to go, safely and affordably." Measures:
   - traffic deaths (NHTSA's FARS);
   - the share of roads and bridges in poor condition (FHWA);
   - transportation's share of household spending (BLS);
   - transit access for households without a car;
   - transportation emissions (EPA).

   Recommended: yes. It is a tier-3 charter change in its own PR.
8. **Division letters and the hybrid highway division (A-1, A-2).** Drafting choices; recommended: keep. If no five-year law passes, the hybrid becomes a full drafted title I (point 1).

## How the choices fit together

- **Revenue and size are linked through the general fund, not through each other.** Memos 1 and 2 set the dedicated receipts. Memo 3 sets what is spent. The difference is the general-fund draw, which the settlement balances plan-wide. A smaller revenue choice in memo 1 raises the draw without changing a program.
- **The split (memo 3) decides which account becomes self-sufficient first.** Memo 4's operating help and memo 6's rail program sit on accounts that reach self-sufficiency late, so they lean longest on the general fund.
- **Conditions (memo 5) change no figure.** They change what states may do with formula money.
- **The baseline (point 1) changes timing, not design.** If no five-year law is in place, the 2031 bill's programs move earlier, and the 2029 act is unchanged.

- **Weight-distance experience.** Oregon's evasion and collection-cost figures are now in section 2 [EV-MOV-118] [EV-MOV-122]; they inform section 5's audits, not the choice.
- **Transit capital costs.** The Transit Costs Project's ranking is in section 2 [EV-MOV-125]. An independent study that corroborates or qualifies it would inform gate terms, not the choice.
