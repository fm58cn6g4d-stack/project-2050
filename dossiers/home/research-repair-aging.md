# Research note: repair and renovation; aging in place

Role: research for the section 3 addendum, part 2 of 2 (board step C-4i). On November 9, 2026 Espen asked for two things the plan had not weighed: making sure homeowners and renters can affordably repair or renovate their homes, which crosses with the clean-power agenda; and policies to help older people stay in their homes as they age, if they want to but are at financial risk of losing them, under an "age with dignity" frame, which crosses with care. Part 1 (`research-ownership-supply.md`, PR #158, memos 9 and 10) covers buying a home and broad supply. This note covers repair and aging in place. Its forks are in `memos/11-repair.md` and `memos/12-aging-in-place.md`. Status: draft (stage C), awaiting Espen's decisions. It does not edit sections 4-9.

Evidence is EV-HOU-600 to 699 in `evidence.json`, plus earlier entries where cited; sources are SRC-1900 to 1997. Executive-branch counts and program rules are data. Causal claims by agencies are analysis, and those from the second Trump administration (2025 on) are presented as that administration's view (`DEC-2026-10-30-01`). Many licensed sources were read through a search tool because their hosts block downloads; the catalog rows say which, and the reviewer should check those quotations against the originals.

## In brief

1. **The repair need is large and falls on low-income owners.** Needed repairs to occupied homes cost about $198.4 billion in 2024; households under 200 percent of poverty carry $74.6 billion of it, and 5.4 million households face repairs of $10,000 or more [EV-HOU-602]. About 3.3 million homes with young children have lead hazards [EV-HOU-603]. Under 5 percent of homes are livable for someone with moderate mobility limits [EV-HOU-608].
2. **Federal repair help is small, fragmented and often hard to use.** HOME finished 2,488 owner repairs in fiscal 2024 at about $57,724 each, because a HOME repair must bring the whole house to code [EV-HOU-609] [EV-HOU-610]. Weatherization serves about 32,000 homes a year with DOE money [EV-HOU-618] and turns away about one in five low-income homes because they need repairs first [EV-HOU-623] [EV-HOU-639]. The ROAD to Housing Act's whole-home repair pilot exists only if appropriated and ends in 2031 [EV-HOU-230].
3. **Simple emergency repair programs reach many owners cheaply; rule-heavy ones reach few.** Philadelphia's program averaged about $8,900 a repair and repairs over 3,000 homes a year [EV-HOU-647] [EV-HOU-649], with one study linking repairs to less crime on the block [EV-HOU-646]. Pennsylvania's Whole-Home Repairs drew 25,715 applications, repaired 1,151 homes by mid-2024 and made one landlord loan, because counties declined its 15-year rent terms and renter paperwork [EV-HOU-656] [EV-HOU-657].
4. **Renters need repair money paired with a light rent term.** Enforced repairs can raise rents [EV-HOU-668] and orders to vacate can displace tenants [EV-HOU-667]. Light terms tied to public money, like the ROAD Act pilot's three-year cap [EV-HOU-230], New York's limits on passing upgrade costs into rent [EV-HOU-665] and the federal energy rebates' two-year rule [EV-HOU-632], are the precedents.
5. **Clean power needs repair.** The home energy credits end after 2025 [EV-HOU-625] [EV-HOU-626] and went mostly to higher-income households [EV-HOU-628]. DOE's 2026 notices narrowed the state energy rebates and dropped their low-income reservation [EV-HOU-630] [EV-HOU-631]. The legacy clean-power agenda pays for low-income heat pumps (CLI-5W), but homes that need roofs or wiring first will be turned away unless repair money is joined to it.
6. **Older owners' risks are taxes, repairs, titles and loans.** Owners 65 and over have the highest cost-burden rate of any age group, 28 percent, and property taxes rose 31 percent from 2019 to 2025 [EV-HOU-670]. Higher property taxes raise older owners' moving rates [EV-HOU-681]. Tangled titles, reverse-mortgage defaults and tax foreclosure take homes and equity [EV-HOU-684] [EV-HOU-678] [EV-HOU-677].
7. **Relief works when it is automatic.** Circuit breakers exist in 29 states and DC [EV-HOU-682]; California's deferral program, with yearly requalification, reached about 1,000 of more than a million eligible owners [EV-HOU-683].
8. **Home changes help; their lasting effect and savings are less certain.** CAPABLE cut disability scores 30 percent in a randomized trial at $2,825 a person [EV-HOU-686]; the effect faded by 12 months and Medicare's savings estimate is nonrandomized [EV-HOU-688] [EV-HOU-687].
9. **Older renters are the largest group the plan leaves out.** 2.46 million older renter households had worst case needs in 2023 [EV-HOU-672]. No new Section 202 homes have been funded since 2012 [EV-HOU-672]. Espen decided to reconsider older non-disabled renters' rental help once the care program is scoped (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`); memo 12 does not reopen that, and weighs buildings and services instead.

## 4. Renter-side tools

- **Landlord repair loans.** Philadelphia's Rental Improvement Fund lends small landlords up to $100,000, forgiven if rents rise no more than 3 percent and code violations are fixed [EV-HOU-651]; stakeholders reported low uptake because landlords feared enforcement and the rules [EV-HOU-652]. Pennsylvania's 15-year terms produced one loan [EV-HOU-656].
- **City repair funds tied to code enforcement.** New York City's Alternative Enforcement Program has put 4,137 buildings through repairs and discharged 81 percent; it billed owners over $108 million and collected $88 million, through liens on the tax bill [EV-HOU-663]. HPD says it works best on larger buildings whose owners have money but neglect upkeep [EV-HOU-664].
- **Receivership.** The Massachusetts Attorney General's fund financed rehabilitation of 88 blighted properties with 181 homes, turning $4 million of revolving loans into $7.80 million of work [EV-HOU-669] (mostly vacant properties; one publisher's report).
- **Keeping rents from jumping after subsidized work.** New York's 2019 law caps rent increases for building upgrades at 2 percent a year, removes them after thirty years and subtracts any government grant [EV-HOU-665]. The federal energy rebates bar raising rent because of the upgrade for two years [EV-HOU-632]. The ROAD Act pilot caps rent increases at 5 percent or inflation for 3 years [EV-HOU-230].
- **Proactive inspection.** Los Angeles inspects rentals about every four years for a $67.94 per-unit fee, half of which can be passed to tenants, with rent escrow for owners who do not fix violations [EV-HOU-666].
- **The displacement risk.** San Antonio issued at least 607 orders to vacate or demolish occupied homes in 2015-20, and only 4 percent of households given vacate orders got relocation help [EV-HOU-667]. In Chicago, resolved violations were linked to higher rents [EV-HOU-668].

**Reading.** Repair money for renters works through landlords, cities and receivers, and each route needs a rent term light enough that landlords take the money, plus relocation help where enforcement closes a home. That is memo 11, part 2.

## 5. Where repair meets clean power

**The home energy credits are gone by 2029.** The 25C improvement credit (30 percent, up to $3,200 a year) and the 25D clean energy credit end after December 31, 2025 [EV-HOU-625] [EV-HOU-626]. For tax year 2023, 2.3 million families claimed more than $2 billion of 25C and over 1.2 million claimed over $6 billion of 25D [EV-HOU-627]. Over two decades the top fifth of households got about 60 percent of clean energy credits and the bottom three fifths about 10 percent (Borenstein and Davis, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2024) [EV-HOU-628]. The legacy clean-power agenda restores them as refundable, point-of-sale credits (CLI-1A); that is the clean-power dossier's decision.

**The state rebates survive, narrowed.** The Inflation Reduction Act's $8.8 billion of home rebates run until the money is spent or September 30, 2031 [EV-HOU-632]; by September 2024 four states had rebates open [EV-HOU-629]. DOE's May 2026 notices, under the second Trump administration, end rebates for switching from gas or oil to electric equipment, require insulation before heating upgrades, drop the 40 percent low-income reservation and remove on-site inspections [EV-HOU-630] [EV-HOU-631]. The two-year renter protection remains in DOE's rules [EV-HOU-632].

**Weatherization's savings are contested; present both views.**
- In a randomized study of about 30,000 Michigan households, projected savings were more than three times actual savings and costs about twice the savings (Fowlie, Greenstone and Wolfram, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2018) [EV-HOU-636]. In Illinois, model bias and uneven workmanship, not households using more energy, explained most of the gap (Christensen and others, 2023) [EV-HOU-637].
- DOE's national evaluation of 2008 found $4,890 of energy savings per home against $4,695 of cost, plus $14,148 of modeled health and home benefits [EV-HOU-638]. DOE cites $2.78 of non-energy benefits per dollar [EV-HOU-619]. These are the agency's evaluation of its own program.
- *Reading.* On energy alone, weatherization is at best break-even. Its case rests on health, comfort and safety, which are the same benefits repair programs claim. That supports running them together.

**Repairs block energy work.** About 19 percent of income-eligible households seeking weatherization in 2023 were deferred because the home needed repairs, and about 40 percent of those were never served; the leading causes were roof leaks, floor damage and unsafe panels [EV-HOU-639] [EV-HOU-623]. About 21 percent of homes have panels of 100 amps or less, and panel and service upgrades can cost $2,000 to $30,000 [EV-HOU-640]. Congress added a Weatherization Readiness Fund in 2022 [EV-HOU-622]; DOE allocated $30 million to it in July 2025 [EV-HOU-621]. ACEEE estimates about $94 million a year would fix nearly all the homes the program defers [EV-HOU-623].

**Floods and heat.** FEMA's main mitigation grant program was ended in April 2025, ruled unlawful in December 2025 and reopened with $1 billion in 2026; the infrastructure law's extra money ends after FY2026 [EV-HOU-641]. The National Flood Insurance Program has nearly 4.5 million policies and is authorized only until December 11, 2026 [EV-HOU-642]. Heat killed 2,391 people in 2024, with the largest share at ages 65-74 [EV-HOU-643], and LIHEAP's cooling help reached only 751,000 households in FY2024 against 5 million for heating [EV-HOU-644]. The legacy agenda's household resilience credit and disaster programs (CLI-5, CLI-7) belong to clean power; memo 11's fund pays for repairs (roofs, cooling, wiring) that also improve resilience, but adds no separate resilience program.

**Reading.** The overlap is a sequencing problem. The clean-power plan pays for heat pumps and weatherization; housing's repair fund can pay for the roofs and wiring that let those programs serve the homes that need them most. Memo 11, part 3 weighs one front door.

## 6. Older owners' financial risks

- **Cost burden.** In 2023, 34 percent of older households, over 12.4 million, were cost burdened: 58 percent of older renters (4.5 million households) and 7.9 million older owners [EV-HOU-671]. In 2024 owners 65 and over had the highest burden rate of any age group, 28 percent: 29 percent with a mortgage, 16 percent without [EV-HOU-670].
- **Property taxes.** Taxes rose 31 percent from 2019 to 2025 [EV-HOU-670]. A $100 rise in annual property tax raised two-year moving rates of owners over 50 by 0.76 percentage points, about 8 percent, an upper bound (Shan, Federal Reserve working paper, 2008) [EV-HOU-681].
- **Tax foreclosure.** In Tyler v. Hennepin County (2023) a unanimous Court held that a county that sold a home for $40,000 to collect about $15,000 of tax and kept the $25,000 surplus plausibly took property without compensation; thirty-six states already required the surplus returned [EV-HOU-677]. The ruling protects equity left after a sale; it does not prevent the sale.
- **Tangled titles and heirs' property.** Philadelphia had at least 10,407 homes with tangled titles, worth over $1.1 billion; clearing one costs about $9,200 [EV-HOU-684]. A tangled title can block repair grants, tax relief and insurance. The Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act was enacted in 24 states, the Virgin Islands and DC by the end of 2025 [EV-HOU-685] (other counts run higher; check before citing).
- **Reverse mortgages.** FHA insured 28,149 reverse mortgages (HECMs) in FY2025, and the HECM book has a stand-alone capital ratio of 24.06 percent [EV-HOU-679]. Borrower defaults, mostly unpaid taxes and insurance or occupancy, rose from 2 percent of loan endings in FY2014 to 18 percent in FY2018, and only about 22 percent of borrowers behind on charges got a repayment plan (Government Accountability Office, 2019) [EV-HOU-678]. Since 2023 servicers may wait while arrears are $5,000 or less and offer repayment plans of up to five years [EV-HOU-680]; that is agency policy, which a later administration can change.

## 7. Property-tax circuit breakers and deferrals

- **Who has them.** 29 states and DC had circuit breakers in 2025, most aimed at seniors; costs run from about three one-hundredths of a percent of property-tax collections in Washington to 9 percent in Vermont, and average benefits from $100 to $2,000 (Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy, 2026, an advocacy group) [EV-HOU-682].
- **Deferral take-up is low.** California's postponement program had about 1,000 participants against over a million potentially eligible older owners, with yearly requalification and high costs to participants (California Legislative Analyst's Office, 2018) [EV-HOU-683].
- **Federal role today.** None found. No current federal circuit-breaker bill was found.

## 8. Home modification, and Medicaid home care as it touches housing

- **Falls.** 14 million older adults (27.6 percent) reported a fall in 2020; 38,742 died from falls in 2021; medical costs are about $50 billion a year [EV-HOU-690].
- **CAPABLE.** In a randomized trial of 300 low-income older adults in Baltimore, nurse and occupational-therapist visits plus up to $1,300 of repairs and devices cut daily-living disability scores 30 percent at the end of the program, at $2,825 a person (Szanton and others, *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2019) [EV-HOU-686].
- **Its savings are contested; present both views.** A nonrandomized evaluation of the CMS Innovation Center demonstration estimated Medicare savings of $22,000 over two years per participant [EV-HOU-687]. An independent review notes the disability benefit was not maintained at 12 months and only 171 of 281 participants were in the cost analysis [EV-HOU-688].
- **HUD's grants.** More than $109 million to 67 grantees in January 2025 [EV-HOU-689]; an August 2024 round awarded more than $3.7 million to serve more than 525 families, about $7,000 each (arithmetic) [EV-HOU-673].
- **Medicaid home care.** Forty states keep waiting lists with almost 650,000 people, who wait 34 months on average [EV-HOU-691]. From July 1, 2028, states may run home-care waivers for people below nursing-home level of care, which cannot pay room and board [EV-HOU-693]. The July 2025 law also shortens retroactive coverage from 2027, caps the home-equity limit for long-term-care eligibility at $1,000,000 from 2028 [EV-HOU-694] and squeezes the provider taxes that help states fund waivers, to 5 percent in FY2029 for expansion states [EV-HOU-695]. Money Follows the Person, which moved older adults out of nursing homes with spending falls of up to $1,840 a month and found housing the most-reported barrier [EV-HOU-696], expires after FY2027 under current law [EV-HOU-697].

**Reading.** Medicaid can pay for some home changes, but not rent, and its waiting lists are long. Housing can pay for repairs and changes through one front door (memo 11's fund, memo 12 part 2); the care program decides the clinical benefit and Medicaid home care.

## 9. Accessory units, home sharing and older renters

- **Accessory units.** 75 percent of adults 50 and older want to stay in their home [EV-HOU-699]. In California, 62 percent of owners who built an accessory unit relied at least partly on cash or family money, at a median cost of $150,000 [EV-HOU-675]. Title I loans can now finance one [EV-HOU-220]. No age breakdown of builders was found.
- **Home sharing.** HomeShare Vermont matched 364 people in FY2026 at an average rent of $413, on about $0.7 million of public money (self-report, no comparison group) [EV-HOU-692].
- **Older renters.** 2.46 million older renter households had worst case needs in 2023, and fewer than 38.8 percent of very low-income older households got any rental help [EV-HOU-672]; 1.749 million extremely low-income older households had severe problems [EV-HOU-186]. Section 202 had $1,031 million in FY2026, renewing rent contracts for about 118,000 households (average income $16,854) and about 1,500 service coordinators; no new homes have been funded since FY2012 [EV-HOU-672]. The FY2026 budget proposed ending it [EV-HOU-674]. The ROAD Act asks GAO to study 202's capital advances [EV-HOU-676].
- **Service coordination.** Vermont's housing-based care coordination slowed Medicare spending growth by about $1,100 a person a year in one group, but not across all participants (HHS evaluation, 2019) [EV-HOU-698].

## 10. Where this meets the care program, and what the plan already does for older renters

**What the plan does now.**
- Stage 1 of the rental guarantee (2030-33) reaches older people only if they are without a home (HOU-4.03).
- Stage 2 (from 2034) is for families with children.
- Stage 3 (from 2038) is for extremely low-income renters with long-term disabilities; its age limit is undecided (`6-cost.md`, question 2), and many older renters can meet a disability test (`7-validation.md`).
- Older non-disabled renters are reconsidered once the care program is scoped (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`).
- The 1437a rent rule deducts medical costs above 10 percent of income for elderly and disabled families [EV-HOU-409] and claws back 30 percent of Social Security increases (`7-validation.md`).

## 12. What it means for the plan

- **Memo 11** recommends a standing, ten-year home repair fund through the existing grantee network, with one plain income test and no credit test (part 1 C); landlord loans with the ROAD Act pilot's three-year rent term, city repair funds, receivership and relocation help (part 2 B); and one front door with the clean-power programs, the fund paying for readiness repairs (part 3 B).
- **Memo 12** recommends a federal match for state property-tax relief that enrolls owners from records and offers deferral (part 1 B); an older-adult track in the repair fund on the CAPABLE model, with the clinical part left to care (part 2 B); legal help for titles and wills, reverse-mortgage relief in law and a payment-plan offer before tax sales (part 3 B); and HOU-3C pointed at older renters' homes, with service coordinators (part 4 C).
- **Design lens.** Every recommendation uses self-attestation or records the government holds, no credit or tax-current tests, published counts and a review instead of caps, and conditions only on new money. Where the evidence shows rules choking a program (Pennsylvania's landlord terms [EV-HOU-657], California's yearly requalification [EV-HOU-683], Detroit's credit test [EV-HOU-661]), the memos drop them.
- **With part 1.** Memo 10's manufactured-home loans and its reform list cover accessory units in the zoning reward; FHA's Limited 203(k), now up to $75,000 [EV-HOU-617], stays a buyer's tool (part 1's note covers it as buyer finance). Part 1 finds manufactured-home owners older on average (`research-ownership-supply.md`, section 4); they can use memo 11's fund.
- **For other dossiers.** Clean power confirms the readiness link when it weighs CLI-5 and CLI-5W, and owns the energy credits, rebates, LIHEAP cooling and resilience. Care decides CAPABLE's clinical benefit, Medicaid home care, Money Follows the Person and older renters' rental help.
