# Research note: supportive housing with ongoing services, and treatment that works

Role: research for the housing specification (section 4) after Espen approved memo 5, option B, on November 9, 2026: Housing First plus a voluntary recovery-housing option (`memos/5-homelessness.md`). Espen asked for more research on behavioral health and addiction treatment best practices, and on whether supportive housing with ongoing services can spread nationally "in a way where you're not threatening people with a return to homelessness if they don't accept treatment but that there is an expectation of engaging with service navigators/etc at a baseline." Status: draft (stage C). It recommends; Espen decides. It does not reopen memo 5. Evidence is EV-HOU-300 to 339 in `evidence.json`, plus earlier entries where cited.

Executive-branch sources from the second Trump administration (2025 on) are presented as that administration's view (`DEC-2026-10-30-01`). Where a quotation comes from a page read through a search tool because the host blocks downloads (CDC, SAMHSA, HRSA, ASPE), the catalog row says so and the reviewer should check it against the original.

## In brief

1. **The treatments with the strongest evidence are medicines and practical supports, not conditions.** Methadone and buprenorphine cut deaths sharply while people stay on them [EV-HOU-300] [EV-HOU-301]. Naloxone and syringe services prevent deaths and infections and bring people into treatment [EV-HOU-302] [EV-HOU-303]. Small rewards for negative drug tests (contingency management) are the best-tested treatment for stimulants such as methamphetamine [EV-HOU-304] [EV-HOU-305]. Assertive community treatment, a team that brings care to the person, helps homeless people with serious mental illness stay housed [EV-HOU-308] [EV-HOU-309].
2. **Making treatment a condition does not have good support.** Reviews of compulsory treatment find no overall benefit [EV-HOU-313], though court-mandated patients in one large cohort did as well as volunteers [EV-HOU-314]. The best treatment-first trial raised abstinence [EV-HOU-306], but most of its participants never reached stable housing [EV-HOU-307].
3. **Supportive housing keeps people housed; it does not reliably pay for itself.** Two recent U.S. trials housed most of those offered it and kept them housed [EV-HOU-316] [EV-HOU-317]. Savings elsewhere offset about half the cost in Denver [EV-HOU-318]. The National Academies advise not to expect net savings [EV-HOU-315].
4. **The model Espen describes already exists, and it is the original Housing First.** Pathways to Housing, the program that started Housing First, required regular staff visits but not treatment [EV-HOU-322]. Its fidelity scale scores a weekly visit as full fidelity [EV-HOU-323]. Canada's national trial required a weekly visit [EV-HOU-321]. So "no treatment condition, but an expectation of contact" is Housing First as designed, not a departure from it.
5. **The open design question is what happens when someone doesn't engage.** HUD-VASH, the veterans' program, ends that voucher if a veteran won't take part in case management, though the family may be moved to a regular voucher [EV-HOU-325]. Finland treats silence as a reason to try harder: housing counsellors keep trying and notify social services before any eviction for unpaid rent [EV-HOU-327]. SAMHSA's own scale treats "services expected, but no eviction for refusing" as a middle level of fidelity [EV-HOU-324].
6. **Current federal rules point the other way.** The 2025 executive order asks HUD to require treatment as a condition of help [EV-HOU-107]. HUD's 2026 competition rewards treatment and required participation [EV-HOU-334], and an appeals court has let it proceed [EV-HOU-335]. A president taking office in 2029 can reverse all three.
7. **Money for services is the hard part.** The ROAD to Housing Act adds none (`road-act.md`). Medicaid tenancy supports are the largest possible source, but CMS withdrew its 2023 guidance [EV-HOU-339]. From 2027 waivers must not raise federal spending [EV-HOU-337], and work requirements begin [EV-HOU-336]. The care flagship's Medicaid restoration and HCBS work (HLT-1, HLT-4) are where most of that money would come from.
8. **Workforce limits scale more than money does.** HRSA projects large shortages of addiction counselors, psychiatrists and mental health counselors [EV-HOU-338]. The federal design has to fund case managers, peers and teams, not only units.

## 1. What works in treatment for people who are or were homeless

| Practice | What it is | What the evidence shows | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Medications for opioid use disorder (methadone, buprenorphine) | Daily or long-acting medicines that block cravings and withdrawal | Deaths per thousand person-years fell from 36.1 to 11.3 in methadone treatment and from 9.5 to 4.3 for buprenorphine [EV-HOU-300]; up to half as likely to die on long-term treatment, and lack of counseling is no reason to withhold them [EV-HOU-301]. The weeks starting methadone and right after leaving either treatment are the most dangerous [EV-HOU-300] | Strong (large cohort meta-analysis, National Academies consensus); observational, but consistent |
| Naloxone | Overdose-reversal medicine handed out to people at risk and those around them | Meets all nine standard tests for a causal effect; reduces overdose deaths among participants and in the community [EV-HOU-302] | Moderate to strong (observational; the review's authors have naloxone interests) |
| Syringe services | Clean supplies, testing and referral | Users are five times as likely to enter treatment; safe and cost saving without raising drug use or crime, in CDC's summary [EV-HOU-303] | Moderate (observational); the 2025 order bars SAMHSA discretionary grants for "harm reduction" [EV-HOU-107] |
| Contingency management | Small, escalating rewards (gift cards) for negative drug tests | The best-tested treatment for stimulants: medium-to-large effects across 74 trials [EV-HOU-304]; in the VA, 41% lower death risk over a year in a matched cohort [EV-HOU-305] | Strong for short-term abstinence; long-term effects and effects for homeless people specifically are less studied |
| Assertive community treatment (ACT) | A team (psychiatry, nursing, case management, peers) that brings most care to the person | Reduced homelessness 37% more and symptoms 26% more than standard case management for homeless people with serious mental illness [EV-HOU-308]. Intensive case management reduced days homeless and substance use; standard case management did little [EV-HOU-309] | Strong for housing stability; hospital use unchanged [EV-HOU-308] |
| Integrated behavioral health clinics (CCBHCs) | Clinics paid to offer crisis, addiction and mental health care in one place | Lower hospitalization in some states, such as 23% in Nevada, but no net savings [EV-HOU-310] | Moderate, mixed, not homeless-specific; one evaluation |
| Recovery housing | Sober shared housing, such as Oxford Houses | Positive outcomes, but few, small, single-site studies: "moderate" evidence [EV-HOU-311]. SAMHSA says recovery homes should not bar prescribed medicines, including for addiction [EV-HOU-312] | Moderate; not tested for people coming straight from homelessness |

**Treatment as a condition: both sides.**
- *Against.* A review of nine studies of compulsory treatment found no overall improvement, and some harm [EV-HOU-313]. That review includes drug detention abroad, which is not what U.S. proposals mean.
- *For.* In a VA cohort of 2,095, people mandated by courts did as well as or better than volunteers, which the authors read as support for mandates as a door into treatment [EV-HOU-314]. NIDA's research guide also says treatment need not be voluntary to work (not in the register).
- *Housing tied to abstinence.* In the Birmingham trial, housing that required abstinence produced more abstinence than housing that did not [EV-HOU-306]. But in the same team's follow-up, only 34.1% had stable housing at a year, partly because housing programs would not take anyone short of perfect abstinence [EV-HOU-307]. Housing First trials show the reverse pattern: much more housing stability, no clear change in drug use [EV-HOU-099] [EV-HOU-100].
- *Reading.* Conditions can raise treatment entry. They also cost housing stability, and the people who fail the condition end up back outside. Medicines, contingency management and ACT work without making housing the stake.

**What this means for people who are or were homeless (judgment).** The practices that work share three features: they are offered where people are, they keep people alive while they are still using, and they reward progress rather than punish relapse. A supportive housing program should be able to deliver or connect people to all of them on site or by visit: buprenorphine or methadone, naloxone in every unit, contingency management for stimulants, and ACT or intensive case management for people with serious mental illness.

## 2. Permanent supportive housing: the evidence

**Housing First is a philosophy; supportive housing is a program.** Permanent supportive housing is a lease with no end date plus services. Housing First is the rule for getting in and staying: no sobriety or treatment test to enter or remain. The two can separate: a supportive housing program can attach conditions, as the HUD-VASH rule and the 2026 competition show (section 3).

**It keeps people housed.**
- In Santa Clara County, Eighty-six percent of chronically homeless frequent users offered supportive housing were housed, against 36 percent with usual care. Psychiatric emergency visits and shelter days fell; total emergency, hospital and jail use did not [EV-HOU-316].
- In Denver, 79 percent of frequent jail users referred were located, engaged and housed, 77 percent of those living were still housed at three years, and jail days fell 27 percent [EV-HOU-317].
- These match the earlier trials: 71% of time stably housed against 29% in Canada [EV-HOU-099], and large housing gains in reviews [EV-HOU-100] [EV-HOU-101].

**It does not reliably pay for itself.**
- The National Academies found no substantial evidence (except for HIV/AIDS) that it improves health or cuts health costs, and advised against expecting net savings [EV-HOU-315].
- Denver offset about half its cost, $6,876 per person a year [EV-HOU-318]. In Canada, each $10 returned $9.60 for the highest-need group and $3.42 for moderate needs [EV-HOU-320].
- Pre-post studies show savings; randomized studies show net costs [EV-HOU-319]. The Community Guide found benefits exceeding costs in U.S. studies [EV-HOU-102], partly by counting benefits beyond public budgets.
- *Reading.* Savings are real and largest for the highest-cost people, so the case for supportive housing should rest on housing people, with savings as a partial offset. Section 6 should count offsets only for the highest-need group.

**Across whole communities** the effect is smaller and contested (`2-what-works.md`; [EV-HOU-175] [EV-HOU-083]). The second Trump administration's HUD argues that chronic homelessness rose 80.5 percent since 2013 despite a 44 percent rise in supportive housing beds [EV-HOU-333]. That is a trend, not a causal estimate: it does not hold other causes fixed, such as rents (`2-what-works.md`, section 1), and independent studies find more supportive housing goes with somewhat less chronic homelessness [EV-HOU-175].

## 3. "Low-barrier but not no-contact": models with an expectation of engagement

### How programs set the expectation

| Model | What is expected | What is not required | What happens if someone doesn't engage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pathways to Housing (New York) | Rent at 30% of income through money management; meeting staff at least twice a month, "applied flexibly" [EV-HOU-322] | Treatment, sobriety | Not a ground for losing housing; the requirements are "applied flexibly" [EV-HOU-322] |
| Pathways fidelity scale (2013) | A standard lease and one face-to-face visit a week, the top fidelity score [EV-HOU-323] | Everything else can be refused [EV-HOU-323] | Not a ground for losing housing |
| At Home/Chez Soi (Canada's national trial) | Meeting a support team member at least once a week [EV-HOU-321] | Treatment | The sources in the register do not say; the trial housed people far more often than usual care [EV-HOU-099] |
| SAMHSA supportive housing scale (2010) | Middle score: rules require ongoing services, but refusing them does not lead to eviction [EV-HOU-324] | Top score: tenancy not contingent on any program participation [EV-HOU-324] | Lowest score: tenancy revoked for not participating [EV-HOU-324] |
| HUD-VASH (veterans) | VA case management as a condition of the voucher; the VA decides whether it is needed and met [EV-HOU-325] | Treatment, sobriety | Non-participation "without good cause" ends HUD-VASH assistance; the housing agency may, not must, offer a regular voucher [EV-HOU-325]. Graduating from case management never ends the voucher [EV-HOU-326] |
| Finland (Y-Foundation and housing advice) | An ordinary lease and contact with housing counsellors, mainly when rent falls behind | Sobriety or any other term [EV-HOU-327] | Repeated contact attempts, then a notice to social services; eviction for unpaid rent only after that [EV-HOU-327] |
| Continuum of Care rule (U.S., 2012) | Projects may require services that are not disability-related, such as case management, to stay in the program [EV-HOU-332] | Treatment, except in projects whose purpose is addiction treatment [EV-HOU-332] | The rule allows ending program participation, which in supportive housing can mean losing the unit |
| Medicaid tenancy supports (Washington's Foundational Community Supports) | Voluntary help finding and keeping housing, paid by Medicaid | Everything | Not a housing condition at all; exits from homelessness about 4 percentage points higher than a matched group [EV-HOU-328] |

**What the models share (judgment).** In each case the expectation is contact with a person, not compliance with a treatment plan. The difference between models is whether the program or the tenant carries the burden when contact lapses. In Pathways, At Home/Chez Soi and Finland, a missed visit obliges staff to try again. In HUD-VASH and under the Continuum of Care rule, it can cost the person their assistance.

### Where expectations shade into coercion

- **The line Espen drew is the line the evidence supports.** Requiring treatment raises treatment use without better outcomes in the founding Housing First trial (`2-what-works.md`; [EV-HOU-098]), and compulsory treatment has weak support [EV-HOU-313]. A visit is different: it is how staff notice a crisis, a lapse in rent or a medical problem early.
- **Even a visit requirement can feel like a price.** At Home/Chez Soi's Toronto site found the weekly visit may have hindered recovery for some, such as people with jobs who did not want to be seen with caseworkers [EV-HOU-331]. Residents of Housing First programs perceive less coercion than other supportive housing residents, with the same satisfaction [EV-HOU-330]. No rigorous study in the register tests whether a contact expectation itself lowers engagement or retention; that is a gap.
- **The administration uses the same history to argue the other way.** HUD's September 2026 notice says Pathways' original visits and HUD-VASH show Housing First never ruled out participation requirements [EV-HOU-333]. On the history, it is right: the original model expected contact [EV-HOU-322] [EV-HOU-323]. But HUD then uses that point to reward treatment and services requirements and to move $1.3 billion toward transitional housing and services-only projects [EV-HOU-334], which the trials do not support [EV-HOU-101].
- **Signals that an expectation has become coercion (judgment):** losing housing, a subsidy or a place on a list for not engaging; a requirement to accept a specific treatment or medicine; contact rules applied to some tenants and not to others; a provider allowed to exit people for non-engagement without first showing it tried to reach them.

**Civil commitment and court-ordered treatment** (New York's 2025 expansion of involuntary commitment, California's CARE Court) are a separate question from supportive housing and are outside this note. The executive order pushes civil commitment for people living on the street [EV-HOU-107]; the evidence on it belongs in the care flagship and the public safety work.

## 4. How the model could scale federally

### The baseline the plan inherits in 2029

- **Executive policy.** The July 2025 order directs HUD to require treatment as a condition of participation and ends support for Housing First [EV-HOU-107]. A president can revoke it in January 2029.
- **The Continuum of Care competition.** HUD's FY2026 notice sets aside $1.3 billion of $4.04 billion for transitional housing and services-only projects and scores treatment and required participation; Congress required at least 60 percent of each community's renewals to be funded [EV-HOU-334]. The district court vacated it, the First Circuit stayed that order, and HUD must award by December 1, 2026 [EV-HOU-335]. The 2027 and 2028 competitions will likely follow the same design (judgment), so some supportive housing may have converted to transitional housing by 2029.
- **The CoC rule** already lets projects require case-management participation, not treatment [EV-HOU-332]. The design below tightens that: participation can be expected, but never enforced with loss of housing.
- **The ROAD to Housing Act** has nothing on Continuum of Care services, Medicaid or SAMHSA; its homelessness provisions are the shelter-spending waiver for FY2027-2030 [EV-HOU-237], HUD-VASH income rules [EV-HOU-233] and a new national plan due July 2027 [EV-HOU-238] (`road-act.md`).
- **Medicaid.** CMS rescinded its 2023 and 2024 guidance on housing and social-needs services in waivers in March 2025 and now reviews requests case by case [EV-HOU-339]. From January 1, 2027, a waiver can be approved or renewed only if CMS's actuary certifies it does not raise federal spending [EV-HOU-337]. Work requirements start by January 1, 2027, and homelessness alone is not an exemption, though addiction or a disabling mental disorder can be [EV-HOU-336]. Minnesota's Medicaid housing benefit ended on November 1, 2025, after the state found credible allegations of fraud by providers [EV-HOU-329].

### The funding streams, and what each can carry

| Stream | What it can pay for | Limits | Fit for the model |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HUD Continuum of Care | Rent and operating costs, case management and other services in supportive housing [EV-HOU-332] | Competitive and renewed yearly; service dollars are small and fixed; rules set by each year's notice [EV-HOU-334] | Core rent and operating money; the place to write the engagement standard into rules |
| HUD vouchers (Housing Choice, HUD-VASH, Emergency Housing Vouchers) | Rent | No services money of their own; HUD-VASH ties services to the VA [EV-HOU-325] | Rent for scattered-site units; the guarantee (HOU-4A) can carry it |
| Medicaid: HCBS state plan (1915(i)), waivers (1915(c)), section 1115 | Tenancy-sustaining services, housing navigation, ACT, peers, addiction treatment; not rent | Rescinded guidance [EV-HOU-339], budget neutrality from 2027 [EV-HOU-337], coverage losses from work requirements [EV-HOU-336], fraud risk in fee-for-service navigation [EV-HOU-329] | The largest possible services source; depends on the care flagship |
| Medicaid core benefits | Medicines for opioid use disorder, ACT, contingency management where states cover it | Each state chooses; eligibility depends on coverage | Treatment itself, for tenants who choose it |
| SAMHSA block grants and homeless-specific grants | Treatment and services for people without coverage; outreach | Discretionary, flat, and steered away from harm reduction by the 2025 order [EV-HOU-107] | Gap filler, uninsured people, start-up |
| VA | Case management and treatment for veterans | Veterans only | Already in place through HUD-VASH |

### Workforce

HRSA projects shortages, under current trends, of 77,050 addiction counselors, 36,780 adult psychiatrists and 99,780 mental health counselors [EV-HOU-338]. ACT and intensive case management need small caseloads, so every new unit with high-need tenants needs staff. In this dossier's judgment the binding limits are wages in homeless services, which are low (not yet in the register), and the supply of licensed clinicians. Peer specialists (people with lived experience) and case managers without clinical licenses can carry most contact work, with clinicians on teams. Contingency management needs little clinical time and can be run by trained non-clinicians (judgment, from how the VA and California run it; not in the register).

### Interactions

- **Care flagship (HLT-4, HCBS; HLT-1, restoring Medicaid).** Tenancy-sustaining services fit the HCBS framework that HLT-4 expands; a federal HCBS benefit that names tenancy supports would spare states the waiver route that budget neutrality now narrows [EV-HOU-337]. HLT-1's repeal of the 2025 Medicaid cuts would protect tenants' coverage from work requirements [EV-HOU-336]. The care dossier should own the benefit design; this dossier should state what supportive housing needs from it.
- **Health coverage work.** The health coverage model should count the people in supportive housing as a population with high Medicaid use, so services paid through Medicaid show up in the coverage cost, not only in housing.
- **Housing guarantee (memos 2 and 8).** Rent for supportive housing tenants can come from the guarantee's awards for people without a home; services cannot.
- **Recovery housing (memo 5, option B).** The option should follow SAMHSA's guidance that recovery homes not bar prescribed medicines [EV-HOU-312], or it will exclude people on methadone or buprenorphine, the most effective treatment [EV-HOU-301].

## 5. Recommendations for section 4

These are recommendations for the specification only. Espen decides.

1. **Write the engagement standard as a duty on providers, not a condition on tenants.** Every supportive housing tenant is offered a named service navigator or case manager, with an expected contact at least monthly, more often at first. If a tenant doesn't engage, the provider must keep trying through a set protocol (visits, calls, letters, a peer, a change of worker). Non-engagement can never end the lease, the subsidy or the tenant's place in the program. Tenancy is governed by the same lease rules as any tenant. This is Housing First as first designed, with weekly or twice-monthly contact (Pathways and At Home/Chez Soi; Tsemberis and others, 2004; Goering and others, 2014) [EV-HOU-322] [EV-HOU-321], and it follows Finland's practice of escalating contact before any eviction step (Y-Foundation, 2022) [EV-HOU-327].
2. **Close the gap in today's rules.** Amend 24 CFR 578.75(h), and the HUD-VASH requirement, so that not taking part in case management cannot end housing assistance (HUD regulation, 2012; HUD-VASH notice, 2024) [EV-HOU-332] [EV-HOU-325]. For HUD-VASH, a veteran who stops engaging should move to a regular voucher automatically, not at the agency's option.
3. **Never make treatment a condition; make treatment easy.** Revoke the 2025 order's treatment-condition directive [EV-HOU-107]. Require supportive housing providers to offer or connect tenants to medicines for opioid use disorder, naloxone, contingency management for stimulants, and ACT or intensive case management for people with serious mental illness, on site or by visit (Sordo and others, 2017; Bolívar and others, 2021; Coldwell and Bender, 2007) [EV-HOU-300] [EV-HOU-304] [EV-HOU-308].
4. **Pay for services through Medicaid, with a federal backstop.** Ask the care flagship to include tenancy-sustaining services, housing navigation and ACT in its HCBS expansion (HLT-4), so states do not need waivers that must now be budget neutral (Public Law 119-21) [EV-HOU-337]. Fund a HUD services grant for tenants without Medicaid. Pay providers on contracts with staffing and contact standards, not open fee-for-service billing, given Minnesota's fraud experience (CMS, 2025) [EV-HOU-329].
5. **Fund the workforce.** Tie service funding to wage floors and caseload limits, and fund peer-specialist training (HRSA, 2025) [EV-HOU-338]. Section 5 should size it.
6. **Scale where the evidence is strongest first.** Start with chronically homeless people who use the most public services, where offsets are largest (Urban Institute, 2021; Mental Health Commission of Canada, 2014) [EV-HOU-318] [EV-HOU-320], and count offsets only for them in section 6.
7. **Keep recovery housing a choice that allows medicines.** Option B's recovery homes should accept residents on methadone or buprenorphine (SAMHSA, 2023) [EV-HOU-312] and be evaluated, since the evidence is moderate (Reif and others, 2014) [EV-HOU-311].
8. **Measure what matters for this model.** Report contact rates, housing retention, overdose deaths, medicine starts and exits by reason, for each program, so the plan learns whether the contact expectation helps or hinders, the question no trial has answered [EV-HOU-330] [EV-HOU-331].
9. **Restore the Continuum of Care's permanent housing priority in 2029.** Reverse the treatment and services-participation scoring and the transitional-housing set-aside (CRS, 2026) [EV-HOU-334], and give the national plan due in July 2027 a successor built on this standard [EV-HOU-238].
