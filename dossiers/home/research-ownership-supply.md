# Research note: homeownership finance and broad housing supply

Role: research for the section 3 addendum, part 1 (board step C-4h). Espen wrote on November 9, 2026 that the plan has "focused too exclusively on low-income households and the actively homeless or those at risk of homelessness, and neglected more robust policy treatments for housing production more broadly ... and the ability to buy a home for more middle-class households, especially younger ones". This note weighs the tools for helping people buy a first home, how far buyer help raises prices, and the supply levers beyond the zoning reward (HOU-6.01) and the construction money (HOU-3C). The forks it leaves go to `memos/9-homeownership.md` and `memos/10-broad-supply.md`. Status: draft (stage C). It edits no part of sections 4-9; the fold-in waits on Espen's decisions. Part 2 of the addendum (repair and renovation; aging in place) is for a later session; overlaps are noted, not weighed.

Evidence is EV-HOU-500 to 599 and 700 to 702 in `evidence.json`, plus earlier entries where cited. Sources are SRC-1800 to 1899, 2000 and 2001 in `library/catalog.csv`. Many licensed sources were read through search tools, because their hosts block downloads (TIGTA, GAO, JCT, CBO, HUD User); their catalog rows say so, and the reviewer should check those quotations against the originals. Figures labeled "judgment" or "this note's arithmetic" are the project's estimates (grade D), not evidence. The plan starts in January 2029 (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`), so every option below is dated from 2029 on.

## In brief

1. **The gap is real, and it is about age, race and income.** In the third quarter of 2026, 36.6% of householders under 35 owned their home, against 79.1% of those 65 and over; 44.4% of Black householders owned, against 75.1% of non-Hispanic White householders; and 52.6% of households below the median income, against 78.3% above it (Census) [EV-HOU-563]. A median-income household needs 43.9% of its income to own the median home (Atlanta Fed) [EV-HOU-564]; only 32% of households, and 16% of renters, earn the $120,800 the median home needs (Harvard Joint Center) [EV-HOU-565].
2. **Most buyer help goes to people who would have bought anyway.** The 2009-10 first-time buyer credit induced about 400,000 purchases, at roughly $28,000 to $53,000 of federal money per added buyer; 65% to 90% of the money went to buyers who would have bought anyway [EV-HOU-509] [EV-HOU-510]. England's Help to Buy drew in about 37% of its buyers [EV-HOU-544]. The mortgage interest deduction costs about $28,397 a year per added owner and raises ownership only where building is easy [EV-HOU-514].
3. **Where supply is tight, buyer help raises prices.** A flat Bavarian purchase grant was fully capitalized [EV-HOU-546]; Help to Buy raised prices without added building in Greater London but added building with no price effect near the Welsh border [EV-HOU-545]. The 2009-10 credit raised prices only about 1.1% per standard deviation of exposure, in a market with a glut of empty homes [EV-HOU-508]. A targeted cut in FHA's insurance premium showed no price effect within a year [EV-HOU-550].
4. **The best-evidenced, most targeted lever is the cost of an FHA loan.** FHA's 2015 premium cut of 50 basis points raised purchases by FHA-reliant borrowers by nearly 14%, mostly lower-income ones (Bhutta and Ringo) [EV-HOU-550]. 83% of FHA purchase loans go to first-time buyers, 20% to Hispanic and 13% to Black borrowers [EV-HOU-552]. The insurance fund holds 11.47% capital against a 2% legal floor [EV-HOU-552].
5. **Down payment help works best as part of a delivery package.** State housing finance agencies already give it to about three-quarters of their borrowers [EV-HOU-536]. Their borrowers default about 30% less than similar borrowers [EV-HOU-537]; studies of help alone conflict [EV-HOU-527] [EV-HOU-538]. A first-generation test, by self-attestation, aims help at Black and Latino families [EV-HOU-526] [EV-HOU-539]: California's first-generation round reached more than twice the share of Black buyers its open round did [EV-HOU-540] [EV-HOU-541].
6. **Building homes to buy needs credit for small builders.** Loans for one-to-four-family construction stand 56% below their 2008 peak [EV-HOU-568], banks are still tightening [EV-HOU-567], and the median new home is 2,142 square feet [EV-HOU-569]. The plan's federal loans (HOU-3C.02) are rental-only as written.
7. **Supply levers beyond the plan cost little federal money but rest on thin evidence.** Manufactured homes cost about half as much per square foot as site-built ones [EV-HOU-575], but local zoning can still exclude them [EV-HOU-578] and most are financed as costly personal-property loans [EV-HOU-576]; the ROAD to Housing Act fixed the chassis rule, not these. Single-stair buildings cost 6-13% less [EV-HOU-580]. Federal land could add 0.1 to 1 million homes, almost all in the interior West; estimates conflict [EV-HOU-583] [EV-HOU-584]. Parking, lot-size and condo-liability reforms show effects on what gets built, not yet on how many homes [EV-HOU-592] [EV-HOU-594] [EV-HOU-597].
8. **What this points to** (memos 9 and 10). Pair modest, targeted buyer help with supply: a time-limited down payment program through state agencies with a first-generation priority and self-attestation; a cut in FHA's annual premium; and letting the plan's federal loans finance starter homes and condos for sale. Widen the zoning reward's list of reforms instead of adding mandates; sell federal land near growing western cities; write a categorical exclusion for assisted infill housing into law. Skip a broad buyer tax credit: per lasting added owner it costs about the same as the targeted tools, but about five times as much in all, mostly paid to buyers who would have bought anyway, and it capitalizes most where homes are scarce.

### 2.1 First-time buyer tax credits

**The 2008-10 credits.** The 2008 credit (HERA) was refundable, worth 10% of price up to $7,500 and repaid over 15 years, an interest-free loan; the 2009 stimulus law raised it to $8,000 and dropped repayment; the late-2009 extension added a $6,500 credit for some repeat buyers, raised income limits to $125,000 single and $225,000 joint, and barred homes over $800,000 [EV-HOU-500]. JCT estimated $4.9 billion, $6.5 billion and $10.8 billion over ten years for the three versions [EV-HOU-501]. Claims reached almost $30.4 billion by July 2011 [EV-HOU-504].

**What it bought.**
- Berger, Turner and Zwick find home sales rose by about 490,000 (9.8%), and induced buyers were three years younger than typical first-time buyers (claimants' median age 32) [EV-HOU-508]. Their working paper puts induced sales at 24-35% of claims, about $28,000 of fiscal cost per induced sale [EV-HOU-509].
- Hembre counts 399,846 induced first-time buyers: $52,770 of spending per new owner, or $27,010 counting only the deadweight loss [EV-HOU-510]. It worked better where homes were cheaper [EV-HOU-511].
- Against: Dynan, Gayer and Plotkin find at best small, mostly temporary effects [EV-HOU-512]; Brogaard and Roshak find no rise in sales and a price gain that reversed [EV-HOU-513]. CRS's estimates before the fact were far lower than the later causal estimates [EV-HOU-502]. Both views stand. The causal studies agree it raised purchases; they disagree on how much was only earlier buying.

**What went wrong in running it.** TIGTA found $139 million claimed for homes not yet bought, more than $513 million of likely erroneous refunds, and $9.1 million paid to prisoners [EV-HOU-504] [EV-HOU-505] [EV-HOU-506]. Recapture was hard to run: 62% of one batch of change-of-residence notices went out because a vendor flagged the wrong address [EV-HOU-507]. The lesson for the design lens: a credit claimed on a tax return, after closing, is open to error at the front end and costly to claw back at the back end.

**Recent designs.**
- *Biden FY2025 budget:* refundable, 10% of price up to $10,000, paid over two years, phased out between $100,000 and $200,000 of income, with full recapture within three years [EV-HOU-521]. HUD's "3.5 million families" counts every claimant, not added buyers [EV-HOU-522].
- *H.R. 2863 (2021, Rep. Blumenauer):* refundable, 10% up to $15,000, income cap of 160% of area median income, a phase-down above 110% of the area median price, 25% a year recapture over four years; not advanceable [EV-HOU-523].
- *H.R. 4717 (2025):* the same credit, phased out from 150% of area median income, limited to federally backed mortgages, and **advanceable**: the buyer can transfer the credit to the lender at closing, with Treasury advancing payments to lenders [EV-HOU-524]. Advancing it fixes the cash-at-closing problem and puts the check on eligibility with the lender.
- The second Trump administration's ideas (50-year, portable or assumable mortgages, retirement savings for down payments) exist only in press reports; FHFA's director said in January 2026 that 50-year mortgages had given way to "other priorities" [EV-HOU-561]. None is current law or a bill this note weighs.

### 2.2 Down payment help

**How it runs now.** State housing finance agencies financed almost 148,800 first mortgages in 2025, and nearly 77% of their borrowers got down payment help [EV-HOU-536]. In 2022 help averaged $10,360, as grants, amortizing seconds or deferred and forgivable seconds, with combined loan-to-value up to 105% [EV-HOU-535]. 42.31% of FHA purchase loans in fiscal 2025 used help, 17.74% from government sources [EV-HOU-528]. HOME has funded 419,263 homebuyer units, at about $11,000 each over its life [EV-HOU-531], and the ROAD to Housing Act lets HOME's homebuyer help reach families up to 100% of area median income [EV-HOU-226]. The American Dream Downpayment Initiative (FY2003-08) was never evaluated; GAO said its accomplishments were "not known" [EV-HOU-530].

**The Downpayment Toward Equity Act (H.R. 4069, 2025).** $100 billion authorized, 75% to states by formula and 25% competitively; up to the greater of $20,000 or 10% of price; income up to 120% of area median (140% in high-cost areas); prorated repayment if the buyer leaves within five years [EV-HOU-525]. The first-generation test is a self-attestation that the buyer's parents do not own (or did not at death), or that the buyer was in foster care; the bill bars any further documentation and shields lenders who rely on it in good faith [EV-HOU-526]. That is the design lens's "one plain test, self-attestation" already written into a bill. California went the other way: attestation under penalty of perjury, random audits, and now supplemental documents [EV-HOU-542].

**Who first-generation targeting reaches.** Urban Institute researchers count about 5.37 million potentially eligible households aged 25-54 at 40-120% of area median income: 32% Black, 27% Latino, 31% White [EV-HOU-539]. California's open first round was 3% Black and 35% White [EV-HOU-540]; its first-generation round was 7% Black and 40% Hispanic [EV-HOU-541]. Black buyers rely more on government and second-mortgage help than on family gifts [EV-HOU-538].

**Does help raise default?** The evidence conflicts.
- Worse: FHA's own unadjusted data show serious delinquency more than 25% higher on loans with help [EV-HOU-529]; GAO, with controls, found 49% more claims on non-seller help (2000-02 loans) [EV-HOU-527].
- No worse or better: with race and credit controls, help was not significantly tied to default [EV-HOU-538]; state agency borrowers, who usually get help plus counseling and direct servicing, defaulted about 30% less than similar borrowers (peer-reviewed) [EV-HOU-537]; HOME and ADDI buyers foreclosed 1.2 points less than FHA borrowers generally [EV-HOU-534].
- *Reading.* The package matters more than the cash: counseling, sound first loans and the agency's own servicing. That argues for running new help through the state agencies, not through tax returns.

**Who would have bought anyway.** No U.S. causal estimate exists. England's Help to Buy put "demand additionality" at a central 37% [EV-HOU-544]; in a Tulsa randomized trial, the ownership gain faded as the control group caught up, so the help mainly moved purchases earlier [EV-HOU-547]. In this note's judgment, a first-generation, income-capped program reaches more buyers held back by a lack of cash than a broad credit does, so its induced share sits toward the top of the 12-37% range found for broad programs, perhaps 25-37%; that is a hopeful reading, since no U.S. study measures it. The Tulsa result means about half of those purchases may only come earlier, as for every tool here. On that basis each lasting added owner costs about five to eight times the grant (this note's arithmetic, section 5).

### 2.3 Cheaper mortgages: the federal levers

- **FHA's annual premium.** The 2015 cut of 50 basis points, from 135 to 85 (about $1,000 less in the first year on a $200,000 loan), raised purchases by FHA-reliant borrowers by nearly 14%; the response fell with income, and the authors found no price effect within 12 months [EV-HOU-550]. The 2023 cut, from 85 to 55 basis points, was projected by HUD (Biden administration) to save 850,000 borrowers about $800 a year [EV-HOU-551]; it has no independent evaluation yet. The fund's capital ratio was 11.47% in fiscal 2025, against a 2% floor, and 4.42% under a severe stress test [EV-HOU-552], so there is room for a further cut without breaching the floor (judgment; the stress figure is the binding check).
- **The GSEs' guarantee fees.** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac charged 65.2 basis points on average in 2024, roughly at cost including capital [EV-HOU-553]. Ten basis points go to the Treasury under the 2011 payroll-tax law, extended to 2032 [EV-HOU-554]; CBO scores two more years of it at $6.7 billion [EV-HOU-556]. In 2022 FHFA dropped upfront fees for first-time buyers at or below 100% of area median income [EV-HOU-555]. CBO estimated that a 10-basis-point fee rise cuts new GSE guarantees about 10% [EV-HOU-557], so a cut would mostly move loans between FHA, the GSEs and private lenders; the register has no study of how much of a fee cut reaches the borrower's rate.
- **Bond purchases.** The Fed's 2008 announcement cut mortgage rates about 85 basis points before it bought anything [EV-HOU-558]; Treasury's 2008-09 purchases of $225 billion returned $250 billion [EV-HOU-559]. Those were crisis effects. In January 2026 the second Trump administration directed the GSEs to buy up to $200 billion; one broker-dealer analyst attributes only 5-10 basis points of the rate drop to it [EV-HOU-560]. In this note's judgment, bond purchases are a crisis tool, not a standing first-buyer program.
- **Rate buydowns.** AEI (a think tank with a market-oriented stance) estimates a 100-basis-point permanent buydown costs a builder about 3.2% of the price, against a 10% price cut for the same payment, and argues buydowns let the largest builders avoid 10-12% price cuts [EV-HOU-570]. That is one source's contested reading; it is the clearest claim in the register that a rate subsidy holds prices up.
- **Direct federal mortgage lending already exists, for rural buyers.** USDA's Section 502 Direct program lends straight to very low- and low-income buyers in eligible rural areas who cannot get credit elsewhere. Payment subsidies can cut the effective rate to as low as 1%, no down payment is generally required, and terms run to 33 years, or 38 for borrowers below 60% of area median income (Congressional Research Service, 2022) [EV-HOU-599].
  - *Scale.* It peaked at nearly 133,000 loans in FY1976; by FY2021 it made about 5,000 a year, against about 127,000 USDA-guaranteed loans, with $55.4 million of appropriations supporting up to $1 billion of lending [EV-HOU-599]. In FY2026 it lent $571 million to 2,115 families, at an average of $269,000 (USDA, second Trump administration) [EV-HOU-700]. Its borrowers had an average income of $35,220 in FY2019, against $63,240 for guaranteed borrowers [EV-HOU-599].
  - *Performance.* Nearly a fifth of the 159,208 active direct loans were delinquent in March 2025, double what an internal 1993 report called acceptable, and USDA left some Maine loans in default for nearly nine years before foreclosing (ProPublica and the Bangor Daily News, from internal USDA data) [EV-HOU-701]. USDA reports first-year delinquency falling from 3.82% to 2.89% in 2026 [EV-HOU-700]; that is a narrower measure.
  - *Evaluation.* No causal evaluation of the direct program was found. One peer-reviewed study finds states with more Section 502 loans, direct and guaranteed together, have higher homeownership, especially for Black and Hispanic households, but it is a correlation across states and does not isolate the direct program (Petach, 2024) [EV-HOU-702]; it is one source's view.
  - *Other direct channels.* In rental housing, the Federal Financing Bank's multifamily risk-share program has committed $7.03 billion, and a lenders' group says the FY2027 budget stopped new commitments [EV-HOU-572].
  - *Reading.* Two different options follow. **Expanding or adapting Section 502 Direct** builds on a working channel for very low- and low-income rural buyers: more lending authority, servicing that acts on defaults sooner, and perhaps a wider area or income test. Its limits are its rural and income rules, its small scale and its weak delinquency record [EV-HOU-701]. **A new national public mortgage lender for middle-income buyers** has no U.S. model: it would compete with FHA and the two companies, which already set most terms, and nothing in the register shows what it would add. This note finds evidence to weigh the first, and none for the second.

### 2.4 Deferred, forgivable and shared-appreciation second loans; shared equity

- **Deferred and forgivable seconds** are the state agencies' standard form [EV-HOU-535]. A deferred, zero-interest second repaid at sale returns money to a revolving fund; a forgivable one is a grant over time.
- **Shared-appreciation loans.** California's Dream For All lends up to 20% of price with no payments, repaid with a matching share of appreciation, capped at 2.5 times the loan [EV-HOU-540] [EV-HOU-541]. Its first $300 million went in 11 days to 2,182 buyers, average loan $113,000 [EV-HOU-540]. Its first-generation round drew more than 18,000 eligible applicants for about 2,000 awards [EV-HOU-542]. CalHFA itself warns that continued high help "risks perpetuating market distortions" and proposes shifting some money to a supply pilot [EV-HOU-543]. At about $115,000 per buyer, a federal version would reach few people unless its loans are much smaller.
- **Shared-equity homes** (community land trusts, deed-restricted homes) keep the subsidy in the home: the price is held below market at each resale. Urban Institute's evaluation of seven programs found very few foreclosures and over 90% still owning after five years [EV-HOU-548]; a land-trust survey found 1.30% of loans seriously delinquent in 2010, against 8.57% in the market [EV-HOU-549]. Both studies are advocate-funded, without comparison groups. This is the one ownership form that answers the price question directly, because no subsidy leaks into the next sale.

### 2.5 Building homes to buy: starter homes, condos and builder credit

- **Credit for small builders.** One-to-four-family construction and land loans were $91.1 billion at the end of 2025, 56% below the 2008 peak, with banks under $1 billion in assets holding 21.7% (FDIC data via the home builders' association) [EV-HOU-568]. Banks tightened standards again in the third quarter of 2026 [EV-HOU-567]. A higher capital rule adds about 40 basis points to some construction loans, though it exempts one-to-four-family homes [EV-HOU-574].
- **Size and lots.** The median new home completed in 2025 was 2,142 square feet; 41% of homes built for sale sat on lots under 7,000 square feet [EV-HOU-569]. Lot-size rules add more than $400,000 to a quarter-acre lot in San Francisco and under $25,000 in a dozen interior metros [EV-HOU-594]. Starter homes are a zoning question on the coasts and a credit and cost question inland (judgment).
- **The plan's own tools.** HOU-3C.02's $300 billion of federal loans finance rentals: mixed-income, social and public housing, nonprofit and tax-credit projects (`4-specification.md`). The neighborhood homes credit (HOU-2D.03) is the only plan tool for homes to buy, about 150,000 homes in struggling neighborhoods (`4-specification.md`, HOU-3C.04). Opening part of HOU-3C.02 to short construction loans and construction-to-permanent loans for starter homes and condos, sold to owner-occupants under a price cap, would use the same channel for ownership. The register has no analysis of federal construction lending to small builders; the case rests on the credit data above and judgment.
- **FHA products already exist.** The Limited 203(k) loan now finances up to $75,000 of repairs [EV-HOU-573] (a part 2 overlap), and FHA's 2019 condo rule allows single-unit approvals, which HUD expected to add 20,000-60,000 loans [EV-HOU-571].
- **Condos.** In Denver, condos were 4% of for-sale building in 2008-20; Colorado's legislative staff put defect-related costs at about $15,000 per condo [EV-HOU-597]. Colorado's 2025 law offers builders narrower defect claims in exchange for a no-cost warranty and third-party inspection [EV-HOU-598]. No causal study separates liability from the 2008 crash and condo lending rules.

## 3. The price question, weighed honestly

**What the evidence shows.**
- *Broad, large help in tight markets is capitalized.* Bavaria's flat grant raised prices about €10,000, consistent with full capitalization [EV-HOU-546]. Help to Buy raised prices with no detectable added building in Greater London [EV-HOU-545]. The mortgage interest deduction raised prices and home sizes in Denmark without raising ownership [EV-HOU-515], lowers ownership where supply is restricted [EV-HOU-514], and in a structural model its repeal lowers prices and raises ownership [EV-HOU-516]. Seller-funded down payment help raised sale prices 2-3% [EV-HOU-527].
- *Where building responds, help adds homes instead.* Near the Welsh border, Help to Buy raised construction without a price effect [EV-HOU-545].
- *Targeted, modest help showed small or no price effects.* The 2009-10 credit raised prices about 1.1% per standard deviation of exposure, in a market with many empty homes [EV-HOU-508], and Hembre found no role for supply elasticity in its effect [EV-HOU-511]; the FHA premium cut showed no price effect within 12 months [EV-HOU-550]. Both studies are from slack markets or short windows, so they say less about tight markets in 2030 (judgment).
- *Disagreements.* Brogaard and Roshak find the 2009-10 price gain reversed [EV-HOU-513]. On the 2017 tax law's trim to the deduction, one study finds price effects through the state and local tax cap [EV-HOU-519] and another finds the net effect near zero [EV-HOU-520]. AEI says buydowns hold up new-home prices [EV-HOU-570]; that is contested. Both views stand on each.

**How designs limit capitalization.** In this note's reading, the evidence supports five design rules:
1. **Target cash-constrained buyers.** Help that changes who can buy (first-generation, income-capped, first-time) is less of a windfall than help to everyone, and the FHA evidence shows the response is concentrated among lower-income buyers [EV-HOU-550].
2. **Keep it modest and time-limited.** A flat, broad, large grant is what capitalized fully in Bavaria [EV-HOU-546]. A ten-year program with a published review can be ended if prices respond (judgment).
3. **Pair it with supply.** Help works where homes can be built [EV-HOU-545]. Two pairings follow: give the plan's own construction loans to starter homes and condos, and make buyer help count double where it buys a newly built home or a home in a place that earns the zoning reward (judgment; no study tests the pairing).
4. **Keep the subsidy in the home where possible.** Shared-equity homes hold the price down at each resale [EV-HOU-548]. Shared-appreciation loans return part of the gain to the public fund [EV-HOU-540].
5. **Prefer lowering costs to giving cash.** Cutting a fee the government sets (FHA's premium) reaches the same buyers without a lump sum at the closing table, and its best study found no price effect [EV-HOU-550]. A price cap on eligible homes is a weaker tool: it piles demand at the cap (judgment).

**What this note does not claim.** No study tests capitalization of U.S. down payment help directly (evidence still needed). The rules above follow from evidence on related subsidies abroad and on the 2009-10 credit; each is a reasoned design choice, not a measured result.

## 4. Broad supply levers beyond the zoning reward and the construction money

### What the ROAD to Housing Act already does

| Small-dollar mortgages | FHA pilot and a CFPB study [EV-HOU-221] | Pilot only |

### The levers

- **Manufactured and factory-built homes.** A new manufactured home averaged about $123,000 in 2024, $84.45 a square foot against $168.86 for a site-built house's structure alone; shipments were about 103,000 [EV-HOU-575]. 42% of manufactured-home purchase loans are chattel loans, at a median 8.6% against 4.9% for manufactured-home mortgages; only 27% of applications are financed, against 74% for site-built homes; and 17% of borrowers who own their land still take chattel loans [EV-HOU-576]. These homes are 13% of rural and small-town homes, and their owners are older and lower-income [EV-HOU-577]. The HUD code preempts building codes but not zoning; the chance a HUD-code home was placed fell from 77.5% to 53.9% where zoning was a significant barrier [EV-HOU-578]. Modular building cuts timelines 10-30%, but cost savings are inconsistent [EV-HOU-579].
- **Building codes.** Single-stair buildings of four to six stories cost 6-13% less, and all but three large U.S. cities require two stairs at that height (Pew and the Center for Building in North America, both reform advocates) [EV-HOU-580]. A U.S. elevator costs about $150,000, against about $50,000 in peer countries [EV-HOU-581]. No peer-reviewed cost study was found.
- **Federal and surplus land.** Estimates conflict sharply: AEI says 135-180 square miles of the best BLM land could yield about 1 million homes in ten years and about $15 billion of land receipts [EV-HOU-583]; Smart Growth America finds only about 118,000 homes in the commuting zones of the 50 largest metros, with 15 of them having no such land [EV-HOU-584]. Assumed density explains most of the gap. In September 2026 BLM sold 940 acres to Las Vegas for $94 million, for up to 6,000 homes [EV-HOU-585]: land sales earn money. Surplus federal buildings matter little in volume; the homeless-provider first claim (McKinney-Vento Title V) transferred 122 properties in 27 years [EV-HOU-586].
- **Infrastructure grants tied to housing.** HUD's second PRO Housing round gave $100 million to 18 winners, with more than $10 requested per $1 available, and reports planned uses, not homes [EV-HOU-587]. Urban Institute finds some of the most exclusionary towns get almost no federal pass-through money to condition [EV-HOU-588]. No evaluation shows that grant conditions add homes.
- **Permitting and environmental review.** Federal environmental review reaches only federally assisted housing. The only cost figure in the register is a 2007 HUD-sponsored study: about $5,000-15,000 per lot, 4.7% of a finished lot's price, "not a major factor" [EV-HOU-589]. State review matters more for most housing: California's 2025 law exempts zoning-consistent urban infill from its environmental review [EV-HOU-590], and its earlier fast-track law approved 156 projects with over 18,000 units in four years, saving about a year [EV-HOU-591].
- **Lot size and parking.** After Buffalo ended parking minimums, mixed-use projects built 53% fewer spaces, though the overall cut was not significant [EV-HOU-592]. Bundled garage parking costs renters about $1,700 a year, 17% of rent [EV-HOU-593]. States now act directly: Washington caps parking minimums [EV-HOU-595] and Texas caps minimum lot sizes on new tracts in large cities at 3,000 square feet [EV-HOU-596]. These measure what gets built, not added homes; the general zoning evidence stays as in fork 4 (0.8% more housing in three to nine years [EV-HOU-177]).
- **Condo defect liability.** See 2.5 [EV-HOU-597] [EV-HOU-598].

**Reading.** Most of these levers cost the federal government little or nothing, and some earn money (land sales). Their evidence is mostly capacity estimates and case data. The plan already has the vehicle that suits them under the design lens: HOU-6.01 pays for homes finished, so adding these reforms to what it rewards adds no new rule for anyone, only a wider set of ways to earn the award.

Because much of every tool's effect is earlier buying [EV-HOU-512] [EV-HOU-547], this note assumes about half of induced purchases last, for every tool alike (judgment). **Reading the buyer rows.** On one basis, the three buyer tools cost about the same per lasting added owner: roughly $65,000 to $230,000, with wide, overlapping ranges. They differ in total cost (the broad credit is about five to ten times the others), in who they reach, and in how much leaks into prices. Those differences, not cost per added owner, decide among them.

**Who each reaches, in brief.** The broad credit and GSE fee cuts reach the most middle-income buyers, including the third and fourth income fifths the plan's D3 test watches (`3-design.md`, fork 3, D3). The FHA premium cut reaches lower-income, younger, Hispanic and Black buyers most [EV-HOU-550] [EV-HOU-552]. First-generation help reaches Black and Latino renters most [EV-HOU-539]. Plan loans for homes to buy reach buyers wherever starter homes get built, which on the evidence is mostly the interior and the South (judgment, from [EV-HOU-594]). Federal land is almost all in the interior West [EV-HOU-583] [EV-HOU-584].

**Fiscal class (D4).** A tax credit or a permanent premium cut is a permanent claim, paid for from FY2040. A down payment program written for 2030-39 is time-limited and can borrow inside the D4-B envelope, like HOU-3C's grants, which matters while section 6 shows FY2040 about $20 billion short (`6-cost.md`). That argues for a time-limited program with a review, not a permanent entitlement (judgment).
