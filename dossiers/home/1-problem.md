# A home you can keep: the problem

Role: section 1 of the housing dossier: what is wrong, for whom and how large. Status: draft (stage C, step 1; 2025 rent burden added from the ACS). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`). The claims still missing evidence are listed at the end and are not made here.

Outcomes this section frames:
- OUT-04, a home people can keep;
- OUT-02, planning beyond the next paycheck;
- OUT-01, child poverty.

## What is wrong

**Rent takes too much of too many incomes.**
- In 2025, 21,551,355 renter households spent more than 30% of their income on housing costs. That is 49.6% of the 43,437,030 renter households for whom the burden can be measured [EV-HOU-038]. In 2023 it was 49.7% of 42.5 million [EV-HOU-001], so the share has barely moved.
- 11,084,148 renter households, 25.5% of those measured, spent more than half their income [EV-HOU-039].
- Counted from the 30.0% bin of another Census table, the figures run higher: 22,486,693 households (51.8%) at 30.0% or more and 11,401,379 (26.2%) at 50.0% or more [EV-HOU-040]. That table counts about 0.9 million more households than the one above in 2023 as well, so this section uses the first and does not mix the two.
- HUD calls a household cost-burdened above 30% and severely cost-burdened above 50% [EV-HOU-002].

**The squeeze tightened.**
- Real median rent rose 3.8% in 2023, faster than home values, the largest real rise since at least 2011 [EV-HOU-006].
- Median gross rent rose again in 2024, from $1,448 to $1,487 in 2024 dollars (gross rent includes utilities) [EV-HOU-005]. In 2025 it was $1,557 [EV-HOU-042], in the survey year's dollars, so it is not set against the 2024 figure here.
- The median renter still spends about 31% of income on rent: 31% in 2024 [EV-HOU-005] and 30.9% in 2025 [EV-HOU-041]. But the median hides the people the project cares most about. From 2019 to 2021 the share of income going to rent rose, and the lowest-income renters saw the largest increases [EV-HOU-007].

**More renters are falling behind.**
- In 2025, twenty-three percent of renters were behind on rent at some point in the past year. That is up 2 points from 2024 and 6 points since 2021 [EV-HOU-008].
- The median reported rent held at $1,200 [EV-HOU-010]. These national figures do not show what caused the rise in arrears; rents for the renters falling behind may have risen even while the median held.

## For whom

**Lower-income renters.**
- Nearly a third of renters with incomes under $50,000 were behind on rent at some point in the past year.
- Among renters earning at least $100,000, the figure was 5 percent [EV-HOU-009].

**By race and ethnicity.**
- In 2025, 4,633,829 Black renter households (56.7%) were cost-burdened, above the national 49.6% [EV-HOU-038] [EV-HOU-043]. 2,518,375 (30.8%) were severely cost-burdened, spending more than half their income on housing, against 25.5% of all renter households [EV-HOU-039] [EV-HOU-044]. In 2023 the figures were 4.6 million (56.2%) and about 2.5 million (30.6%) [EV-HOU-003] [EV-HOU-004].
- Worst case needs fall hardest on Black and Hispanic households overall: 10.1 percent of all Black households and 11.5 percent of all Hispanic households had them in 2023, against 7.5 percent of Asian and 4.3 percent of White households [EV-HOU-036]. Households headed by non-White people accounted for 56.5 percent of all worst case needs [EV-HOU-037].
- Measured only among very low-income renters, the order changes: 55.1 percent of Asian, 48.0 percent of Hispanic, 45.0 percent of White and 38.1 percent of Black households had worst case needs [EV-HOU-034]. The two measures answer different questions: how common severe need is in each group, and how often a very low-income renter in that group is left with no assistance and a severe housing problem, meaning rent above half of income or severely inadequate housing. Worst case needs require both [EV-HOU-020].
- 39.4 percent of Black very low-income renters reported housing assistance, against 24.8 percent of White, 22.1 percent of Hispanic and 20.6 percent of Asian ones [EV-HOU-034]. An assisted household cannot have worst case needs by definition [EV-HOU-020], and HUD attributes the lower rate for Black renters to this greater access to assistance [EV-HOU-035]. The lower rate among very low-income renters sits alongside a rate among all Black households more than twice the White one [EV-HOU-036].

**Families with children.**
- 2.33 million families with children had worst case needs in 2023, 42.9 percent of very low-income renter families with children [EV-HOU-030] [EV-HOU-031].
- Nearly 150,000 children were homeless on a single night in 2024, 33 percent (32,618) more than a year earlier [EV-HOU-026].
- People in families with children had the largest one-year rise in homelessness of any group, 39 percent [EV-HOU-026].

**Older adults.** 2.46 million older adult households without children had worst case needs in 2023, 38.8 percent of very low-income older renters [EV-HOU-030] [EV-HOU-031].

**People with disabilities.** About one in seven households with worst case needs, 1.16 million, included someone younger than 62 with a disability. 37.0 percent of very low-income renter households with such a member had worst case needs [EV-HOU-032].

**Single adults and other households.** The largest group with worst case needs was other nonfamily households, mostly single individuals: 2.80 million, 49.2 percent of very low-income ones [EV-HOU-030] [EV-HOU-031]. Other family households, such as relatives living together without children, had the highest rate, 52.2 percent [EV-HOU-031].

**By region.** 3.17 million households with worst case needs lived in the South, 2.29 million in the West, 1.53 million in the Midwest and 1.48 million in the Northeast [EV-HOU-033]. Assistance reached 24.4 percent of very low-income renters in the West and 38.0 percent in the Northeast [EV-HOU-022].

Still missing: rent burden among children's households, rural and tribal areas, and people leaving homelessness or institutions. They are on the evidence list below.

## How large

**8.46 million households have the most severe needs.** HUD counts a household as having *worst case needs* when it has a very low income, gets no housing assistance, and pays more than half its income for rent, lives in severely inadequate housing, or both.
- In 2023, 8.46 million renter households had worst case needs. That is 44.2 percent of the 19.14 million very low-income renter households at risk [EV-HOU-020].
- The count is barely below the record 8.53 million of 2021, which topped the 8.48 million of the 2011 recession [EV-HOU-021].
- Beyond them, over 21 million renter households are cost-burdened [EV-HOU-001], and almost a quarter of renters fall behind on rent in a year [EV-HOU-008].

**Most eligible households get no help.** Federal funding for rental assistance does not meet demand [EV-HOU-012].
- In 2023, only 28 percent of very low-income renter households received housing assistance. The share ranged from 24.4 percent in the West to 38.0 percent in the Northeast [EV-HOU-022].
- 13.77 million very low-income renter households went unassisted, and 61.4 percent of them had worst case needs [EV-HOU-023].
- The Congressional Research Service's rougher estimate agrees: about one in four eligible households is helped [EV-HOU-011]. For Housing Choice Vouchers, the largest program, with more than 2.3 million households [EV-HOU-013], housing authorities keep waiting lists [EV-HOU-012].

**Hundreds of thousands have no home at all.**
- On a single night in 2024, 771,480 people were homeless, about 23 of every 10,000 people in the country. That was the highest number ever recorded, 18 percent more than a year earlier [EV-HOU-025].
- The 2025 count was 745,652: 479,332 in shelters and 266,320 unsheltered [EV-HOU-014]. It is slightly below 2024 but well above 2007's 647,258 and the 2016 low of 549,928 [EV-HOU-015].
- Chronic homelessness among individuals reached 155,750 in 2025, the highest number recorded, up from 77,486 in 2016. This count leaves out chronically homeless people in families, who are reported separately, so it understates the total. In law, a chronically homeless person has a disability and has been homeless for at least one year continuously, or on four separate occasions totaling one year over three years [EV-HOU-016].

**There are too few homes the poorest renters can afford.**
- In 2023 there were only 59 affordable and available rental homes for every 100 very low-income renter households, and only 38 for every 100 extremely low-income renter households [EV-HOU-024].
- An outside estimate puts the gap for extremely low-income renters at 7.1 million homes [EV-HOU-018]: only 35 affordable and available homes for every 100 such households, with shortages in every state and major metropolitan area [EV-HOU-019].
- Estimates of the national shortage across all incomes range from roughly 4 million to 5 million homes. Methods differ, and some observers argue there is no overall shortage, only shortages in particular places or types of housing, including homes low-income renters can afford [EV-HOU-017].

**Grades.** Worst case needs, the assistance share, the HUD shortage ratios and the 2024 homelessness count come from HUD's reports (grade A). The 2025 rent-burden figures are Census American Community Survey counts (grade A); the shares are the project's arithmetic on them. The 2025 homelessness figures are HUD's counts as the Congressional Research Service reports them, and the outside shortage estimates are its summaries (grade C).

## Questions this section leaves for the next ones

- **Help or homes.** Where affordable homes are this scarce, can vouchers alone house the lowest-income renters, or must the design also build (HOU-2, HOU-3, HOU-6)? Section 2 weighs the evidence.
- **Causes.** The counts in this section are data. What caused them is analysis, and HUD's two reports offer different accounts. The worst case needs report, published under the current administration, calls immigration "one key cause" alongside weak wage growth [EV-HOU-027]. Its own decomposition credits household formation, which it describes as largely driven by record immigration and declining marriage, with adding 321,000 cases in 2021-2023, the largest upward demographic factor. Gains in homeownership, incomes and assistance more than offset it, and a tight rental market added 196,000 [EV-HOU-029]. The decomposition does not measure immigration on its own, so how much immigration added is the report's reading, not its estimate. The homelessness report, published under the previous administration, lists the affordable housing crisis, inflation, stagnant wages, systemic racism, disasters, immigration and the end of pandemic-era aid such as the expanded child tax credit, without estimating their shares [EV-HOU-028]. Neither account is used here until independent research corroborates it. Section 2 weighs that research, including peer-reviewed work on immigration and rents, before the design relies on any cause.
- **Affordability and incomes.** How much of the burden is rent outpacing incomes, and how much is incomes too low for any market rent? The answer separates supply measures (HOU-2, HOU-3, HOU-6) from income-side help (HOU-4 vouchers). It belongs in section 2, "What works".
- **Who is reached first.** Who must the guarantee reach first: renters at risk of losing their homes, or everyone over the burden line? That is a design fork for section 3 and a decision memo for Espen.
