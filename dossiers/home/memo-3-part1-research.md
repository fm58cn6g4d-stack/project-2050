# Memo 3, part 1: research on the housing tax credits

Role: the evidence behind the revised part 1 of `memos/3-production.md` (tax credits and lasting affordability), written after Espen re-opened that part on November 9, 2026. Status: draft, awaiting Espen's decision on memo 3 part 1. The memo is what Espen decides from; this file holds the detail. ## What was asked

Espen, November 9, 2026: "memo 3, part 1 i am actually inclined to do a bit of all three options (A, B, C) or to request more detailed research on recommendations for U.S. federal policy. maybe this is my bias but i would be surprised if a middle income housing tax credit was without merit since many folks i respect have raised it (but could be it's just not the best use of funds)."

This file answers with: the bills as they stand (section 1), the evidence on each credit (section 2), designs that keep a middle-income credit while answering its risks (section 3), and what remains unknown (section 4). Espen's prior was not used as evidence either way.

## 1. The law in 2029 and the bills

### Current law: what the 2025 tax law did, and what stands in 2029

- The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21, July 4, 2025) permanently raised each state's 9% credit allocation by 12% and cut the share of a bond-financed project's basis that bonds must cover from 50% to 25%, for buildings placed in service in tax years beginning after 2025 (Joint Committee on Taxation, 2025) [EV-HOU-260] [EV-HOU-113].
- JCT scored it at $15,689 million over 2025-2034 (Joint Committee on Taxation, 2025) [EV-HOU-261].
- The House-passed version also treated rural and tribal areas as high-cost areas with a basis boost; the final law dropped it. It did not enact the extremely-low-income boost (Affordable Housing Credit Improvement Act text, 2025) [EV-HOU-263].
- No law since, including the ROAD to Housing Act (P.L. 119-101), amends the credit's section of the tax code; that Act lets banks hold more tax-credit equity (20% of capital instead of 15%) and asks GAO for a middle-income housing study by July 2027 (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-241] [EV-HOU-242]. The search covered the public laws of the 119th Congress through November 9, 2026, by title and by text for section 42.
- So the 2029 baseline is: allocations 12% above the old level, indexed for inflation; the 25% bond test; no deep-income boost; no middle-income credit; no Neighborhood Homes credit.

**Who backs each, and their strongest case.**
- *LIHTC expansion.* The broadest coalition of the three: bipartisan sponsors, state housing agencies, developers and investors, and housing advocates. Strongest case: it is the main federal tool that builds rental homes for low-income tenants at all, it reaches extremely low-income renters when rental help is attached [EV-HOU-118], and a boost aimed at 30%-of-median units targets the group with the worst shortage, 33 affordable and available homes per 100 [EV-HOU-282].
- *Middle-income credit.* Wyden, the senior Democrat on Senate Finance, has proposed it since 2018 and put it in his DASH Act, the most comprehensive progressive housing bill in Congress. Its current House version is bipartisan. Industry groups (apartment owners, realtors, mortgage bankers, property managers) and Up for Growth back it (Rep. Panetta's office, 2026) [EV-HOU-267]. Strongest case: rents for new buildings in costly places are above what teachers, nurses and other middle earners can pay, no federal tool serves them, cost burdens are climbing the income ladder, and a credit can be combined with LIHTC in mixed-income buildings. Harvard's Joint Center finds such programs "hold some promise" where affordability is severe or building is hard (Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2024) [EV-HOU-283].
- *Against the middle-income credit.* From the left, the National Low Income Housing Coalition calls it "wasteful and misguided": just 2% of renters at 80-100% of area median income are severely cost-burdened, and the shortage is at the bottom (NLIHC, 2023) [EV-HOU-282]. From the right, AEI's Housing Center argues it spends heavily without touching the cause, land-use limits (AEI event page, undated; not in the register).
- *Neighborhood Homes credit.* Bipartisan sponsors, state housing agencies, community lenders and a coalition of more than 50 national groups (National Council of State Housing Agencies, 2026; not in the register); it was in the House Build Back Better bill (2021) and President Biden's budgets (Congressional Research Service and Treasury, not in the register). Strongest case: in neighborhoods where homes sell for less than they cost to build or repair, nothing gets built without a gap subsidy, and vacant homes drag the block down.

## 2. The evidence on each credit

### LIHTC: how many homes it adds, at what cost, for whom

- **Mostly displacement, nationally.** The newest causal estimate finds that for every ten tax-credit homes about eight replace private homes that would have been built within ten years; the credit has added about 500,000 homes on net, at about $1 million of federal cost per net added home (Soltas, MIT working paper, 2024) [EV-HOU-275]. That matches the peer-reviewed studies: nearly full offset in one (Eriksen and Rosenthal, 2010) [EV-HOU-115], a third to a half of a home added per subsidized home in another (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114].
- **Much less displacement where markets are tight and dense.** The same study finds the cost per net added home is about $515 thousand in tracts above median density against about $1371 thousand below it (Soltas, 2024) [EV-HOU-277]. A peer-reviewed study finds crowd-out in gentrifying areas but not in stable or declining ones (Baum-Snow and Marion, 2009) [EV-HOU-116]. These differ on where crowd-out bites, so targeting is supported but its exact rule is not settled.
- **Where the money goes.** Developers capture around half the subsidy and the cost of competing for it absorbs a quarter; tenants get about three-tenths (Soltas, 2024) [EV-HOU-276]. Rents average about 12% below market and the rent limit does not bind for about half of applications, but the credit roughly triples the share of tenants with incomes under $20,000 (Soltas, 2024) [EV-HOU-278]. Most of the deepest-income tenants also have rental help (O'Regan and Horn, NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].
- **Rental help is cheaper per household helped** (Congressional Research Service, 2019; Soltas, 2024) [EV-HOU-112] [EV-HOU-276]. This is the memo's original reason for preferring help over broad credits, and the new evidence strengthens it.
- **Affordability runs out**: about a third of 2.5 million tax-credit homes leave their rent limits by 2035 (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].
- **What follows.** A larger credit adds homes mainly where the market would not build them and buys deeper affordability where a boost reaches the poorest. The deep-income boost is the best-supported piece, because it is where the rent limit binds hardest. An across-the-board allocation increase has the weakest support per added home.

### Middle-income credit: does a cap at 60-100% of median income buy anything?

- **In most places the cap sits above typical rents.** A rent cap at 80% of area median income is above the Fair Market Rent for a two-bedroom in all but five counties, and above the small-area Fair Market Rent in zip codes holding 87% of metro residents; at 120%, over 99% (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-272]. Where a cap is above market rent, the credit lowers no one's rent; it pays for building that would happen anyway or for nothing.
- **But that compares with existing homes, not new ones.** Fair Market Rents are 40th-percentile rents of the existing stock [EV-HOU-272]. The Terner Center's development math for three California markets finds market-rate studios generally priced below 100% of median and South Bay rents at 100% "comparable to market rate rents", yet "not one single policy change" makes middle-income building pencil (Terner Center, Garcia and Metcalf, 2024) [EV-HOU-279]. Read together: in costly places the market is not building for middle earners, so a credit's value there is homes that would not otherwise be built, not a lower rent on homes that would. That is the strongest case for a credit, and it holds only where building is stalled; it is also where crowd-out should be lowest, by the same logic as the low-income credit in dense, tight tracts [EV-HOU-277].
- **State and local analogues show the leak.**
  - California's government-owned "middle-income" bond deals spent about $5 billion buying 9,000 existing apartments at over $540,000 each; units capped at 120% of median were "effectively at market rates" (CSG Advisors, HR&A Advisors and California Housing Partnership, 2021) [EV-HOU-280].
  - Seattle's tax exemption produced 7,047 rent-restricted homes, but restricted rents exceed market rents in lower-cost neighborhoods and only about half the cost returns as lower rents (University of Washington, 2024) [EV-HOU-281].
  - Other states lend or grant per unit (Massachusetts up to $100,000 at 60-120% of median; Colorado's Middle Income Housing Authority; Georgia and Kansas grants at about $23,000-27,000 a unit), with no independent evaluation found (Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2024; not separately in the register). Texas's public facility corporation exemptions removed more than $5 billion of property from Harris County's tax rolls, and the 2025 legislature tightened them after doubts about their discounts (Baker Institute, 2026; Texas Housers, 2025; not in the register).
  - None of these is a federal construction credit, and none was evaluated for added homes. What they show is consistent: a shallow cap near market rent buys little; the benefit comes where the cap is well below market.
- **Need is lower but not zero.** About 32% of renters at 80-120% of median had housing problems in 2023, and 4.9% severe ones, against 75% and 42% below 80% (Congressional Research Service, from HUD data, 2026) [EV-HOU-273]. Middle-income programs "do not serve most working renters with affordability challenges, who are overwhelmingly lower-income" (Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2024) [EV-HOU-283].
- **Independent publishers converge on targeting.** CRS names targeting to high-cost areas, measured as LIHTC's difficult development areas are, and warns that widening low-income programs serves the middle at the expense of the poor (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-274]. Harvard's Joint Center recommends programs that do not divert money from the lowest-income households and operate only where the market cannot serve middle incomes (2024) [EV-HOU-283].
- **What follows.** The evidence does not support the credit as filed, nationwide at $1 per resident. It does support a narrow version: only in high-cost areas where middle-income building is stalled, with a real rent discount, kept apart from the money for the poorest. That is a design case, not an evaluated program: no middle-income credit has been evaluated anywhere.

### Neighborhood Homes credit

- **The best causal evidence of the three, from a close analogue.** Missouri awards a similar credit by lottery. In St. Louis, winners got nearly two-thirds more building permits than losers, crowd-out was smaller in declining neighborhoods, and each $1 of credit raised the recipient's assessed value by about $0.50 (Clarke and Freedman, working paper, 2018) [EV-HOU-284]. That fits the finding that subsidized building crowds out little in declining areas (Baum-Snow and Marion, 2009) [EV-HOU-116].
- **The risks are cost control and who gains.** Because the credit fills the gap dollar for dollar, developers may set lower prices and spend more, raising the cost per home; a direct grant may be cheaper, though tax-equity investors add oversight (Congressional Research Service, 2021) [EV-HOU-271]. In the Missouri study about half the credit shows up in the value of the home, a gain to the buyer and the block, not only to builders (Clarke and Freedman, 2018) [EV-HOU-284].
- **Who it serves.** Owner-occupants at or below 140% of median income, in tracts with low incomes, high poverty and low home values [EV-HOU-269]. It is the one credit that builds wealth for buyers in places the market has left, and it reaches middle earners.
- **Scale.** Backers aim at 500,000 homes over a decade (Neighborhood Homes Coalition, 2026; not in the register). No independent estimate exists.

**The blend Espen asked about ("a bit of all three").** One version that takes something from A, B and C and follows the evidence:
- From A and C: keep all three credits in law.
- From B: put the most money where the evidence is strongest, the LIHTC increase with the deep-income boost [EV-HOU-263] [EV-HOU-118]; and make every rental home the plan pays for permanently affordable.
- The middle-income credit as M2 + M3 + M4 + M5: tight markets only, a real discount, zoning pairing, permanent affordability, a five-year trial with an evaluation.
- The Neighborhood Homes credit at the bill's design for 2030-34 with an evaluation (about $3 billion a year at $9 per resident by CRS's method [EV-HOU-270] [EV-HOU-269], so about $15 billion over five years), renewed if homes get built at a reasonable cost.
- Total: about $0.10-0.14 trillion over 2030-39, against $0.15 trillion (range $0.10-0.20) for all three credits as filed (legacy estimates for the LIHTC part; this dossier's arithmetic for the rest).

## 4. What remains unknown

- **No official cost estimates** for any of the three bills as they stand; JCT must score them. The legacy LIHTC estimate predates the 2025 law, which enacted part of the old bill.
- **No evaluation of any middle-income credit**, federal or state, for homes added. The JCHS review found no outcome evaluations; this file found none either.
- **No count of where new-building rents exceed middle-income caps**, metro by metro. CRS's comparison uses existing-stock rents [EV-HOU-272]. It could be built from HUD income limits and new-lease rent data; that would size M2's eligible area.
- **GAO's middle-income housing study is due July 11, 2027** [EV-HOU-242], before the plan starts. It may define "workforce housing" and should be read before section 4 writes any credit.
- **The Neighborhood Homes evidence is one working paper** in one city [EV-HOU-284]; its sponsors' own scale estimates are advocacy figures.
- Not yet in the register: AEI's critique, the Baker Institute and Texas Housers' Texas figures, Massachusetts and Colorado program data, the NYC Comptroller's 421-a analysis, HUD's 2023 LIHTC tenant tables (HUD blocks automated downloads), the Novogradac DASH Act summary and Congress.gov cosponsor counts.
