## Part 1. The brief: a home you can keep

### The problem, in a few true numbers

Rent takes too much of too many paychecks. In 2025, about 21.6 million renter households, half of all renters, spent more than 30% of their income on housing, and 11.1 million spent more than half (Census Bureau, American Community Survey, 2025) [EV-HOU-038] [EV-HOU-039]. Almost one renter in four fell behind on rent at some point that year, and nearly one in three of those earning under $50,000 (Federal Reserve, *Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households*, 2026) [EV-HOU-008] [EV-HOU-009].

Help exists, but most people who qualify never get it. In 2023 only 28% of very low-income renters received any housing assistance (HUD, *Worst Case Housing Needs*, 2025) [EV-HOU-022]. 8.46 million households had what HUD calls worst case needs: a very low income, no help, and rent above half their income or a home in very bad condition [EV-HOU-020]. For the poorest renters there were only 38 affordable homes available for every 100 households [EV-HOU-024].

Owners have problems too. Needed repairs to occupied homes were estimated at $198.4 billion in 2024, and lower-income households carried $74.6 billion of it (Philadelphia Fed, *Home Repair Costs*, 2025) [EV-HOU-602]. Among renters, 2.46 million older households without children had worst case needs in 2023 [EV-HOU-030].

And more people have no home at all. On one night in 2024, 771,480 people were homeless, the most ever counted, nearly 150,000 of them children (HUD, *Annual Homelessness Assessment Report*, 2024) [EV-HOU-025] [EV-HOU-026]. The 2025 count was 745,652 (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-014].

### What the plan does for people

The plan starts in 2029. At its center is one rental help system, built step by step, reaching the people the evidence says it helps most first (section 4, HOU-4.02 and HOU-4.03). Around it are help to buy a first home, more homes of every kind, repairs for owners and landlords, and help for older people to stay in their homes (section 4, parts 7 and 8).

**A million new rent awards, 2030-33.** 250,000 a year, paid as permanent spending so they never lapse in a budget fight (HOU-4A.01). They go first to people with no home of their own: people who are homeless, families with children who are doubled up with others, and people leaving prison, jail, a hospital or foster care with nowhere to go, who can apply before they leave. A family shows it by its own word or a school record. There is no cap and no deadline test (`DEC-2026-11-09-12`; HOU-4.03).

**A guarantee for the poorest families with children.** From 2034, families with children with the lowest incomes and severe housing problems enroll, along with families with children who have no home of their own, up to 300,000 a year. From 2037 it becomes a right: every eligible family that applies gets help (HOU-4D.01). The right is to a home, not just a voucher. If a family's search fails, the housing agency must make an offer within 60 days (an apartment with help attached where one is free, otherwise a new voucher with more search help and a higher rent limit) and keep offering until the family is housed. An application gets a decision within 30 days, or it counts as approved. A family turned away can go to court, with its legal fees paid if it wins (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`, `-14`).

**Then renters with long-term disabilities, at any age.** From 2038, extremely low-income renters with a long-term disability join, older renters included, with SSI, SSDI or Medicaid disability coverage as proof, so there is no new disability exam (HOU-4.08; `DEC-2026-11-09-14`, `DEC-2026-11-10-03`).

**Vouchers that work.** Search help comes with every new award. Payment limits are set by neighborhood, so families can rent in more places. A national rule would make it illegal to turn someone away just because they pay with a voucher, with money to test for that discrimination (HOU-4.05 to HOU-4.07). In tight markets, some help is attached to homes the plan builds or buys, with a right to move on after a year (HOU-4.04).

**An emergency fund for a crisis.** A standing fund pays back rent, utilities, a deposit or moving costs for renters with low incomes who face an eviction notice, a filing, a shutoff or a disaster, up to four months of the area's rent in a year, with legal help besides (HOU-1D.01; `DEC-2026-11-09-17`). People can show need with their own statement, and landlords are checked before they are paid (HOU-1D.02). It starts with $15 billion in 2030-31 and then about $5 billion a year. On our estimate that reaches roughly 1.4 million households a year, at about $3,500 each; if households need more each, it reaches fewer (section 6, 2.2; section 7, 1.6).

**Housing First.** Federal homelessness money goes back to Housing First: a home without first proving sobriety or entering treatment, with services offered (HOU-4.09). Supportive housing expects providers to stay in touch with tenants, a duty on the provider, never a reason to put anyone back on the street (HOU-4.11). A voluntary recovery-housing option is tested on its results (HOU-4.10). Places that clear encampments without offering shelter or housing get no new homelessness money (HOU-4.09).

**Building and keeping homes.** State housing funds get about $72 billion of grants, and $300 billion of federal construction and purchase loans are offered, all awarded by the late 2030s (HOU-3C.01, HOU-3C.02). Up to $30 billion of those loans can build starter homes and condos for sale, and part of the grants can make homes to buy that stay affordable for later buyers (HOU-3C.05). Each state also sets a share of the money for homes for older and disabled renters, by its own need (HOU-3C.06). Public housing is repaired first, with a right to return for tenants and no re-screening (HOU-5.01, HOU-5.02). A social housing pilot and a small federal builder test public options (HOU-8.01, HOU-9A.01). Every rental home the plan pays for stays affordable for good (HOU-2D.04).

**Tax credits.** A bigger low-income housing credit, with a boost for buildings that house the poorest renters, and two five-year trials: a middle-income credit in high-cost areas and a credit for building or repairing homes for sale in low-income neighborhoods (HOU-2D.01 to .03; `DEC-2026-11-09-08`).

**Rewards for places that allow homes.** Towns and states that change their rules get paid for homes actually finished, and new transportation money comes with the same condition (HOU-6.01, HOU-6.02). The list of rules that count includes single-stair apartment buildings, manufactured homes treated like other homes, small lots, no parking minimums and state review exemptions for infill (`DEC-2026-11-09-20`). Federally funded housing on infill sites skips federal environmental review, and a review that still applies counts as done after 90 days (HOU-11.03). Federal land near growing western cities is sold at fair value for housing (HOU-11.02), and manufactured homes get cheaper loans, with grants for residents to buy their communities (HOU-11.01).

**Help to buy a first home.** First-time buyers up to 140% of the area's median income, 160% in high-cost areas, can get $20,000 or 10% of the price, whichever is more, up to $25,000, or $30,000 for a newly built home, toward the down payment on a home priced within FHA's limit for the area, through their state's housing finance agency, with first-generation buyers first when money is short. A buyer's own signed statement is enough. States can stack the help with their own and local programs, with no extra federal paperwork, and free counseling is offered, not required (HOU-10.01; `DEC-2026-11-09-19`, `-23`, `-24`, `DEC-2026-11-10-03`). FHA's yearly mortgage insurance premium on purchase loans falls from 0.55% to 0.30% for loans made in 2030-39, as long as its insurance fund stays well capitalized, with a review before it is renewed (HOU-10.02). On our estimate the down payment help reaches about 1.1 to 1.2 million buyers over the decade (section 6, 2.12).

**Repairs.** A standing repair fund, run by the states, cities and tribes that already run federal housing grants, fixes roofs, heat, wiring, plumbing, lead and accessibility for low-income owners: grants up to $25,000 a home at or below 80% of area median income, plus up to $15,000 for lead or accessibility work, and zero-interest loans repaid at sale above that, up to 120%. Income is shown by a signed statement or by enrollment in SNAP, Medicaid or similar programs, with no credit score and no rule that owners be current on taxes. On our estimate it reaches about 220,000-330,000 homes a year (HOU-12.01; `DEC-2026-11-09-21`, `-25`; section 6, 2.9). Small landlords get repair loans that are forgiven if rent increases stay small, tenants ordered out of an unsafe home get moving costs and up to three months' rent, and one front door connects repairs with weatherization and heat pumps (HOU-12.02, HOU-12.03).

**Aging with dignity.** Older and disabled people get home changes such as grab bars, ramps and a first-floor bathroom, with nurse and therapist visits on a tested model, from the repair fund (HOU-12.04). The federal government pays half of state property-tax relief for owners 65 or over and disabled owners up to 80% of area median income, in states that meet simple standards: enrollment from records the state already holds, no yearly reapplication, and the right to defer the rest of the bill until sale (HOU-13.01; `DEC-2026-11-09-22`, `-25`). Legal help clears tangled titles and fights tax foreclosures; more buildings for older renters get service coordinators; older owners can borrow to build a unit for a caregiver; and small grants match older owners with tenants who help around the house (HOU-13.02 to .04, HOU-12.01).

**Tribal housing.** Tribes get their own shares: 3% of the emergency fund, 3% of the new rent awards and 5% of the construction grants, about $14 billion over the decade, through tribal governments and under each tribe's own plan (HOU-4.13; `DEC-2026-11-09-16`).

**Rural homes through local offices.** USDA's rural offices are rebuilt to about 5,000 staff, so applications are decided by people who know the county. Rural families up to the area's median income can get a direct USDA home loan, and guaranteed loans reach the same 140% and 160% limits as the down payment help. Rural owners can get the repair fund at a USDA office, with no age-62 rule. When a rural apartment building's mortgage is paid off, its tenants keep their rent help, and a fund helps nonprofits buy and fix these buildings. Self-help and farmworker housing grow. One simple test decides which places count as rural, and no place or household loses eligibility in the change (HOU-14.01 to .06, HOU-12.01; `DEC-2026-11-10-02`).

**Targets anyone can check.** About 1.3 million homes the plan pays for, built or preserved, over 2030-39; homes finished in rewarded places at least 1% of their stock above their own past pace; and a national goal of 15 million homes finished in the decade, today's pace held steady (HOU-3C.04; `DEC-2026-11-09-17`).

**Fair to every family.** No work or treatment rules to get or keep help, no residency rules, and protection for families where some members are immigrants without full status, so the citizens and eligible members keep their help (HOU-4.01, HOU-4.03; `DEC-2026-11-09-10`). The shared household record holds basic information and can never be used for immigration enforcement (HOU-4.02; `DEC-2026-11-09-11`). An honest mistake on a form is corrected, never punished; help ends only for fraud (HOU-4D.01).

By the early 2040s, the rental help houses about 3.0 to 3.6 million more households than today, depending on how many awards turn into a lease (section 6, 1.4 and 2.12).

### What it costs and how it's paid for

About $0.81 trillion over 2030-39 (range about $0.60-1.14 trillion). The rental help, building and credits are about $0.65 trillion of it; help to buy and broad supply add about $57 billion, repair and aging in place about $61 billion, the rural channel about $14.5 billion, and stage 3's older disabled renters with stage 2's full count about $22 billion (section 6, "In brief", 2.8, 2.9, 2.11 and 2.12). The lasting cost is about $110 billion a year by 2040, about 0.22% of the economy. That counts tax credits, a property-tax match and more besides rent help, and even so it is a small fraction of the 1.4% of the economy the United Kingdom spends on housing allowances alone (OECD Affordable Housing Database) [EV-HOU-159]. The construction money, the repair fund, the down payment help and FHA's lower premium are time-limited and end in 2039; the rent help, the emergency fund, the credits and the property-tax match are lasting.

The project's budget rule says lasting programs must be paid for from 2040 on. Housing's lasting costs are not yet fully paid for in 2040: the gap is about $40 billion that year, $51 billion counting construction and repair money still being spent. The largest pieces are help for renters with disabilities, about $18 billion, and stage 2's families, including those with no home of their own, about $16 billion; the property-tax match adds about $3 billion on our estimate (section 6, "In brief", 2.12, 3.3). 2041 is short too, by about $7 billion on the central figures, which leave out interest on the borrowing and several named risks; from 2042 the rule passes (section 6, 3.5). Espen decided that the 2040 gap goes to the plan's financing settlement, where the whole plan's revenue is decided, rather than cutting housing now (`DEC-2026-11-09-09`); whether 2041's does too is still open.

### What we're honest about

- **Vouchers only help if families can use them.** The plan's numbers assume 90% of awards end up leased. The closest recent program reached 67% after about two years, and only 57% of voucher searches succeeded in 2022 (UC Berkeley study of Emergency Housing Vouchers, Economy and others, 2023; NYU Furman Center, Ellen and others, 2025) [EV-HOU-345] [EV-HOU-395]. That is the plan's weakest number. At 75%, about 3.0 million households are housed instead of 3.6 million (section 6, 1.4). The duty to house answers it for families with the right from 2037, not for the first million awards.
- **It doesn't reach everyone who needs it.** About 3.0 to 3.6 million households is a large step against 8.46 million with worst case needs. Single adults who are not homeless, older renters without a disability and families just above the line have no funded route to rental help in the 2030s; the plan names a next stage for them in the 2040s, not yet funded or dated (section 4, HOU-4.03; section 7, 5.8). Older owners now have routes: repairs, home changes, property-tax relief and legal help. Older renters get service coordinators and a share of new homes, but not rent help, until the care program is scoped (section 7, 3.7; `DEC-2026-11-09-10`).
- **Help to buy can raise prices** where homes can't be built, as it did in London (Carozzi, Hilber and Yu) [EV-HOU-545]. So the help is capped at $25,000, or $30,000 for a new home, only for homes within FHA's price limit, ends in 2039, and HUD watches prices where it's used most (section 7, 3.6; section 8, 1.14). An estimated 0.17-0.26 million of the down payment help's buyers are lasting added owners who wouldn't otherwise have bought, a judgment, since no U.S. study measures it; the rest would have bought later or anyway (section 6, 2.8).
- **Repair needs outrun the fund.** It meets perhaps half of lower-income households' repair needs, so waits will remain (section 7, 3.7). Whether the property-tax match keeps older owners in place rests on one study, and the home-visit model's gains faded by a year (section 7, 3.7) [EV-HOU-681] [EV-HOU-688].
- **The plan's own building is small next to the shortage.** About 1.3 million homes the plan pays for is about 9% of the national goal; most homes will still be built privately (HOU-3C.04).
- **It doesn't promise to end homelessness.** For a family that gets a voucher, the effect is large and well tested. How much the national count falls is unknown, because people keep losing homes (section 7, 1.5).
- **The 2040 gap is real** and not yet paid for (above).
- **Some rules still take money back.** A single parent with two children on a voucher and SNAP, earning in the earned-income credit's phase-out range, keeps about 25 cents of each added dollar before payroll and income taxes, as under today's law. Free child care would have raised voucher rent; the plan's rule now ignores its value (section 7, 3.1-3.2; HOU-4.01).
- **Protections need a law.** The national voucher protection, the mixed-status protection and Housing First in statute need a regular bill, not only the budget bill (section 4, consistency notes). Until it passes, they are not in place.
- **What would change our minds** is written down. If, despite search help and full fees, lease-up stays below 70% after 18 months, the plan leans further toward homes with help attached. Every trigger starts a published review, and no review takes help away from a household (section 8; `DEC-2026-11-09-15`).

## Part 2. Organizing material

### The case in one paragraph

Half of America's renters spend more than they can afford on rent, almost one in four fell behind last year, and only about one in four of the poorest renters gets any help. This plan, starting in 2029, makes rental help something families can count on. It funds a million new rent awards, first for people with no home of their own, then guarantees a home, not just a voucher, to the poorest families with children by 2037, then reaches renters with disabilities of any age. It adds a standing emergency fund so a missed paycheck doesn't become an eviction, brings back Housing First, and pays for homes built, bought and repaired that stay affordable for good, with rewards for towns that allow more homes of every kind and shares that tribes control. It helps first-time buyers with the down payment, fixes the homes of owners who can't afford repairs, and helps older people stay in the homes they own. It costs about $0.81 trillion over ten years. It doesn't reach everyone yet, its lasting costs are not fully paid for in 2040, and it says so, with published counts and a review whenever the numbers fall short.

### Hard questions, in plain words

These follow section 7's objections (`7-validation.md`, 5). Each answer says what the evidence shows and what is still true on the other side.

**"Won't vouchers just push rents up?"** In some places, yes, a little. The best later study in the dossier's evidence found more vouchers didn't raise rents overall; the rises were in units near the voucher limit, in cities that can't build (Eriksen and Ross, 2015) [EV-HOU-096]. An older study found larger effects (Susin, 2002) [EV-HOU-397]. So the plan sets payment limits by neighborhood, attaches some help to new and purchased homes in tight markets, rewards towns that build, and phases in at 250,000 a year. It also publishes rents near the voucher limit every year, so we'll see it if it happens (section 7, 5.1 and 6).

**"Doesn't subsidized building just replace private building?"** Partly. On average one subsidized home adds a third to a half of a home to the market (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114]. That's why the plan is mostly rent help, why its building money leans toward buying and preserving homes and deep affordability, and why the new credits are five-year trials that have to show results (section 7, 5.2).

**"It costs too much."** About $110 billion a year by 2040 is about 0.22% of the economy, counting tax credits and the property-tax match as well as rent help. The United Kingdom spends 1.4% of its economy on housing allowances alone (OECD Affordable Housing Database) [EV-HOU-159]. Much of the rest is time-limited: the building money, the repair fund and the down payment help end in 2039. It goes first to the groups with the strongest evidence it works (section 7, 5.3).

**"Is it paid for?"** Not fully, in 2040 or 2041. The gap in 2040 is about $40-51 billion; the largest pieces are help for renters with disabilities and stage 2's families. It goes to the financing settlement, with the rest of the plan's revenue. 2041 is short by about $7 billion on the central figures, and the budget rule passes from 2042 (section 6, "In brief", 3.3 and 3.5; `DEC-2026-11-09-09`).

**"Won't down payment help just push up home prices?"** It can, where homes can't be built: help to buy raised prices in London and in Bavaria (Carozzi, Hilber and Yu; Krolage, 2023) [EV-HOU-545] [EV-HOU-546]. So the help is a fixed yearly amount, capped at $25,000, or $30,000 for a newly built home, only for homes within FHA's price limit, ends in 2039, and comes with more building. HUD publishes prices where it is used most, and a fast rise starts a review (section 7, 3.6; section 8, 1.14).

**"States will refuse."** The core doesn't need them. Rent awards go to housing agencies, with a backup administrator where one declines; building and emergency money can go to cities, counties, nonprofits or straight to borrowers; the voucher protection is a federal civil rights floor (section 7, 5.5).

**"Landlords won't take vouchers."** In tests, most landlords refused voucher holders in three of five cities, and far fewer did in the two where the law forbade it, though still 31% in Newark (HUD, 2018) [EV-HOU-093]. The plan adds the national protection, testing for discrimination, payment within 30 days, a damage fund, quick inspections and search help (section 7, 5.6). This is still where the plan could stumble: it's the lease-up number.

**"Housing help makes people stop working."** The effect is modest. A rent rule built to reward work didn't raise earnings in six years (MDRC, Rent Reform Demonstration, 2024) [EV-HOU-400], and homeless families with vouchers saw no change in average earnings [EV-HOU-086]. The plan adds no new phase-out and no work rule. The remaining problem, several programs phasing out at once, belongs to the plan's income floor (section 7, 5.7).

**"It doesn't go far enough."** That's fair. It reaches about 3.0 to 3.6 million households against 8.46 million with worst case needs, and it says so. Each stage is written so a later Congress can open the next one, and the social housing pilot and the federal builder can grow if they work (section 7, 5.8 and 5.9).

### Who it helps: illustrations

*Every person below is a composite type built from the plan's rules. None is a real person. Use them as "a family like this one", never as a name or a quote.*

- **A mother and two children on her sister's couch** (illustration). They've been doubled up since an eviction. In 2030 she tells the intake worker they have no home of their own, and the school's record backs her up. She gets one of the new rent awards, with someone to help her search, and the voucher pays the rent above about 30% of her income (HOU-4.03, HOU-4.07).
- **A home health aide whose hours were cut** (illustration). Two months behind, with a notice on the door. She applies to the emergency fund, writes down what happened, and the fund checks that the landlord really owns the building and pays the back rent. A lawyer helps her answer the notice. If another crisis hits the same year, she can come back under the same four-month cap, and the second time she's pointed toward longer-term help (HOU-1D.01, HOU-1D.02; `DEC-2026-11-09-17`).
- **A man who has slept outside for years** (illustration). He has a serious mental illness. Under Housing First he gets an apartment without having to start treatment first. His provider has to keep reaching out with a service navigator, and if he says no to treatment, he keeps his home (HOU-4.09, HOU-4.11).
- **A family whose search fails in 2037** (illustration). Their income is very low, and they have three kids and a rent they can't pay. They now have a right to help. Their voucher search runs out without a lease, so within 60 days the agency must make an offer: an apartment with help attached if one is free, or else a new voucher with more search help and a higher rent limit. Offers keep coming until they're housed. If the agency misses the deadline or turns them down, they can go to court (HOU-4D.01).
- **A tribal housing authority** (illustration). Its homes are crowded and in poor repair, which a voucher can't fix. It uses its share of the construction grants to repair and build, under its own housing plan, without going through the state (HOU-4.13).
- **A town council that legalizes duplexes** (illustration). The plan pays it for the homes actually finished above its past pace (HOU-6.01).
- **A first-generation buyer** (illustration). A nurse's aide and her partner earn about the area's median income, and neither of their parents owned a home. They sign a statement saying so, get $25,000 toward a new $250,000 starter home built with the plan's loans, stack it with their city's second mortgage, and pay a lower FHA premium every month (HOU-10.01, HOU-10.02, HOU-3C.05).
- **An older owner on a fixed income** (illustration). A widower of 78 owns his house outright, but the roof leaks and the property tax keeps rising. The repair fund fixes the roof and adds grab bars on his signed statement, with no credit check. His state, which joined the tax match, credits back the tax above a share of his income and lets him put off the rest until the house is sold, enrolled from the records it already holds (HOU-12.01, HOU-12.04, HOU-13.01).
- **Who the plan doesn't yet reach** (illustration, for honesty). A single man working full time in a city, paying 60% of his income in rent, not homeless and not disabled. In the 2030s the plan has no funded route for him beyond the emergency fund in a crisis. The plan names a next stage for people like him in the 2040s, not yet funded. New homes and the zoning reward may ease his rent over time, but no one should promise him more (section 7, 5.8; HOU-4.03).
- **A man leaving prison with nowhere to go** (illustration). Three months before release he tells the prison's reentry staff he has no home to go to. He applies then, on his own word and the discharge plan, so the award is ready when he walks out (HOU-4.03).
