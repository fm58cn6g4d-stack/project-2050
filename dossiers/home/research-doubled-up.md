# Research note: doubled-up families and the order of reach

Role: research for HOU-4.03 (the order of reach) in section 4. It answers the question section 7 raised (`7-validation.md`, 1.5 and question 1): should families doubled up because of economic hardship count as "without a home" in stage 1 of the rental guarantee? Espen asked on November 9, 2026 for "more research on this and make a final rec". Status: draft (stage C). Its recommendation (section 6) is superseded by Espen's decision `DEC-2026-11-09-12`; its counts and evidence stand, and section 7 gives the stage 2 count the cost pass needs. It does not edit section 4.

Evidence is EV-HOU-420 to 434 in `evidence.json`, plus earlier entries where cited. Counts from executive-branch sources are data; this note relies on no executive-branch causal reading (`DEC-2026-10-30-01`). Several sources were read through a search tool because their hosts block downloads; the catalog rows say which, and the reviewer should check those quotations against the originals.

## In brief

1. **"Doubled up" means three very different sizes.** HUD's homelessness programs count a doubled-up household only when it must leave within 14 days, or, for families with children, in a narrow case that needs a school's or other agency's finding plus two moves in 60 days [EV-HOU-420]. The schools' definition counts every child sharing housing because of hardship: 1.15 million students over the 2023–24 school year [EV-HOU-424]. Survey-based research counts 3.2 to 3.7 million people of all ages on an average night [EV-HOU-425] [EV-HOU-426].
2. **Literal homelessness alone already fills stage 1.** About 0.59 million households are homeless on a single night [EV-HOU-434], and about 1.06 million households used shelter over 2022 [EV-HOU-427]. The million awards (250,000 a year, 2030-33) go almost entirely to stage 1 under every option. So the real effect of the definition is not mainly on stage 2. It decides **who within stage 1** is housed first.
3. **Adding all doubled-up families with children would push single homeless adults back by years.** Stage 1 puts families with children first. About 0.4 to 0.7 million doubled-up families with children (this note's estimate) would come ahead of the 0.5 million single adults homeless on a night [EV-HOU-434], including the 155,750 chronically homeless individuals [EV-HOU-016] whom HOU-4.11 says to house first.
4. **Stage 2, as written, leaves doubled-up families out entirely.** Stage 2 covers *renter households* with HUD's worst case needs. A family staying in someone else's home is not a renter household, and HUD treats overcrowding as only a moderate problem [EV-HOU-432]. The overlap the lead expected between the two stages is small. Families with no home of their own, doubled up or in shelter, have no route into stage 2 at all. That is a drafting gap in HOU-4D.01, whatever Espen decides here.
5. **Doubled-up families fare badly, and vouchers help them.** Doubling up is the usual step before shelter. 29 percent of sheltered families came straight from staying with family or friends [EV-HOU-427], and 85 percent of families in the Family Options Study had doubled up as adults [EV-HOU-428]. Their children do worse in school than poor children with homes of their own; compared with sheltered children, the findings are mixed [EV-HOU-430] [EV-HOU-431]. In two randomized trials, vouchers sharply cut doubling up and homelessness [EV-HOU-428] [EV-HOU-429].
6. **Decided differently (`DEC-2026-11-09-12`).** Espen chose one plain test, "no home of your own", with no deadline test and no cap (section 6). The recommendation it replaced: stage 1 takes a doubled-up family with children only when its arrangement is ending: the host has said it must leave within 14 days, or it has moved twice or more in 60 days. These families are capped at 20 percent of each year's stage 1 awards (50,000 a year). The count is published yearly. Every other poor doubled-up family with children goes to stage 2 from 2034, once stage 2's wording is fixed to admit families without a home of their own.

**Working estimate (judgment).** About 0.4 to 0.7 million poor families with children are doubled up for hardship at any one time. The school count over a year sits near the top of the range, the survey measure near the bottom. The families-per-student ratio is an assumption (grade E); no source in the register gives it. All doubled-up people, single adults included, come to roughly 2 to 2.5 million households. That rests on the same assumption, applied to 3.2 to 3.7 million people.

**Severe conditions.** No national source counts doubled-up families by overcrowding, or by how soon the arrangement ends. Among very low-income renter households, 797,000 were overcrowded in 2023, 94.5 percent of them families with children [EV-HOU-432]. That count is of hosts and other renter households, not guests. The nearest measure of "about to lose the arrangement" is the flow into shelter: about 41,000 families a year came from family or friends [EV-HOU-427].

## 2. How doubled-up families fare, and whether vouchers help

**Doubling up is the step before shelter.**
- 29 percent of families entering shelter in 2022 came straight from staying with family or friends [EV-HOU-427].
- In the Family Options Study, 85 percent of families in shelter had doubled up as adults, and 45 percent did so just before shelter [EV-HOU-428].
- In California's statewide study, nearly half of homeless people had lived doubled up first [EV-HOU-426].

**After shelter, doubling up is where families land.** At 20 months, 31 percent of families in usual care had recently doubled up [EV-HOU-428].

**Children.**
- In Houston, across 400,000 students, doubled-up students fared relatively well on most outcomes; children in shelter fared worst [EV-HOU-430].
- In Washington, doubled-up students did no better than other homeless students. 15 percent were proficient in math, against 25 percent of housed low-income students [EV-HOU-431].
- *Reading.* Doubled-up children do worse than poor children with homes of their own in both places. Against sheltered children, the evidence is mixed: better in one district, the same across one state. The Washington comparison group, housed low-income students, is the nearest the register has to stage 2's families. No source compares doubled-up families directly with extremely low-income renters with worst case needs (evidence still needed).

**Vouchers work for them.**
- In the welfare-to-work voucher trial, among families who had been living with friends or relatives, homelessness over a year fell from 18 to 12 percent. Vouchers also cut doubling up [EV-HOU-429].
- In the Family Options Study, recent doubling up fell from 31 percent in usual care to 12 percent with a long-term subsidy [EV-HOU-428].
- An independent randomized evaluation of supportive housing for families in child welfare found the same direction: less doubling up, fewer moves and evictions [EV-HOU-433].
- Tenant vouchers are better than building-based subsidies at reaching people who would otherwise have no home of their own [EV-HOU-114].
- *Reading.* A voucher helps a doubled-up family at least as much as a renter family with a severe rent burden: it gives the family a home of its own rather than lowering a rent. But each such voucher needs one more vacant unit, so lease-up in tight markets is the risk (section 7, 1.2).

Plan numbers: 250,000 awards a year in 2030-33 (HOU-4A.01). Stage 1 puts families with children first (HOU-4.03). Stage 2 opens under HOU-4D.01 in 2034, at up to 300,000 families a year, for extremely low-income *renter* families with children with worst case needs: 1,717 thousand households in 2023 [EV-HOU-186]. **Why stage 2 is mostly about order, not size.** Under every option, stage 1 absorbs the million before 2034. HOU-4D.01 opens in 2034 on its own money. So none of the options delays stage 2's start, and section 7's trigger ("stage 1 above 800,000 awards by 2032") cannot fire: only 750,000 awards exist by the end of 2032. What changes is whether 2030-33's awards go to single adults on the street, or to families who have a roof but not a home. The second question is whether doubled-up families can enter stage 2 at all.

**The gap in stage 2.** HOU-4D.01 covers "renter households ... with severe problems (... HUD's worst case needs test)". A family staying as a guest is not counted as a renter household; its host may be [EV-HOU-432]. Overcrowding alone is not a severe problem in that test [EV-HOU-432]. As written, then, stage 2 excludes two groups:
- poor doubled-up families with children;
- homeless families with children still unhoused when HOU-4A's new awards end in 2033.

Section 7 already warned that when stage 2 becomes a right, doubled-up families may form their own households and apply (7-validation.md, 1.3). Writing them in makes that orderly. It also enlarges stage 2's group from about 1.7 million to about 2.1 to 2.4 million families (this note's estimate), which section 6 would need to price.

## 4. Administration: verifying status without intrusive checks

Espen's rule for the shared record is to collect only the basic information collected for everyone, where possible (decision 11 of November 9, PR #140, not yet merged). The narrow test can meet it.

1. **Same questions for everyone.** The common application (HOU-4.02) asks every applicant where they stayed last night (own place on a lease, with others, shelter, outside, hotel), and, if with others, whether they must leave and by when, and how many times they have moved in the past 60 days. These are housing-situation questions every applicant answers, and HMIS already records prior living situation for everyone entering shelter [EV-HOU-427]. Nothing about disability, abuse history or criminal record is asked to decide this status.
2. **The applicant's word first.** HUD's rule lets a household's oral statement that its host will not let it stay more than 14 days count, if the intake worker records it and finds it credible. The statement is confirmed with the host or, failing that, after documented effort [EV-HOU-421]. Lack of papers may never delay shelter [EV-HOU-421]. Recommended: self-certification recorded by the intake worker is enough. A call to the host is optional, never a home visit.
3. **Reuse findings that already exist.** HUD already accepts a school district's McKinney-Vento certification as proof for families with children [EV-HOU-421]. Schools identified 1.15 million doubled-up students in 2023–24 [EV-HOU-424]. With the family's consent on the common application, that finding counts as proof of the two-moves test or of doubling up. No new check is needed.
4. **Never contact the host's landlord.** Overcrowding may breach the host's lease and bring eviction [EV-HOU-432]. Renters hide extra household members for the same reason [EV-HOU-425]. A check that reached the host's landlord could make two households homeless.
5. **Drop HUD's "barriers" prong.** Category 3 also requires expected instability from disability, abuse histories or barriers to work [EV-HOU-420]. Collecting that is intrusive, conflicts with Espen's rule, and adds nothing the two-moves test does not.
6. **Check after, not before.** A random sample of awards is reviewed after lease-up, as the point 5 note does for emergency help (`point-5-emergency-aid-rules.md`). This is judgment.
7. **Coordinated entry** stays an access point (HOU-4.02). It records the category from the same questions. How each Continuum ranks households inside stage 1 is not in the register (evidence still needed: HUD's coordinated entry notice and prioritization guidance).

## 5. What the options mean for others in the plan

- **HOU-4.11** asks that stage 1 awards house chronically homeless high-users first. Options (b) and (c) would push them back years under "families first"; (d) does not.
- **HOU-4.03's "imminent risk to HOU-1D" rule** suits renters with a lease, where a month's rent can save the home. A guest family whose host asks it to leave has no rent to pay. HOU-1D cannot keep it housed, so option (d) moves only doubled-up *families with children* out of that rule. Single adults at imminent risk stay with HOU-1D and coordinated entry.
- **The 10 percent limit in current law** [EV-HOU-423] shows Congress has already capped school-defined homelessness as a share of homelessness money. A share cap here follows that precedent.
- **Race and place.** Doubled-up homelessness is relatively more common among Hispanic people and in some rural areas than literal homelessness is [EV-HOU-425]. Option (a) alone would under-serve both. Option (d) and the stage 2 fix reach them.

## 6. Recommendation (superseded by DEC-2026-11-09-12)

**Decision.** Espen decided on November 9 that stage 1 uses one plain test, "no home of your own": people literally homeless and doubled-up families with children, shown by the family's own word or an existing school record, with no deadline test and no cap. Local need-based intake sets the order. Counts are published yearly, and an imbalance two years running triggers a review. Stage 2 (HOU-4D.01) is widened from 2034 for doubled-up and homeless families (`DEC-2026-11-09-12`, under the design lens `DEC-2026-11-09-13`). The recommendation below is kept as the record of what was weighed.

**Recommendation as written (option d, with a stage 2 fix).** It rests on judgment applied to the counts above and to the voucher trials (Welfare to Work Voucher evaluation, Mills and others, 2006 [EV-HOU-429]; Family Options Study, Bush and Shinn's summary, 2017 [EV-HOU-428]). The plain-language version:

1. **Who comes first in 2030-33.** People with no home at all: on the street, in a shelter, or fleeing violence. They are joined by doubled-up families with children whose arrangement is ending: the family must leave within 14 days, or has moved twice or more in the past 60 days.
2. **How it is checked.** Everyone answers the same short questions about where they stay. The family's own statement, recorded by the intake worker, is enough, or a school's existing finding that the children are homeless. There are no home visits, no questions about disability or history, and no contact with the host's landlord.
3. **The safeguard.** Doubled-up families take at most 20 percent of each year's stage 1 awards: 50,000 a year, 200,000 over 2030-33. HUD publishes the count by category every year from 2030, and again before the 2032 awards. If the cap binds two years running, HUD reports to Congress, and the cap is not raised automatically.
4. **Everyone else who is doubled up** (poor families with children) **goes to stage 2 from 2034, a right from 2037.** That needs one fix in section 4: HOU-4D.01 should cover extremely low-income families with children who have no home of their own (doubled up or homeless), not only renter households. Section 6 prices it.

**What it implies.** About 40,000 to 80,000 families a year qualify under the narrow test, and at most 200,000 get awards before 2034. At least 800,000 of the million go to people literally without a home. About 0.4 to 0.7 million other doubled-up families with children wait for stage 2 and enter it from 2034 once the wording is fixed.

**Why not the alternatives.**
- *Literal only (a)* leaves families with children sleeping on a host's floor with nothing until they reach a shelter. It also leaves them out of stage 2 for good.
- *All doubled-up families with children (b)*, the lead's earlier recommendation, would hand most of the million to families who have a roof, ahead of single adults on the street. That cuts against HOU-4.11's priority.
- *All doubled-up (c)* is three times the million.

**Questions for Espen** (both answered by `DEC-2026-11-09-12`: no cap or deadline test; stage 2 widened).
