# A home you can keep: what would change our minds

Role: section 8 of the housing dossier. For each major design choice (13 in all) it names the main uncertainty, the evidence or event that would make the plan revise the choice (with a threshold where one can be set), who would see it and when, and what the plan would do instead. It also covers the political, legal and economic events that would change the plan. Espen decided its four questions as recommended (`DEC-2026-11-09-15`). Status: draft (stage C, step 6). It works only from sections 1-7, the memos and Espen's decisions (`DEC-2026-11-09-01` to `-17`). It adds no evidence entries: every threshold below either comes from section 7's early-warning table (`7-validation.md`, 6) or is marked as judgment. It changes no provision. What it asks of sections 4 and 7 is listed at the end, and the choices that are Espen's are questions at the end and in the README.

Outcomes the triggers protect: OUT-04 (a home people can keep), OUT-02 (planning beyond the next paycheck), OUT-01 (child poverty), and OUT-11 for the fiscal rule.

## How to read this section

- **Five parts per choice.** The main uncertainty; what would change our minds, with the direction of the change; who would see it and when; what the plan would do instead; and whether the trigger is a review or a hard stop.
- **Review or hard stop.** This section applies the design lens (`charter/STANDARDS.md`, "Design lens"; `DEC-2026-11-09-13`). Most triggers start a **review**: a published look at the evidence that can lead to new guidance, money moved between grantees under the published rules, or a proposal to Congress. A review never takes help away from a household by itself. A **hard stop** acts at once, and this section allows one only where waiting would do harm: fraud, a double payment, or a grantee breaking a condition that protects tenants. Every hard stop falls on a payee, a grantee or a provider, never on a household. Section 4 gives the reasons for each.
- **Where the data come from.** Most signals come from the shared household record (HOU-4.02), which section 5 makes public every month by statute (`5-delivery.md`, 4 and 8). The others are HUD's yearly tight-market list (HOU-4.04), the production reports (HOU-3C.04), the emergency fund's payment-error sample and data standard (HOU-1D.03), the supportive-housing measures (HOU-4.11, item 5), the trials' lotteries and evaluations (HOU-4.04, 4.10, 2D.02, 2D.03), the paired testing grants (HOU-4.06), the ROAD to Housing Act's own reports [EV-HOU-224] [EV-HOU-231], and each new section 6 estimate.
- **Thresholds are judgment** unless a tag says otherwise. They say when to look harder. They are not proofs that a choice has failed.

## In brief

1. **The softest assumption is lease-up, and that is where the plan's biggest change would start.** Section 6 assumes 90% of awards are leased. The only precedent measured the same way reached 67% after about two years (`7-validation.md`, 1.2). If national lease-up stays below 70% at 18 months even with full fees, search help and the source-of-income rule, the plan should lean further toward homes with help attached in tight markets. That means a larger attached share, and a stronger case for the construction money. It would not mean a turn to "build first".
2. **Stage 1's plain test is decided. Its risk is who waits.** Doubled-up families with children may come ahead of single adults on the street for years (`research-doubled-up.md`, 3). The decided response is a review after two years of imbalance (`DEC-2026-11-09-12`). Espen adopted this section's working reading of "imbalance" (question 2).
3. **Three choices are trials, and their evaluations decide them:** the middle-income credit, the neighborhood homes credit, and recovery housing (all 2030-34). The attached-help evaluation (2036) decides the fourth. Each has a renewal test, written below as a threshold.
4. **Two choices wait on reports already scheduled in law:** the investor limit (the GAO and HUD reports in 2037) [EV-HOU-224] and the zoning reward's Build Now interaction (HUD's study in 2032).
5. **Hard stops are few and aimed at payees:** fraud, double payments, a recovery-housing operator turning away people on prescribed medicines, and a place sweeping encampments without an offer of shelter or housing. No trigger in this section ends a household's help.
6. **Two decided designs carry open counts:** tribal set-asides against need (1.12) and stage 3's definition, which could double its count (1.13).
7. **The plan's biggest outside risks** are a failed R2 (34 of 50 difficult runs never finish it; `5-delivery.md`, 3), a stalled regular bill, and a hostile HUD after the 2032 election. Each has a fallback below. The largest gap is that a hostile HUD could ignore its own reviews, so Espen decided on one review procedure with GAO as the default reviewer (question 1).

## 1. The design choices

### 1.1 Vouchers or attached homes, matched to the market (memo 1 B; HOU-4.04)

- **Main uncertainty.** Whether help attached to plan-financed homes in tight markets leases faster than a tenant voucher, keeps families out of poor neighborhoods, and keeps rents down near the payment limit. The register has no evaluation of attached help in tight markets (memo 1).
- **What would change our minds.**
  - *Toward more attached homes:* national lease-up below 70% at 18 months, or below 50% at an agency with 500 or more awards (section 7's threshold); or rent growth for units near the payment standard more than 2 points a year above other units for two years, in tight markets (section 7's threshold; the risk is set out in `7-validation.md`, 5.1) [EV-HOU-096] [EV-HOU-095].
  - *Back toward vouchers everywhere:* the 2036 randomized evaluation finds attached homes lease no faster than tenant vouchers in the same markets (judgment: less than 10 points faster at 12 months), or more than a quarter of attached homes sit in tracts that have since passed 30% poverty (judgment), or most households leave at the 12-month right to move (judgment: more than half within two years). Any of these would mean the attachment is not worth giving up choice for [EV-HOU-090].
- **Who sees it, when.** HUD's yearly tight-market list and the record's monthly lease-up by agency and market type, from 2030; rent growth by price band, yearly; the attached-help evaluation, by 2036 (HOU-4.04, item 5).
- **What we'd do instead.** More attached homes: raise the attached share toward HOU-4.04's 50% ceiling in tight markets where either signal fires, after a published review (decided, question 4). Fewer: memo 1's option A, tenant vouchers everywhere, with neighborhood payment limits and search help carrying the load. Memo 1's option C ("build first") stays off the list, because it makes families wait while homes are built [EV-HOU-086].
- **Kind.** Review. HOU-4.04 already lets HUD raise the share only after the evaluation. Espen decided that a published review can do it sooner (question 4).

### 1.2 The guarantee's breadth and order (memos 2 and 8; `DEC-2026-11-09-12`; HOU-4.03, HOU-4D.01)

- **Main uncertainties.**
  - *Who stage 1 reaches first under the plain test.* Literal homelessness alone fills the million awards. Adding 0.4-0.7 million doubled-up families with children could push single homeless adults back by years, chronically homeless people included (`research-doubled-up.md`, 1 and 3) [EV-HOU-424] [EV-HOU-434].
  - *How many families claim the right in 2037.* Take-up is assumed at 85%, inside the range of SNAP and the earned income credit (`7-validation.md`, 1.3) [EV-HOU-398] [EV-HOU-399]. The bigger risk is the count: families now doubled up may form their own households once help is a right.
- **What would change our minds.**
  - *Stage 1 out of balance,* two years running (`DEC-2026-11-09-12`). Decided (question 2): "imbalance" means that one group's share of stage 1 awards (single adults literally homeless, families literally homeless, or doubled-up families) sits more than 15 points below its share of those assessed as eligible by local intake, or that one group's median wait passes 12 months (the numbers are judgment).
  - *Stage 2's sign-up* below 70% by 2038, the low end of section 6's range. That is a sign of outreach or paperwork failure, not of low need, because these families have severe housing problems (`7-validation.md`, 1.3).
  - *Stage 2's count* above section 6's high case after 2037. That changes the cost (see 1.11). It does not change who is entitled.
- **Who sees it, when.** Stage 1's counts by group, published yearly from 2030 (HOU-4.03). Stage 2's applications, approvals and placements, monthly from 2034. Section 6's estimate at each budget cycle.
- **What we'd do instead.** For stage 1, first change the intake guidance HUD gives Continuums of Care and housing agencies. Only if the imbalance persists after that, go back to the research note's narrower option: doubled-up families with children enter stage 1 when their arrangement is ending, with the rest routed to stage 2 from 2034, which `DEC-2026-11-09-12` already opens to them (`research-doubled-up.md`, 6). For low sign-up, more outreach through schools and the common application (HOU-4.02). A cap on the right is not a fallback: memo 2 B made it a right, and section 6 prices the cost risk.
- **Kind.** Review. `DEC-2026-11-09-12` says so in terms.

### 1.3 Tax credits: option D and its two trials (`DEC-2026-11-09-08`; HOU-2D.01 to .04)

- **Main uncertainties.** Whether a middle-income credit buys anything (no such credit has been evaluated; its rent caps sit above typical rents almost everywhere [EV-HOU-272]). Whether the neighborhood homes credit builds at a reasonable cost per home [EV-HOU-271]. How much of the bigger low-income credit replaces building that would have happened anyway [EV-HOU-275] [EV-HOU-277]. Whether permanent affordability drives developers away (research variant M4).
- **What would change our minds.**
  - *Middle-income trial:* the lottery comparison shows no measurable rise in homes finished in awarded places, or restricted rents at lease-up are less than 10% below documented market rents (HOU-2D.02's decided test). Either ends the trial in 2034. The renewal rule is decided: renewed only if it adds homes.
  - *Neighborhood credit:* public cost per home finished above about $400,000, the line where section 7 finds the plan's other capital stops being good value (`7-validation.md`, 1.4 and 6; judgment), or homes that resell without the owner-occupancy the credit pays for.
  - *Low-income credit:* the Joint Committee on Taxation's score above section 6's high case ($0.12 trillion over FY2030-39) goes to a review of size. Before the bill: GAO's July 2027 study [EV-HOU-242] finds the middle-income gap lies somewhere other than high-cost areas, in which case HOU-2D.02's targeting is redrawn before 2029.
  - *Permanent affordability:* state agencies cannot place their full allocation for two years running, against full use today (judgment). That would mean the rule is costing homes.
- **Who sees it, when.** GAO, July 2027. JCT, when R2 is scored in 2029. The trials' interim reports by 2034 and final reports by 2037 (`DEC-2026-11-09-14`; HOU-2D.02). State allocating agencies' yearly reports to the IRS.
- **What we'd do instead.** If both trials fail: memo 3 part 1's option B, the low-income credit with the boost for the poorest renters and permanent affordability, at the lowest cost. If only one fails, end that one. If permanent affordability shrinks placement: keep permanence for homes the plan finances directly (HOU-3C.03) and use a long fixed term with a nonprofit right to buy for new credit homes (memo 3, option A's rules as a floor; judgment).
- **Kind.** Review. The trials end on their own dates unless renewed, so no stop is needed.

### 1.4 The construction money and the small federal builder (memo 3 parts 2 and 3; HOU-3C.01 to .04, HOU-9A.01)

- **Main uncertainties.** Cost per home, which decides whether HOU-3C.04's first number can be met: about 1.3 million homes the plan pays for, built or preserved over 2030-39 (range 0.8-1.9 million), of which 900,000 come from the construction money (600,000-1.3 million) and the rest from the credits (`DEC-2026-11-09-17`). Whether new state revolving funds can spend $10-11 billion a year from 2032; new state programs have started slowly [EV-HOU-150]. Whether the loans are used, since they need steady rent, much of it from attached help. Whether a U.S. public builder can deliver at all: the register has no evaluation of one (memo 3).
- **What would change our minds.**
  - *Target at risk:* homes financed below the yearly path to HOU-3C.04's first number (section 7's threshold), or public money above about $400,000 a home, where section 7 finds the capital stops being good value and the construction money's 900,000 falls toward the bottom of its range (`7-validation.md`, 1.4; HOU-3C.04).
  - *A state behind:* a state below its share of the path for two years (judgment).
  - *Loans not used:* commitments below half the yearly schedule for two years while grant demand exceeds the grants (judgment). That would mean deals need deeper subsidy than loans give.
  - *The builder:* by 2035, its homes cost more per home or take longer to finish than state-channel homes in the same markets (judgment). That would argue against any growth. Better results would support the scale-up memo 3 left for a later decision.
- **Who sees it, when.** HOU-3C.04's yearly reports by place and income band, from 2031. Section 5's state fund plans, 9 months after R2. The builder's first project reports.
- **What we'd do instead.** A missed target is a published warning, not a reason to withhold money (HOU-3C.04, "What the targets do"). A state that lags gets technical help first, then section 5's pass-through order (cities and counties, then a regional fund, then direct loans), and the small builder may build where the state missed its share (HOU-9A.01). Unused loans: memo 3 part 3's option C, more grants and fewer loans, which costs about $65 billion more against the budget. Very high costs in loose markets: memo 3 part 3's option D, less building and more rent help there. The builder: memo 3 part 2's option B (fold it into the social housing pilot) or C (no builder).
- **Kind.** Review. A fraud finding on a project is a hard stop for that project's draws (see section 4).

### 1.5 The zoning reward through the ROAD Act fund (memo 4 B; HOU-6.01, HOU-6.02)

- **Main uncertainties.** Neither the Innovation Fund nor Build Now has been funded or evaluated (memo 4). Paying for homes finished may mostly reward places that were already building [EV-HOU-177]. Build Now rewards a rise in a place's own growth rate rather than a high level of building [EV-HOU-244].
- **What would change our minds.**
  - Awards in 2030-34 go mostly to places whose building was already rising before they reformed (judgment: more than half of award dollars).
  - Rewarded places miss HOU-3C.04's second number: homes finished above their own prior pace of at least 1% of their housing stock within five years of the award, about 600,000 homes nationally (`DEC-2026-11-09-17`). Across rewarded places, a shortfall for most of them by 2035 would mean the reward pays for less than it should (judgment).
  - Lower-cost homes, now counted on their own, stay a small share of homes rewarded (judgment: below a quarter).
  - HUD's 2032 study finds that Build Now penalizes steady builders below 4% growth (HOU-6.01).
  - The regular bill does not pass, so the money must run under the Act's current rules: cities, counties and tribes only, at $10 million an award at most [EV-HOU-206].
- **Who sees it, when.** The fund's required reporting of homes finished and of lower-cost homes, yearly. HUD's Build Now study, 2032. The regular bill's fate, 2029-30 (`5-delivery.md`, 3).
- **What we'd do instead.** Rewrite the award formula to pay for building above a place's own trend. If the regular bill fails, run the money under current rules and move the statewide part to the 2031 transportation condition (HOU-6.02). If the reward does not add homes, memo 4's option D: fund the law as written and put the rest into HOU-3C. The reward's life after 2039 is already open (section 6, question 6: it ends unless renewed on results).
- **Kind.** Review.

### 1.6 Housing First, voluntary recovery housing and the provider engagement standard (memo 5 B; HOU-4.09 to 4.12)

- **Main uncertainties.** Housing First's housing results are strong [EV-HOU-099] [EV-HOU-101]. Its results for drug use are not shown [EV-HOU-100], and evidence is thin for people with active addiction [EV-HOU-105]. Recovery housing has no evaluation in the register. A duty on providers that never ends a tenancy may not produce engagement unless the services workforce exists (`5-delivery.md`, 2.6) [EV-HOU-341].
- **What would change our minds.**
  - *The core choice* would change only with new randomized evidence that housing conditioned on treatment keeps as many people housed as Housing First, against the 88% reduction in homelessness the CDC's review found [EV-HOU-101]. Nothing in the register points that way.
  - *Housing results below the trials':* stable housing among supportive-housing tenants well below the trials' 71% of time stably housed [EV-HOU-099] (judgment: under 60% at 12 months in a program for two years).
  - *Services not arriving:* more than 10% of tenants without a navigator after 90 days (section 7's threshold), or monthly contact rates falling for two years.
  - *Overdose deaths* among a program's tenants rising for two years, against falling medicine starts (HOU-4.11's measures).
  - *Recovery housing:* the 2034 randomized evaluation finds worse housing stability or more overdose deaths than Housing First placements for comparable people, or few people choose it.
- **Who sees it, when.** Each program's yearly report of contact, retention, overdose deaths, medicine starts and exits by reason (HOU-4.11, item 5). The recovery-housing evaluation, 2034. Section 7's services signal.
- **What we'd do instead.** For low engagement or poor results: more workforce money and treatment access through HOU-4.12 and the care flagship, and a review of the provider. The tenant is never made to engage. If recovery housing does worse: memo 5's option A, Housing First alone, ending the recovery grants in 2034. Memo 5's option C (treatment as a condition, the 2025 order) is not a fallback on the current evidence [EV-HOU-101].
- **Kind.** Review, with two hard stops: a recovery-housing operator that refuses residents on prescribed medicines loses its grant eligibility (HOU-4.10's quality standard), and a place that removes people or their belongings without offering shelter or housing gets no new homelessness money (HOU-4.09, item 4). A provider that ends a tenancy for non-engagement faces the tenant's private right of action (HOU-4.11). A pattern of it starts a review of that provider's funding.

### 1.7 The standing emergency fund (memo 6 C; HOU-1D.01 to .04)

- **Main uncertainty.** Targeting. The fund's value rests on reaching people at real risk of losing their homes. In lottery studies, broad pandemic aid helped people pay rent but changed housing stability little [EV-HOU-153]. An evaluation published under the second Trump administration credits the same program with fewer evictions [EV-HOU-152], so both views stand (memo 6). Chicago's low cost per case averted came from people calling a homelessness line [EV-HOU-108]. At a 5% risk of homelessness, each case averted costs about $93,000; at 20%, about $23,000 (`7-validation.md`, 1.6).
- **What would change our minds.**
  - Fewer than half of paid households have an eviction filing or notice (section 7's threshold).
  - The first-year randomization at the margin of the risk screen (section 7's ask of section 4) shows no fall in shelter entry or eviction judgments among screened households.
  - The published payment-error rate above its target for two years at a grantee (HOU-1D.03).
  - Repeat use concentrated in the same households, in the published count of repeat use that replaced the episode limits (`DEC-2026-11-09-16`; the annual cap of four months of Fair Market Rent, usable across crises, `DEC-2026-11-09-17`). A second crisis in a year already prompts referral to long-term help; a rising share of households at the cap, or of third crises, would mean the fund is carrying people who need a voucher (judgment).
  - In a recession, demand far above the capped yearly amount (section 6, 2.2).
- **Who sees it, when.** Grantees' monthly payment data in the federal format and the cross-grantee duplicate check. HUD's error estimate from the first year's sample, published in 2031 and yearly after. Linked eviction-court and shelter records for evaluation (HOU-1D.03).
- **What we'd do instead.** Tighten the risk screen and fast-track court cases; give a larger share to legal help (point 4). If the fund still averts little, memo 6's option B: one-time help only, with the standing money moved to vouchers or attached homes. In a recession, a one-time top-up through the same network, as HOU-1D.04 was built to allow. Section 6's range ($3-8 billion a year) is the size lever.
- **Kind.** Review for targeting and size. **Hard stops:** a landlord who fails the ownership check is not paid (HOU-1D.02, decided). A payment that the duplicate check flags is held. A grantee or landlord found to have committed fraud is cut off from payments, and the money is recovered from the grantee, never by evicting a tenant over an honest mistake (HOU-1D.03).

### 1.8 Source-of-income protection and neighborhood payment limits (points 1 and 2; HOU-4.05, HOU-4.06)

- **Main uncertainties.** Laws against refusing vouchers cut refusals sharply where they exist: 15% and 31%, against 67-78% where they do not [EV-HOU-093]. Their effect on lease-up is mixed [EV-HOU-094]. Neighborhood payment limits moved families to lower-poverty areas [EV-HOU-095], but their effect on cost per award is unpriced (section 6, 6), and they lower the limit in cheaper ZIP codes.
- **What would change our minds.**
  - Paired tests after 2032 still find refusal rates above 31%, the top of the range seen under existing laws [EV-HOU-093]. That points to weak enforcement, not a wrong rule.
  - Lease-up falls in ZIP codes where the new limits came in below the old metro-wide limit (judgment: more than 5 points below the same metro's other ZIP codes for two years).
  - Cost per award rises above section 6's high case where the limits apply.
- **Who sees it, when.** The Fair Housing Initiatives Program's paired tests, funded at $100 million a year for 2030-34 (`DEC-2026-11-09-17`; HOU-4.06). Lease-up by ZIP code from the record. Section 6's cost per award on each estimate.
- **What we'd do instead.** For weak enforcement: carry the testing money past 2034 and give more weight to private enforcement. If the regular bill fails or a court narrows the protection: point 2's alternative, rewarding states that adopt the protection through the zoning fund (HOU-6), with the 23 state laws and 152 local ones [EV-HOU-247] as the floor. For payment limits: raise the floor in the ZIP codes that lag, and keep the two-year hold harmless for sitting tenants (HOU-4.05).
- **Kind.** Review.

### 1.9 The investor limit, left as enacted (point 8, HOU-7B; HOU-7B.01)

- **Main uncertainty.** What the ban does. The evidence on large investors is mixed [EV-HOU-138] [EV-HOU-139] [EV-HOU-140], and no study of the ban itself exists. Its exceptions are wide, including build-to-rent [EV-HOU-223].
- **What would change our minds.**
  - *Earlier than 2037, toward a permanent ban:* the 2029 GAO and HUD report [EV-HOU-224] finds clear gains for owner-buyers or renters in markets with many investors, or the 2037 report does.
  - *Toward narrowing the exceptions:* investors' yearly holdings reports show their purchases moving into the exempt build-to-rent and renovate-to-rent channels (judgment: holdings in exempt channels growing faster than the ban removed).
  - *Toward letting it lapse:* the reports find no effect, or harm, such as less building of rental homes.
- **Who sees it, when.** GAO and HUD, 2029 and 2037. Investors' holdings reports by city, yearly [EV-HOU-224].
- **What we'd do instead.** The chosen HOU-7: remove the 2042 sunset in the regular bill. The reporting is already law, so nothing else changes.
- **Kind.** Review, at the two dates in law. Nothing else in the plan depends on the ban.

### 1.10 The duty to house and the production target (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`; HOU-4D.01, HOU-3C.04)

- **Main uncertainties.** Whether there are homes to place families in when searches fail in tight markets after 2037. In 2022 only 57% of searches succeeded [EV-HOU-395], and the placements depend on attached homes that the capital must first produce. Whether the 60-day deadline (`DEC-2026-11-09-14`) is workable: the median successful search took 78 days [EV-HOU-395].
- **What would change our minds.**
  - More than 20% of entitled families still unhoused six months after approval (section 7's threshold).
  - Offers made late, past the 60 days, in more than a quarter of failed searches at an agency (judgment).
  - Placements leaning on the 120% payment standard in a market while rents near the standard rise faster (see 1.1).
  - The target's yearly path missed (see 1.4).
- **Who sees it, when.** Monthly placements, offers and deadlines from the record, from 2037. HOU-3C.04's yearly production reports. Private suits under HOU-4D.01's remedies, a signal in themselves.
- **What we'd do instead.** Raise the attached share in that market (question 4). Bring in the backstop administrator where an agency cannot make offers (`5-delivery.md`, 6.3). If the production target is out of reach on cost, publish a lower target with the reason rather than hide the miss. Adding capital would change amounts memo 3 kept, so it would be Espen's decision. The deadline binds the agency, so a missed offer never ends the family's claim (HOU-4D.01; design lens item 4).
- **Kind.** Review. The remedies are the family's, not a trigger.

### 1.11 The 2040 fiscal gap sent to the financing settlement (`DEC-2026-11-09-09`)

- **Main uncertainty.** Whether the financing settlement closes a gap that is still moving. FY2040 fails by about $12 billion on permanent lines, or $19 billion with the capital tail. Stage 2's wider entry adds roughly $7-12 billion that is not yet priced. The high case fails both FY2040 and FY2041 (`6-cost.md`, 3.3).
- **What would change our minds.**
  - Housing's FY2040 path more than $10 billion above section 6's central on a new estimate (section 7's threshold). The pricing follow-up for stage 2's wider entry may cross this line by itself.
  - The settlement does not cover housing's share when it is drafted.
  - The Byrd rule's per-bill test, once built, shows R2's permanent housing lines have no offsets in their titles after the window (`6-cost.md`, 3.3, item 4).
- **Who sees it, when.** Section 6 and the budget model at each revision. The financing settlement (Codex's lead, `charter/STANDARDS.md`, "Roles"). B3's per-bill test.
- **What we'd do instead.** Section 6's fallbacks first: enroll stage 3 over 2038-41 instead of 2038-40, saving about $3 billion in FY2040 (question 1's fallback), and count a time-limited line's tail as time-limited (question 5's alternative). Then, proposed, for Espen (this section's judgment): set the emergency fund at the low end of its range ($3 billion a year). For the Byrd rule: offsets in the same bill, or sunsets on the permanent lines with renewal written in. A lease-up shortfall is not fiscal room (`7-validation.md`, 5.4). Rights already granted are not on this list.
- **Note.** These figures predate PR #146's pricing of stage 2's wider entry; refresh them when it merges.
- **Kind.** Review. Espen accepted slight FY2040 overshoots at this stage (`DEC-2026-11-09-09`), so nothing here acts automatically.

### 1.12 Tribal housing set-asides (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`; HOU-4.13)

- **Main uncertainty.** Whether the set-asides match the need. They are shares of decided amounts: 3% of the emergency fund and of new-award money, and 5% of the revolving-fund grants, about $13 billion over FY2030-39 (`DEC-2026-11-09-16`; `6-cost.md`, 2.6). Tribal needs are mostly physical condition and crowding, at 23% and 16% of households against 5% and 2% nationally [EV-HOU-406], and vouchers pay rent rather than repair homes. The register has no count of tribal homes needing repair or replacement. At $150,000-300,000 a home, the grant share repairs or builds roughly 12,000-24,000 homes (`7-validation.md`, 4.2).
- **What would change our minds.**
  - The 2032 need report (HOU-4.13, item 4; `DEC-2026-11-09-16`) finds the need well above what the set-asides deliver. That would argue for added investment, as Espen's direction asks.
  - Tribal set-asides unspent after three years, or a tribal lease rate on rental awards more than 10 points below urban areas (section 7's threshold for rural places, applied here to tribal areas). That would mean the form or the administration is wrong, not the amount.
- **Who sees it, when.** The shared record's tribal-area reports of awards, leases, payments and homes financed (HOU-4.13, item 6), from 2030. HUD's Office of Native American Programs and the tribes' 2032 need report.
- **What we'd do instead.** Where money sits unspent, more flexibility, worked out with the tribes: more of the award share used to repair or build homes, which item 3 already allows, and technical help to tribally designated housing entities. The money stays available until spent and is not taken back. Where the need is larger, raise the grant share, or add a tribal capital line sized from the need report (section 7's earlier option). Either would change amounts, so it would be Espen's decision. Every route goes through tribes under NAHASDA, never through states.
- **Kind.** Review. Any change is made with tribes, following self-determination (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`).

### 1.13 Stage 3: who counts as disabled, and how many (memo 2's condition; HOU-4.08)

- **Main uncertainties.** The definition is set with the care flagship and not yet written; this dossier's placeholder is the voucher program's definition, with SSI, SSDI or Medicaid disability coverage accepted as proof (`DEC-2026-11-09-14`). The count could double. Section 6 counts about 600,000 households under 62 (450,000-800,000), costing about $14 billion a year by FY2042 ($8-26 billion). HUD counts disability only under 62, and disabled renters 62 and over could be as many again: there are 1.749 million older extremely low-income households with worst case needs [EV-HOU-186] (`6-cost.md`, 2.1; questions 2 and 3 there, still open). Stage 3 also needs Medicaid home and community-based services where it lands (shared rule 7).
- **What would change our minds.**
  - The care flagship's definition takes in disabled people 62 and over, or drops the severe-problems test, and the count passes section 6's high case of 800,000 households. That would push the FY2040-42 cost above section 6's high case (see 1.11).
  - By 2036, two years before opening, the care flagship's Medicaid services are not in place in many of the places where stage 3's households live (judgment: states holding more than a quarter of the projected count).
  - Too few accessible homes reserved: HOU-3C.03's published count of reserved units by place is far below stage 3's demand (judgment: under one reserved unit for every ten households in stage 3 who need one).
- **Who sees it, when.** The care flagship's scoping, which comes before the 2029 bill. Section 6's re-pricing once the definition is set. HUD's readiness certification a year before the 2038 opening (`5-delivery.md`, 3). HOU-3C.03's count of reserved units.
- **What we'd do instead.** For cost: section 6's fallback, enrolling over 2038-41 instead of 2038-40, which saves about $3 billion in FY2040 (`6-cost.md`, question 1). If the older group is added: price it, and either send it to the financing settlement or phase it in after the under-62 group. That is Espen's decision with the care flagship. For services: HOU-4.12's backstop pays where Medicaid does not, and the opening date holds; services are offered, never a condition (HOU-4.08). For accessible homes: tenant-based vouchers with search help in the meantime, and a larger reserved share in the regular bill.
- **Kind.** Review. Readiness checks release extra administrative money, never a delay of the right (`5-delivery.md`, 3).

## Questions for Espen

Each is yes or no. **All four are decided:** Espen answered "as recommended" on November 9, 2026, (`DEC-2026-11-09-15`). They stay here so the reasoning can be traced; section 4 writes them in (asks above).

1. **Should every "review" in the plan follow one written procedure?** Proposed: when a trigger fires, the agency publishes a review within six months, with the data, the likely causes and the options in this section. If it misses the deadline, GAO does the review. A review may change guidance, technical help, or how money moves between grantees under the published rules. Any change to who is eligible, the order of reach or benefit levels goes to Congress. A review never cuts a household's help by itself. Recommendation: yes. It makes the design lens's "review, not restriction" hold up under a hostile HUD (`5-delivery.md`, 8). This rests on judgment. **Decided: yes, as recommended.**
2. **Should "imbalance" in stage 1 (`DEC-2026-11-09-12`) mean** that a group's share of awards is more than 15 points below its share of those found eligible by local intake, or that a group's median wait passes 12 months, two years running? The groups are single adults literally homeless, families literally homeless and doubled-up families. Recommendation: yes. It is a reading of your decision, not a change to it, and the numbers are judgment. It gives the review a clear starting point. The risk it watches is single homeless adults waiting behind doubled-up families (`research-doubled-up.md`, 3). **Decided: yes, as recommended.**
3. **If R2 fails and housing goes into a smaller R3, what goes first?** Recommendation (judgment): keep the rights and their dates (stage 2 in 2034 and 2037, stage 3 in 2038), the million awards with full fees, the record and the emergency fund's network. Stretch the capital and the credit trials before cutting any of those. The rental help has the strongest evidence [EV-HOU-086], and capital can be awarded later without breaking a promise to a family. This is also a scenario point for the lead. **Decided: as recommended.**
4. **Should HUD be able to raise the attached share toward 50% before the 2036 evaluation,** in a tight market where lease-up stays below 70% at 18 months or rents near the payment standard rise more than 2 points a year faster than other rents, after a published review? Recommendation: yes. It is the fallback the evidence points to first if lease-up fails (1.1), and it answers section 7's ask (`7-validation.md`, 5.1). The 50% ceiling and the right to move after 12 months stay. **Decided: yes, as recommended.**
