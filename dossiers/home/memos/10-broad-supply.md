# Memo 10: Building more homes of every kind

Role: decision memo for Espen, part 1 of the section 3 addendum (`../3-design.md`, "Addendum: homeownership and broad supply"). Status: awaiting Espen's decision; four parts, each decided on its own. The research is in `../research-ownership-supply.md`, section 4. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Counts marked "judgment" are this dossier's estimates.

## The question

You asked on November 9 for stronger policy on housing production "more broadly", not only homes for the poorest renters. The plan already has the zoning reward, paid for homes finished (memo 4, HOU-6.01), and about $386 billion of construction money (memo 3, HOU-3C). This memo asks what else the federal government should do so that more homes of every kind get built, at little federal cost. It has **four parts. Decide each on its own.**
- **Part 1.** Which local and state reforms should the zoning reward pay for?
- **Part 2.** Should the plan fix how manufactured homes are financed?
- **Part 3.** Should the plan sell federal land for housing?
- **Part 4.** Should federally assisted infill housing skip federal environmental review?

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** Part 1 widens what memo 4's reward counts; it adds no money and no rule. Parts 2 to 4 add new policies. None reverses an earlier decision.

## What the ROAD to Housing Act already does

The Act, law since July 11, 2026, already:
- lets manufactured homes be built without a permanent chassis and makes states treat them equally (Congressional Research Service, 2026; ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-219] [EV-HOU-250];
- raises FHA's loan limits for manufactured homes and backyard cottages (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-220];
- has HUD write model code language for single-stair apartment buildings (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-243];
- makes cities list the empty land they own (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-214];
- trims environmental reviews for some assisted infill housing, once HUD writes the rules (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-217] [EV-HOU-218].

It does nothing about local zoning that keeps manufactured homes out, high-cost loans for them, federal land, or condo lawsuits.

## Why it matters

- **Some rules raise costs a lot, mostly on the coasts.** Land-use rules add about $410,000 to a quarter-acre lot in San Francisco, but under $25,000 in a dozen interior metros such as Atlanta and Dallas (Gyourko and Krimmel, 2021) [EV-HOU-594].
- **Cheaper ways to build exist.** A new manufactured home costs about half as much per square foot as a site-built house (Census, 2025) [EV-HOU-575]. Small single-stair apartment buildings cost 6-13% less to build (Pew Charitable Trusts and the Center for Building in North America, 2025) [EV-HOU-580].
- **The evidence on most of these levers is thin.** It mostly shows what could be built or what builders do differently, not how many homes are added. Reforms that loosened land-use rules went with about 0.8% more housing in three to nine years (Urban Institute, 2023) [EV-HOU-177].

## Part 1: what the zoning reward pays for

Memo 4 (decided) puts the plan's zoning money into the Act's reward fund and pays places for homes finished. The Act names by-right apartments, parking, lot sizes and backyard cottages as reforms it may reward (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-207].

**A. Keep the current plan.** The Act's list.

**B. Widen the list (recommended).** Places and states also earn credit in the reward for:
- allowing single-stair apartment buildings up to six stories [EV-HOU-580];
- treating manufactured homes like other homes wherever houses are allowed. Zoning, not building codes, still keeps them out: where zoning was a real barrier, the chance a manufactured home was placed fell from 77.5% to 53.9% (Mandelker, *The Urban Lawyer*, 2016) [EV-HOU-578];
- small lots, as Texas now requires in its large cities (Texas S.B. 15, 2025) [EV-HOU-596];
- ending parking minimums. After Buffalo did, mixed-use projects built 53% fewer spaces (Hess and Rehler, 2021) [EV-HOU-592];
- exempting zoning-consistent infill from state environmental review, as California did in 2025 (Bay Area regional agencies' summary, 2025) [EV-HOU-590];
- condo defect-liability reform, as Colorado did in 2025: narrower lawsuits in exchange for a builder's warranty and independent inspection (Colorado General Assembly, 2025) [EV-HOU-598].
- *For people:* a small city that allows six-story single-stair buildings and drops parking rules near its main street wins a larger award when the homes are finished.
- *Cost or risk:* no new money. Because the reward pays for homes finished, a place that adopts a reform but builds nothing gets nothing. The plan's transportation condition (HOU-6.02) uses the reward's list, so these reforms would also count there. Each reform's own evidence is mostly capacity or cost estimates from advocates, not measured homes.
- *Burden:* none for anyone who does not apply. Applicants report which reforms they adopted.

**C. Federal rules.** Federal law overrides local rules on manufactured homes, parking near transit or lot sizes.
- *Risk:* legally risky and politically hard (judgment). Congress just chose rewards over mandates; the Act overrides no local zoning [EV-HOU-206]. It would also need many federal rules and someone to enforce them.

**Recommendation: B.** It extends a reward you already chose, adds no money and no rule, and covers the cost drivers the evidence names. Which reforms make the list is judgment.

## Part 2: financing manufactured homes

42% of loans to buy manufactured homes are "chattel" loans, secured by the home but not the land, like a car loan. Their median rate was 8.6%, against 4.9% for manufactured-home mortgages; only 27% of applications were financed, against 74% for site-built homes; and 17% of buyers who own their land still took a chattel loan (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2021) [EV-HOU-576]. These homes are 13% of homes in rural areas and small towns, and their owners are older and lower-income (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2021) [EV-HOU-577].

**A. Keep the current plan.** Rely on the Act's chassis rule and higher FHA limits [EV-HOU-219] [EV-HOU-220].

**B. Cheaper loans for manufactured homes (recommended).**
- Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy chattel loans under their existing duty to serve this market, with consumer protections like those on mortgages, so chattel rates come down.
- A manufactured home on land the owner holds can be titled as real property, so it gets a mortgage. States that allow it earn credit in Part 1's reward.
- Fund the Act's grants for residents and nonprofits buying their manufactured-home communities (PRICE), which the Act authorizes but does not fund (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-220].
- *For people:* a retired couple in rural Georgia who own their land pay a mortgage rate, not a car-loan rate, on a new home.
- *Cost or risk:* the community grants are small, perhaps $0.2-0.5 billion a year (judgment). Fannie and Freddie take on some new credit risk. No study shows how much rates would fall.
- *Burden:* none for buyers; a title step at the state.

**C. Leave it to the market and the states.**
- *Risk:* the rate gap has lasted for years (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2021) [EV-HOU-576].

**Recommendation: B.** The rate gap is large and well documented [EV-HOU-576], and it falls on older, rural, lower-income owners [EV-HOU-577]. The fix uses existing channels. The community grants' size is judgment. Repair loans for these homes are part 2 of the addendum.

## Part 3: federal land

Interior and HUD set up a task force in March 2025 to find federal land for housing; proposals to sell land were dropped from the 2025 tax law (Congressional Research Service, 2025) [EV-HOU-582].

**A. Keep the current plan.** The small federal builder may use federal land (memo 3, part 2); nothing else.

**B. Sell land near growing western cities at fair value, for housing (recommended).**
- The Bureau of Land Management sells land near cities that need it to local governments with a housing plan, on the model of the Southern Nevada law. In September 2026 it sold 940 acres to Las Vegas for $94 million, for up to 6,000 homes (Bureau of Land Management, 2026) [EV-HOU-585].
- Part of the proceeds pays for water and roads for the new homes; the rest goes to the Treasury.
- Surplus federal buildings keep homeless providers' first claim, which is small in practice: 122 properties in 27 years (Government Accountability Office, 2014) [EV-HOU-586].
- *For people:* a growing city like Boise or St. George can plan a new neighborhood on land the government owned but never used.
- *Cost or risk:* it earns money. How many homes is contested: about 1 million over ten years (American Enterprise Institute, 2025) [EV-HOU-583] or only about 118,000 near the 50 largest metros (Smart Growth America, 2025) [EV-HOU-584]. Both views stand; most of the gap is how densely the land is built. Almost all the land is in the interior West, where water is scarce, and none of it helps the Northeast or Texas.
- *Burden:* a local housing plan for the land.

**C. Give land below market value in exchange for affordable homes.**
- *For people:* more low-cost homes on each site.
- *Cost or risk:* the Treasury gives up the sale price. Discount sales raise fairness questions between places that get land and places that cannot (judgment).

**Recommendation: B.** It costs nothing, the Las Vegas sale shows it works where land is near a city [EV-HOU-585], and Part 1's reward and the plan's construction money can add affordability on the land without giving it away. The number of homes is uncertain, so the plan should not count on it (judgment).

## Part 4: environmental review for assisted infill housing

Federal environmental review applies only to housing that gets federal money. The Act lets HUD exclude more infill once it writes the rules, and only for money given after the rules (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-218].

**A. Keep the current plan.** Wait for HUD's rules.

**B. Write the exclusion into law (recommended).** Federally assisted housing on infill sites (sites with existing infrastructure, outside flood and wildfire zones, as USDA's exemption already defines them [EV-HOU-217]) is categorically excluded from federal environmental review, for every federal agency's housing money, including the plan's. Reviews that remain must finish within 90 days or are deemed complete.
- *For people:* a nonprofit building 60 homes on a vacant lot downtown starts months sooner.
- *Cost or risk:* the only cost figure is old: compliance cost about $5,000-15,000 per lot, 4.7% of a lot's price, and a HUD-sponsored study judged it "not a major factor" (Randolph and others, for HUD, 2007) [EV-HOU-589]. California's fast-track path saved projects about a year (Terner Center, 2023) [EV-HOU-591]. Environmental groups may object; flood and wildfire sites stay under review.
- *Burden:* it removes one. Projects certify the site meets the infill test.

**C. Leave federal review alone and reward states that exempt infill** (Part 1). Most housing never gets federal money, so state review matters more (judgment) [EV-HOU-590].

**Recommendation: B, together with Part 1's reward for state exemptions.** It speeds up the plan's own homes, needs no new money and keeps review where the risks are. That speed matters for the plan, which must spend its capital by 2039 (`DEC-2026-11-09-09`). The time saved is judgment; the register has no current federal figures.
