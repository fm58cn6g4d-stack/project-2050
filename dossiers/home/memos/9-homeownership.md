# Memo 9: Helping people buy a first home

Role: decision memo for Espen, part 1 of the section 3 addendum (`../3-design.md`, "Addendum: homeownership and broad supply"). Status: awaiting Espen's decision; three parts, each decided on its own. The research is in `../research-ownership-supply.md`. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Costs and counts marked "judgment" are this dossier's estimates, not official scores.

## The question

You asked on November 9 for "more robust" help for middle-class households, especially younger ones, to buy a home: a first-time buyer credit, cheaper mortgages, down payment help, and low- or zero-interest or forgivable second mortgages, plus using the plan's construction loans to make buying or building cheaper. The plan as it stands has none of this, apart from the neighborhood homes credit for homes in struggling neighborhoods (`DEC-2026-11-09-08`). This memo has **three parts. Decide each on its own.**
- **Part 1.** What help should first-time buyers get?
- **Part 2.** Should the plan make FHA mortgages cheaper?
- **Part 3.** Should the plan's federal construction loans also build homes to buy?

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** Nothing here reverses an earlier decision. Each part adds a new program or a new use of money already in the plan. The plan starts in 2029; any new help would begin in 2030.

## Why it matters

- **Young adults, Black families and lower-income households are far behind.** In the third quarter of 2026, 36.6% of householders under 35 owned their home, against 79.1% of those 65 and over; 44.4% of Black householders owned, against 75.1% of White householders (Census, 2026) [EV-HOU-563].
- **Prices have run far ahead of incomes.** A median-income household needs 43.9% of its income to own the median home (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, 2026) [EV-HOU-564]. Only 32% of households, and 16% of renters, earn the $120,800 the median home needs (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2026) [EV-HOU-565].
- **Family wealth decides who can put money down.** White families' median wealth is nine times Black families' (Federal Reserve, 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances) [EV-HOU-533]. 65% of Black renters, and 21% of White renters, have parents who never owned a home (Urban Institute, 2021) [EV-HOU-539].

## The price problem, in short

**Help for buyers can simply raise prices where homes are scarce.** A flat home-purchase grant in Bavaria raised prices by about the full amount of the grant (Krolage, 2023) [EV-HOU-546]. England's Help to Buy raised prices in London, where little could be built, but added homes without raising prices near the Welsh border, where builders could respond (Carozzi, Hilber and Yu, 2024) [EV-HOU-545]. Most buyer help also goes to people who would have bought anyway: the 2009-10 federal buyer credit added about 400,000 purchases, and 65-90% of its money went to buyers who would have bought regardless (Berger, Turner and Zwick, 2016; Hembre, 2018) [EV-HOU-509] [EV-HOU-510].

**What limits it:** help aimed at buyers short of cash rather than everyone, modest amounts, a time limit with a review, and pairing with homes that actually get built. Lowering a cost the government sets (an insurance premium) instead of handing out cash showed no price effect in the best study (Bhutta and Ringo, Federal Reserve, 2021) [EV-HOU-550]. The recommendations below follow those rules.

## Part 1: help for first-time buyers

**A. Keep the current plan.** No new buyer help. Buyers keep what exists: state agencies' down payment programs, HOME's buyer help, which the ROAD to Housing Act now lets reach families up to 100% of area median income (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-226], and the plan's neighborhood homes credit.
- *For people:* nothing new for a young couple renting in a costly city.
- *Cost or risk:* none added. It leaves your concern unanswered, and the plan's reach into the middle income groups stays thin.
- *Burden:* none.

**B. A broad first-time buyer tax credit.** On the 2025 bill's design (H.R. 4717): 10% of the price up to $15,000, phased out from 150% of area median income and for homes above 110% of the area's median price, paid to the lender at closing so the buyer has the cash when it counts, and repaid in part if the buyer sells within four years (bill text, 2025) [EV-HOU-524].
- *For people:* a 30-year-old nurse buying a $300,000 condo gets $15,000 at closing.
- *Cost or risk:* the most expensive option, about $160 billion over 2030-39 ($100-220 billion; judgment), reaching about 1.2 million buyers a year. Most would have bought anyway: perhaps 0.7-2.1 million buyers over the decade would not otherwise have bought, about $76,000-229,000 each (judgment, from Berger, Turner and Zwick and from Hembre, counting only purchases that are not just made earlier) [EV-HOU-509] [EV-HOU-510]. It does the most to raise prices where homes are scarce [EV-HOU-545] [EV-HOU-546]. The 2009-10 credit drew more than $513 million of likely wrong refunds, and clawing money back after a sale proved hard (Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration, 2011) [EV-HOU-504] [EV-HOU-507].
- *Burden:* a lender's check of income, price and first-time status at closing, then a four-year recapture through the tax system.

**C. Targeted down payment help through state housing agencies (recommended).** A federal program for 2030-39, run through the state housing finance agencies that already give down payment help to about three-quarters of their borrowers (National Council of State Housing Agencies, 2026) [EV-HOU-536].
- **Who:** first-time buyers up to 120% of area median income (140% in high-cost areas), with priority for first-generation buyers, as in the Downpayment Toward Equity Act (bill text, 2025) [EV-HOU-525].
- **How much:** up to $20,000 or 10% of the price, whichever is greater [EV-HOU-525]. The help counts for more where it buys a newly built home or a home in a place that earns the zoning reward (memo 4), so the money pulls building rather than bidding up existing homes. That pairing is judgment; no study has tested it.
- **Form:** each state chooses: a grant, a zero-interest second mortgage forgiven over five years, or a deferred loan repaid at sale, including a shared-appreciation loan like California's (California Housing Finance Agency, 2024) [EV-HOU-540].
- **One plain test:** first-generation status by the buyer's own signed statement, with no further papers, as the bill writes it (bill text, 2025) [EV-HOU-526]. Income is checked from the mortgage file the lender already has.
- **A review, not a cap:** HUD publishes each year who was helped, by income, age and race, and prices in the places using the most help; a big price rise triggers a review of the amounts.
- *For people:* a 28-year-old whose parents rented all their lives gets $20,000 toward a down payment on a new starter home, through the state agency that also gives her counseling.
- *Cost or risk:* about $30 billion over 2030-39 ($20-50 billion), helping about 1.5 million buyers. Perhaps 0.19-0.28 million of them would not otherwise have bought in the decade, about $107,000-160,000 each (judgment, on the same basis as B). That assumes the targeting draws in more buyers than broad programs do, which no U.S. study has measured, and that about half of the extra purchases only come earlier, as in a Tulsa trial (England's Help to Buy evaluation, 2018; Grinstein-Weiss and others, 2013) [EV-HOU-544] [EV-HOU-547]. Defaults: the evidence conflicts. Help alone has been tied to more FHA claims in one study (Government Accountability Office, 2005) [EV-HOU-527], but state agency borrowers, who get help plus counseling and the agency's own servicing, default about 30% less than similar borrowers (Hembre, Moulton and Record, 2021) [EV-HOU-537]. Some help still goes to buyers who would have bought anyway, and some leaks into prices.
- *Burden:* light for buyers: a signed statement and the usual mortgage file. States must run it, which most already do.
- *Fiscal rule:* written for ten years, it is time-limited and can borrow inside the plan's envelope rather than adding to the FY2040 gap (`../6-cost.md`).

**D. Shared-appreciation loans only.** A federal match for state funds that lend up to 20% of the price with no payments, repaid at sale with a share of the gain, as California's Dream For All does [EV-HOU-540].
- *For people:* a bigger boost per buyer in costly places.
- *Cost or risk:* California's loans average about $113,000-116,000, so $10 billion reaches only about 90,000 buyers (California Housing Finance Agency, 2024 and 2026) [EV-HOU-540] [EV-HOU-541]. Its first-generation round drew more than 18,000 eligible applicants for about 2,000 awards [EV-HOU-542]. The agency itself warns that large help risks "perpetuating market distortions" [EV-HOU-543]. Under C, states can still choose this form.
- *Burden:* an appraisal and a payoff calculation at sale; more complex for buyers to understand.

**Recommendation: C.** It rests on three findings:
1. Broad, large buyer help is the kind that raises prices where homes are scarce (Krolage, 2023; Carozzi, Hilber and Yu, 2024) [EV-HOU-546] [EV-HOU-545].
2. Help works best delivered with counseling and sound loans by the state agencies (Hembre, Moulton and Record, 2021) [EV-HOU-537].
3. A first-generation priority reaches the families furthest behind: California's first-generation round reached more than twice the share of Black buyers its open round did (California Housing Finance Agency, 2024 and 2026) [EV-HOU-540] [EV-HOU-541].

The amount, the ten-year term and the pairing with new homes are judgment. Per lasting added owner, B and C cost about the same; the ranges overlap. C wins on total cost (about a fifth of B's), on reaching the families furthest behind, and on less risk of raising prices. B reaches more middle-income buyers, which the plan's fairness test values. If you want a tax credit anyway, the advanceable design with income and price caps (B) is the one to use.

## Part 2: cheaper FHA mortgages

FHA insures about 650,000 purchase loans a year (this dossier's arithmetic); 83% go to first-time buyers, 20% to Hispanic and 13% to Black borrowers (HUD, FHA annual report, 2025) [EV-HOU-552]. Its yearly insurance premium is 0.55% of the loan since a 2023 cut (HUD, 2023) [EV-HOU-551].

**A. Keep the current plan.** Leave the premium to HUD, as now.
- *Cost or risk:* none. Premiums can move either way under any administration.

**B. Cut the annual premium for purchase loans by a quarter point, to 0.30%, from 2030 (recommended), while the insurance fund stays above a stated safety line.**
- *For people:* about $700 a year less on a $280,000 loan (this dossier's arithmetic).
- *Evidence:* the 2015 cut of half a point raised purchases by FHA-reliant buyers by nearly 14%, mostly among lower-income buyers, with no sign of higher prices within a year (Bhutta and Ringo, Federal Reserve, 2021) [EV-HOU-550]. The fund holds 11.47% of capital against a 2% legal floor, and 4.42% in a severe stress test (HUD, FHA annual report, 2025) [EV-HOU-552], so it has room.
- *Cost or risk:* about $15-25 billion of forgone premiums over 2030-39, for about 0.15-0.23 million lasting added owners, about $65,000-167,000 each, the same basis as Part 1 (judgment); CBO would score it. The 2023 cut has not yet been evaluated. If the fund's stress result falls near the floor, HUD may restore part of the cut, on a published review.
- *Burden:* none for buyers.

**C. B, plus ending early the 0.10% fee the Treasury takes from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac loans, for first-time buyers.**
- *Evidence:* ten basis points of every Fannie and Freddie fee goes to the Treasury until 2032 under a 2011 law (Federal Housing Finance Agency, 2025) [EV-HOU-554]. The register has no study of how much of a fee cut reaches borrowers' rates.
- *Cost or risk:* a few hundred million dollars a year for the years it would otherwise run (judgment, from Congressional Budget Office, 2024) [EV-HOU-556]. The fee ends in 2032 anyway.

**D. A federal mortgage-rate buydown or a public mortgage lender.**
- *Risk:* no evaluated U.S. model. One study argues builders' buydowns hold new-home prices up (American Enterprise Institute, 2025) [EV-HOU-570]; that is contested. A public lender would compete with FHA and the two companies, which already set most terms. This dossier does not recommend it.

**Recommendation: B.** It is the best-evidenced lever in this memo [EV-HOU-550], reaches younger, lower-income, Hispanic and Black buyers most [EV-HOU-552], adds no paperwork and showed no price effect. The size of the cut and the safety line are judgment. C adds little for the years left on the fee.

## Part 3: the plan's construction loans for homes to buy

The plan's $300 billion of federal loans (HOU-3C.02) finance rental homes. Builders of small homes for sale struggle to borrow: construction loans for one-to-four-family homes stand 56% below their 2008 peak (FDIC data, tabulated by the National Association of Home Builders, 2026) [EV-HOU-568], and banks tightened again in 2026 (Federal Reserve, 2026) [EV-HOU-567].

**A. Keep the current plan.** Loans stay rental-only. Homes for sale come from the neighborhood homes credit (HOU-2D.03).

**B. Let up to $30 billion of the loans finance starter homes and condos for sale (recommended).** Short construction loans, and construction-to-permanent loans that become the buyer's mortgage, through state agencies and community lenders, for small, nonprofit and other builders. Homes are sold to people who will live in them, at or under FHA's loan limit for the area. No new money: it is part of the $300 billion.
- *For people:* a small builder in Ohio gets a loan to build 12 starter homes that young families buy with Part 1's help.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.5-1.5 billion of subsidy cost inside HOU-3C's amounts; about 250,000 homes financed (150,000-350,000) as loans revolve, perhaps 75,000-175,000 of which would not otherwise be built (judgment). The register has no evaluation of federal construction lending to small builders. The loans for rentals shrink by the same amount, so the plan's direct count shifts from rentals toward homes for sale.
- *Burden:* builders certify sale to an owner-occupant at or under the price limit; no buyer test.

**C. B, plus permanently affordable homes to buy.** Part of the state revolving-fund grants (HOU-3C.01) may also fund homes sold below market with a resale limit, through land trusts and similar owners, so the discount stays with the home for the next buyer.
- *Evidence:* the subsidy cannot leak into the next sale. Shared-equity owners rarely lost homes to foreclosure, and over 90% still owned after five years (Urban Institute, 2010) [EV-HOU-548]; that study had no comparison group and was funded by advocates.
- *Cost or risk:* no new money; it competes with grants for the poorest renters, so it would sit outside the 30% share reserved for them. Owners build less wealth than on the open market (Urban Institute, 2010) [EV-HOU-548].
- *Burden:* a recorded resale limit; a price check at each sale.

**Recommendation: C.** It uses money already in the plan to pair buyer help (Part 1) with homes that get built, which is the strongest guard against higher prices (Carozzi, Hilber and Yu, 2024) [EV-HOU-545]. The resale-limited homes keep the public's investment working for later buyers. The $30 billion ceiling and the price limit are judgment; B alone is the simpler choice if you want ownership to stay market-rate.

## If you decide

Part 1 C adds a new program, "Down payment help through state housing agencies, 2030-39" (a new agenda row). Part 2 B adds an FHA premium cut. Part 3 opens HOU-3C.02, and under C HOU-3C.01, to homes for sale. Section 4 writes them, section 6 prices them with the budget model and the incidence model checks their reach into the middle income groups. Repair loans and help for older owners (FHA's 203(k), the ROAD to Housing Act's repair pilot) are part 2 of the addendum.
