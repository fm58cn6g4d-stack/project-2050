# Memo 11: Repairing and renovating homes people already have

Role: decision memo for Espen, part 2 of the section 3 addendum (`../3-design.md`, "Addendum, part 2: repair and renovation; aging in place"). Status: decided by Espen (November 9, 2026; `DEC-2026-11-09-21`): part 1 C, part 2 B, part 3 B, with Espen open to part 3 C (merging repair and Weatherization) if the clean-power dossier finds it workable or preferable. Section 4 writes them as HOU-12.01 to .03. The research is in `../research-repair-aging.md`, sections 1 to 5. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Costs and counts marked "judgment" are this dossier's estimates, not official scores.

## The question

You asked on November 9 for ways to make sure homeowners and renters can affordably repair or renovate their homes, and noted that this crosses with the clean-power agenda. The plan today repairs public housing (HOU-5) and lets HOU-3C's money buy and fix rental buildings (memo 3, part 3), but has nothing for the private homes most low-income people live in. This memo has **three parts. Decide each on its own.**
- **Part 1.** Should the plan pay for repairs to the homes of low- and moderate-income owners?
- **Part 2.** Should it help renters get repairs, without pushing their rents up?
- **Part 3.** How should repair money work with the clean-power plan's heat pumps and weatherization?

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** Nothing here reverses an earlier decision. The clean-power agenda (a separate dossier, not yet weighed) carries heat-pump retrofits for about 250,000 low-income homes a year from 2031 (CLI-5W, $67.5 billion in 2025 dollars over 2031-39), a larger LIHEAP and Weatherization program (CLI-5) and restored home energy credits (CLI-1A) (`legacy/domains/domain-climate.md`). This memo does not decide those; Part 3 decides how housing's repair money meets them.

## Why it matters

- **Many homes need repairs their owners cannot pay for.** Needed repairs to occupied homes cost about $198.4 billion in 2024; households under 200 percent of poverty live in 29.0 percent of homes but carry 37.6 percent of the cost, $74.6 billion. 5.4 million households faced repairs of $10,000 or more (Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia, 2025) [EV-HOU-602].
- **Old homes, low incomes.** The poorest fifth of owners in pre-1960 homes spent about $3,400 a year on repairs and improvements, against $12,700 for the richest (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies) [EV-HOU-601].
- **Bad homes make people sick.** About 3.3 million homes with children under six have lead paint hazards, more than 2 million of them low-income (HUD, American Healthy Homes Survey II) [EV-HOU-603]. Home asthma programs that remove triggers cut children's symptom days and missed school days (Community Preventive Services Task Force) [EV-HOU-606].
- **Repairs block energy work.** About one in five low-income homes seeking weatherization is turned away first because the roof leaks, the floor is damaged or the electric panel is unsafe, and about 40 percent of those are never served (American Council for an Energy-Efficient Economy, 2025) [EV-HOU-639].
- **Federal repair help is small and hard to use.** HOME finished 2,488 owner repairs in fiscal 2024, at about $57,724 each, because a HOME repair must bring the whole house up to code (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-609] [EV-HOU-610]. USDA's rural repair grants top out at $10,000 for owners 62 and over [EV-HOU-616]. The ROAD to Housing Act created a whole-home repair pilot that runs only if Congress appropriates money and ends in October 2031 (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-230].

## What works, in short

**Simple emergency repairs reach many homes cheaply and help neighborhoods.** Philadelphia's Basic Systems Repair Program fixes roofs, heat, wiring and plumbing for low-income owners. From 2009 to 2019 it made 11,087 repairs at about $8,900 each (Green, Reina and others, 2022, preprint) [EV-HOU-647], and in 2025 it repaired 3,527 homes (City of Philadelphia, 2025) [EV-HOU-649]. Each repaired home on a block was linked to about 22 percent less crime, comparing repaired blocks with blocks still on the wait list (South, MacDonald and Reina, *JAMA Network Open*, 2021) [EV-HOU-646].

**Heavy rules starve a program.** Pennsylvania's Whole-Home Repairs program drew 25,715 applications for $125 million but had repaired 1,151 homes by mid-2024, with grants up to $50,000 per home (Demos, 2024, on state figures) [EV-HOU-656]. Its landlord loans carried a 15-year rent limit and renter income paperwork; counties declined to run them, and one landlord loan had been identified statewide [EV-HOU-655] [EV-HOU-657]. Detroit's no-interest repair loans approved 37 percent of applicants, 22 percent of the poorest, because of credit-score and tax tests (University of Michigan Poverty Solutions, 2020) [EV-HOU-661].

## Part 1: repairs for owners

**A. Keep the current plan.** Existing programs continue: HOME and CDBG, which the 2026 and 2027 budgets proposed ending and Congress has so far kept (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-611] [EV-HOU-612]; USDA's repair loans and grants; lead hazard grants; the clean-power plan's heat pumps. The ROAD Act pilot stays unfunded.
- *For people:* a retired couple in Cleveland with a failing roof joins a city wait list that can run two to three years, as Philadelphia's has [EV-HOU-645].
- *Cost or risk:* none added. The need far exceeds the help.
- *Burden:* none new.

**B. Fund the ROAD Act pilot as written, to 2031.** Appropriate the whole-home repair pilot at, say, $1-2 billion a year for 2030-31 [EV-HOU-230].
- *For people:* the same couple might get help in a funded city, for two years.
- *Cost or risk:* $2-4 billion (judgment). It is a pilot that ends in 2031, so states would build programs and then close them, as Pennsylvania's did after one year of money [EV-HOU-659].
- *Burden:* the pilot's own rules, set by HUD.

**C. A standing home repair fund through states, cities and tribes (recommended).**
- **Who:** owner-occupants at or below 80 percent of area median income get grants; owners from 80 to 120 percent get zero-interest loans repaid only at sale. Heirs living in the home count as owners (the ROAD Act's pilot already allows this) [EV-HOU-676].
- **What:** health and safety and basic systems first: roofs, heat, wiring, plumbing, lead, mold and asthma triggers, accessibility (memo 12, Part 2), and the repairs that let a home take a heat pump or weatherization (Part 3). Grants up to about $25,000 a home, more where lead or accessibility work is added; the work fixes what is wrong, not the whole house to code.
- **How:** money by formula to the states, cities and tribes that already run CDBG and HOME, with a tribal share as in HOU-4.13. Local programs choose their contractors and order of need.
- **One plain test:** income by the owner's signed statement, or automatic eligibility for anyone already on SNAP, Medicaid, SSI, LIHEAP or Weatherization. No credit score. Owners behind on property taxes are not turned away; the program refers them to memo 12's tax relief and legal help. (Philadelphia's program, by contrast, requires owners to be current on taxes and water bills [EV-HOU-648].)
- **A review, not a cap:** HUD publishes each year homes repaired, wait times and cost per home by place and income, and what share of grants went to the poorest owners.
- *For people:* the Cleveland couple gets a new roof and furnace within months, through the same city office that runs CDBG.
- *Cost or risk:* about $2.5-5.5 billion a year, $25-55 billion over 2030-39, repairing roughly 200,000-300,000 owner homes a year at $12,000-18,000 each with administration (judgment, from Philadelphia's and Detroit's costs [EV-HOU-647] [EV-HOU-660]). That covers perhaps a third to two-thirds of the lower-income repair need over ten years (judgment, against [EV-HOU-602]). Written for ten years, it is time-limited capital, like HOU-3 and HOU-5, and can borrow inside the plan's envelope (`../3-design.md`, "How the choices fit together") rather than adding to the FY2040 gap; a later Congress can extend it. Contractors are scarce in many places; Pennsylvania's counties struggled to find them [EV-HOU-655]. The crime and neighborhood gains come from one city's study [EV-HOU-646].
- *Burden:* light for owners: a signed statement and a home visit. Prevailing-wage rules on federal money raise costs on large jobs; Pennsylvania counties capped grants just below their trigger [EV-HOU-657]. Repairs to owner-occupied homes of one to a few units should not trigger it, as CDBG's wage rule already spares small residential buildings (judgment; section 4 would check the statute).

**D. A broad federal tax credit for home repairs.**
- *For people:* any owner who pays for a new roof gets back a share at tax time.
- *Cost or risk:* the most expensive per home helped, and it pays mostly those who would have repaired anyway. The home energy credits went about 60 percent to the richest fifth of households and about 10 percent to the bottom three fifths (Borenstein and Davis, 2024) [EV-HOU-628]. Owners with no cash up front cannot use it.
- *Burden:* receipts and a tax filing.

**Recommendation: C.** Simple, standing emergency repair programs reach many low-income owners at modest cost [EV-HOU-647] [EV-HOU-649], with evidence of wider benefits [EV-HOU-646]; one-time and rule-heavy programs reach few [EV-HOU-656] [EV-HOU-661]; and tax credits miss the owners who need help most [EV-HOU-628]. The amounts and the ten-year term are judgment.

## Part 2: repairs for renters, without rent jumps

**A. Keep the current plan.** Renters rely on local code enforcement. HOU-3C can buy and repair rental buildings for public and nonprofit owners (memo 3, part 3), but small private landlords get nothing.
- *Cost or risk:* none added.

**B. Help small landlords and cities fix rental homes, with a light rent term (recommended).**
- **Landlord loans.** Part 1's fund may lend to owners of up to about 20 rental units, up to about $25,000 a unit, forgiven after three years if annual rent increases stay at or below 5 percent or inflation, whichever is lower: the ROAD Act pilot's own term [EV-HOU-230]. The landlord signs a statement. No tenant income checks: Pennsylvania's renter paperwork and 15-year limit were why its landlord loans went almost unused [EV-HOU-655] [EV-HOU-657].
- **Subsidized work never raises rent.** The cost of work paid for with public money cannot be passed on, as New York now requires for building upgrades [EV-HOU-665] and the federal energy rebates require for two years [EV-HOU-632].
- **City repair funds and receivership.** Grants to cities to repair hazards a landlord will not fix and bill the owner, recovered through a lien, as New York City does (it has billed $108 million and collected $88 million) [EV-HOU-663]; and revolving loans to court-appointed receivers for neglected buildings, as Massachusetts does [EV-HOU-669].
- **Relocation help** when a city orders tenants out of an unsafe home. In San Antonio only 4 percent of households given vacate orders got it [EV-HOU-667].
- *For people:* a tenant in a duplex with no heat gets it fixed in days when the city's repair crew steps in, and the landlord cannot raise the rent to cover the bill.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.5-1 billion a year inside Part 1's fund (judgment). Enforced repairs can still raise rents: in Chicago, fixing code violations was linked to higher rents (Bartram, 2019) [EV-HOU-668]. The three-year term limits that only for loan-funded homes. Small-landlord programs have had low uptake [EV-HOU-652].
- *Burden:* one signed statement for landlords; none for tenants.

**C. Long affordability terms on every landlord dollar.** As Pennsylvania did: 15 years of capped rents, renter income checks and an ownership requirement [EV-HOU-653].
- *For people:* longer protection for the few tenants whose landlord takes the loan.
- *Cost or risk:* the evidence says almost no one takes it [EV-HOU-656].
- *Burden:* heavy for landlords and the counties that monitor them.

**D. Require proactive rental inspection as a condition of federal money.**
- *For people:* every rental inspected on a schedule, as Los Angeles does about every four years, paid by a per-unit fee partly passed to tenants [EV-HOU-666].
- *Cost or risk:* a federal mandate on local code offices; inspections without repair money can close homes and displace tenants [EV-HOU-667] [EV-HOU-668].
- *Burden:* new inspections and fees; it polices rather than guides.

**Recommendation: B.** It reaches renters through the landlords and cities that can fix homes, with the lightest rent term the evidence suggests landlords will accept, and it pairs enforcement with money and relocation help so that repairs do not become evictions. The loan size, the 20-unit line and the cost are judgment.

## Part 3: repair and clean power

The clean-power agenda's heat pumps and weatherization cannot serve homes with leaking roofs or old wiring: about one in five low-income homes is deferred, and 40 percent of those are never served [EV-HOU-639]. Panel and service upgrades alone can cost $2,000-30,000 (U.S. Department of Energy, 2023, on national laboratory estimates) [EV-HOU-640]. Weatherization's own repair money is small: Congress added a readiness fund in 2022 [EV-HOU-622], and ACEEE estimates about $94 million a year would fix nearly all the homes the program defers [EV-HOU-623].

**A. Keep them separate.** Housing's repair fund (Part 1) and clean power's programs run on their own.
- *For people:* an owner may apply three times, to three offices, and be turned away by one for a roof the other could fix.
- *Cost or risk:* none added; deferred homes stay deferred.

**B. One front door; the repair fund pays for the readiness repairs (recommended).**
- A household applies once. The local program assesses the home once, for repairs, lead, accessibility and energy. Part 1's fund pays for the repairs that make a home ready; Weatherization and the clean-power retrofits pay for insulation, sealing and heat pumps.
- Anyone eligible for one is eligible for all, on the same signed income statement or benefit record.
- Where a state runs Weatherization and housing repair through different agencies, the money can flow to whichever agency the state names.
- *For people:* a widower in rural Georgia gets his roof patched and his panel replaced by the repair fund, then a heat pump the same season from the clean-power program, after one visit.
- *Cost or risk:* readiness repairs for the clean-power program's homes would take roughly $0.25-0.75 billion a year of Part 1's fund (judgment: about one in five of 250,000 homes a year at $5,000-15,000 each). That makes clean power's money go further. Coordination between two agencies is harder than it sounds.
- *Burden:* it removes burden: one application, one visit.

**C. Merge repair and Weatherization into one program.**
- *For people:* the same one-stop service.
- *Cost or risk:* it would rewrite a program the clean-power dossier owns, change long-standing state networks and risk Weatherization's energy rules. The clean-power dossier should decide that, not housing.
- *Burden:* a transition for the agencies.

**Recommendation: B.** It turns away fewer of the homes that need help most and makes both programs' money go further, without rewriting a program the clean-power dossier has not yet weighed. The clean-power dossier should confirm the link when it reaches CLI-5 and CLI-5W.
