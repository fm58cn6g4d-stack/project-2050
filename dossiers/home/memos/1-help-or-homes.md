# Memo 1: Help or homes

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 1 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

**This memo decides the form of the new rental help, including the million new vouchers (HOU-4A) and the $135 billion envelope (HOU-4D).** On October 30 you made both working choices rather than settled ones (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`), so keeping them as plain vouchers is now one option here, weighed like the others. The recommendation changes the form of part of HOU-4A's last two years of awards, in tight markets only; it keeps HOU-4A's count, its 2030-33 schedule and its mandatory funding.

## The question

The plan gives renters more help paying rent and builds more homes. In places where there are almost no empty apartments, a voucher can be hard to use. Should the new help work the same way everywhere, or change where homes are scarce? A "tight" market here means one with few empty apartments for rent. The plan starts in 2029; the first new help arrives in 2030.

**Current law.** The ROAD to Housing Act (2026) does not change this memo's options. It speeds up voucher inspections, including for landlords new to the program (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-232], but adds no rent help and does nothing about landlords who refuse vouchers or places with no empty homes (`../road-act.md`).

## Why it matters

A voucher pays part of the rent at a home the family finds.
- Nationally, about two in five families with a new voucher had not found a home six months later, and fewer succeed where there are few empty apartments (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, Institute for Research on Poverty, 2024) [EV-HOU-092].
- Where the law allows it, most landlords refuse vouchers: 67% to 78% of the time in three cities tested (HUD landlord study by the Urban Institute, published under the first Trump administration, 2018) [EV-HOU-093].

## The options

**A. Keep the plan as filed: vouchers everywhere.**
- All new help, the million vouchers and the $135 billion, is normal vouchers in every market.
- *For people:* families choose where to live, which helps children. Moving to a lower-poverty area before age 13 raised adult earnings by 31% (Chetty, Hendren and Katz, Moving to Opportunity study, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2015) [EV-HOU-090].
- *Evidence for it:* the trial showing that long-term rent help ends family homelessness mostly used normal vouchers (Family Options Study, Gubits and others, 2018) [EV-HOU-086], and normal vouchers seem better than building-based help at reaching people who would otherwise have no home of their own (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114].
- *Risk:* in tight markets many vouchers go unused (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, 2024) [EV-HOU-092]. Raising the voucher limit across a whole city mostly raises landlords' rents (Collinson and Ganong, 2018) [EV-HOU-095].
- *How it fits the plan:* the new homes the plan finances are not linked to the new help, so they reach the poorest renters only by chance or through the building grants.

**B. Match the tool to the market, for all new help (recommended).**
- Where empty homes are scarce, part of the new help comes attached to homes the plan builds or buys. The family gets a home and the help together. Elsewhere, families get a normal voucher.
- This covers the million vouchers too, but in practice only their 2032-33 awards, since the plan's first new homes will not be finished before about 2032 (this dossier's judgment on build times). Homes the plan buys could be ready sooner (memo 3, part 3). The 2030-31 awards stay normal vouchers everywhere.
- Families in attached homes can later move with a normal voucher.
- *For people:* for example, a mother in a tight city who now waits months for a landlord to say yes could move straight into a new building. A family in a city with plenty of empty apartments still chooses its own home.
- *Evidence for it:* tax-credit buildings reach the poorest renters mainly when rent help is attached (O'Regan and Horn, NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].
- *How it fits the plan:* the attached help is the steady rent that lets the plan's construction loans (HOU-3C) pay for homes the poorest renters can afford. That is why memo 3 can keep the loan-heavy construction money.
- *Cost or risk:* about the same money, placed differently. It needs the building program to deliver homes on time. Attached homes must not cluster in the poorest neighborhoods, since moves to lower-poverty areas are what helped children (Chetty, Hendren and Katz, 2015) [EV-HOU-090].

**B-narrow. As B, but leave the million vouchers as they are.**
- Only the $135 billion envelope and later help can be attached; the million stay normal vouchers everywhere. This was the earlier recommendation, written when the million were treated as settled.
- *Difference from B:* only in 2032-33. Under B-narrow, new homes finished in those years in tight markets have no new help to attach until 2034.

**C. Build first.**
- In tight markets, slow new vouchers until new homes exist, and move some voucher money into building.
- *For people:* families wait longer for help, sometimes years, while homes are built. Vouchers sharply cut homelessness for the families who get them (Family Options Study, 2018) [EV-HOU-086], so waiting has a cost.
- *Cost or risk:* building takes years, and many markets are not tight: rental vacancy hit a record high in early 2026 (Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, 2026) [EV-HOU-068].

## Recommendation

**B, for all new help.** It rests on three findings:
1. Vouchers work for homeless families who get them: priority access to a long-term subsidy sharply reduced family homelessness (Family Options Study, 2018) [EV-HOU-086].
2. They fail where homes are scarce and landlords refuse them (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, 2024; HUD landlord study, 2018) [EV-HOU-092] [EV-HOU-093].
3. Buildings reach the poorest renters when help is attached (NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].

Why include the million: a family in a tight city faces the same failed search whether its voucher is in the first million or the second. Plain vouchers are the right answer where homes are available, so B keeps them there; that part of the plan as filed holds up.

**What the evidence doesn't show yet.** There is no evidence in the register on how well help attached to buildings works in tight markets, and one study finds normal vouchers reach people without homes better (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114]. B rests on the evidence that normal vouchers fail in tight markets, and on judgment that attaching help to homes fixes that. So the attached share should start small and be tested. If you would rather leave the million exactly as filed, B-narrow is the cautious fallback.
