# Memo 1: Help or homes

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 1 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

## The question

The plan already gives renters more help paying rent (one million new vouchers, plus the extra $135 billion envelope) and builds more homes. In places where there are almost no empty apartments, a voucher can be hard to use. Should the new help work the same way everywhere, or change where homes are scarce? A "tight" market here means one with few empty apartments for rent.

**What you already decided, and what this would change.** The million new vouchers you decided on are not affected under any option: they stay normal vouchers. This memo is about the help beyond them: the $135 billion envelope and later stages of the guarantee.

## Why it matters

A voucher pays part of the rent at a home the family finds.
- Nationally, about two in five families with a new voucher had not found a home six months later, and fewer succeed where there are few empty apartments (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, Institute for Research on Poverty, 2024) [EV-HOU-092].
- Where the law allows it, most landlords refuse vouchers: 67% to 78% of the time in three cities tested (HUD landlord study by the Urban Institute, 2018) [EV-HOU-093].

## The options

**A. Vouchers everywhere (as now planned).**
- *For people:* families choose where to live, which helps children. Moving to a lower-poverty area before age 13 raised adult earnings by 31% (Chetty, Hendren and Katz, Moving to Opportunity study, 2016) [EV-HOU-090].
- *Risk:* in tight markets many vouchers go unused (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, 2024) [EV-HOU-092]. Raising the voucher limit across a whole city mostly raises landlords' rents (Collinson and Ganong, 2018) [EV-HOU-095].

**B. Match the tool to the market (recommended).**
- Where empty homes are scarce, part of the help beyond the million vouchers comes attached to homes the plan builds or buys. The family gets a home and the help together.
- Elsewhere, families get a normal voucher.
- Families in attached homes can later move with a normal voucher.
- *For people:* for example, a mother in a tight city who now waits months for a landlord to say yes could move straight into a new building. A family in a city with plenty of empty apartments still chooses its own home.
- *Evidence for it:* tax-credit buildings reach the poorest renters mainly when rent help is attached (O'Regan and Horn, NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].
- *Cost or risk:* about the same money, placed differently. It needs the building program to deliver homes on time. Attached homes must not cluster in the poorest neighborhoods, since moves to lower-poverty areas are what helped children (Chetty, Hendren and Katz, 2016) [EV-HOU-090]. That part rests on judgment: the register has no direct evidence on attached help in tight markets.

**C. Build first.**
- In tight markets, slow new vouchers until new homes exist, and move some voucher money into building.
- *For people:* families wait longer for help, sometimes years, while homes are built. Vouchers sharply cut homelessness for the families who get them (Family Options Study, Gubits and others, 2018) [EV-HOU-086], so waiting has a cost.
- *Cost or risk:* building takes years, and many markets are not tight: rental vacancy hit a record high in early 2026 (Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, 2026) [EV-HOU-068].

## Recommendation

**B.** It rests on three findings:
1. Vouchers work for the families who can use them: priority access to a long-term subsidy sharply reduced family homelessness (Family Options Study, 2018) [EV-HOU-086].
2. They fail where homes are scarce and landlords refuse them (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, 2024; HUD landlord study, 2018) [EV-HOU-092] [EV-HOU-093].
3. Buildings reach the poorest renters when help is attached (NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].

**What the evidence doesn't show yet.** There is no evidence in the register yet on how well help attached to buildings works in tight markets. B rests on the evidence that normal vouchers fail there, and on judgment that attaching help to homes fixes that.
