# Memo 13: Supercharging USDA's rural housing programs

Role: decision memo for Espen, after the section 3 addendum (memos 9-12), on USDA's rural housing programs (board row 4j). Status: proposed, waiting on Espen's decisions; nothing here is folded into sections 4-9 until Espen decides. The research is the cross-cutting note `../../foundations/rural-delivery.md` (called "the rural delivery note" below; its sources are SRC-2100 to SRC-2178), plus the statute text saved for this memo (SRC-2300). Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Costs and counts marked "judgment" are this dossier's estimates, not official scores. Dates are from 2029 on (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`).

## The question

You wrote on November 9 that USDA Rural Development and the Rural Housing Service are "some of the most effective direct govt programs the fed govt has", trusted "because they are staffed and run locally", and that you'd "love to just supercharge them". You asked that this memo weigh **bigger programs and broader eligibility**: "not just buyers over 62, etc". It has **six parts. Decide each on its own.**
- **Part 1.** Rural Development's own staff, offices and systems: the channel everything else depends on.
- **Part 2.** Helping rural families buy a home (Section 502), as the rural route for memo 9's buyer help.
- **Part 3.** Repairs (Section 504), as the rural front door for memo 11's repair fund.
- **Part 4.** Rural rental homes (Sections 515 and 521): maturing mortgages and rent help.
- **Part 5.** Self-help housing (Section 523) and farmworker housing (Sections 514 and 516).
- **Part 6.** Which places count as rural.

There is also a short list of things USDA can do on the first day of 2029 without Congress ("Day one"), for a yes or no.

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** Nothing here reverses an earlier decision. The repair fund stays HOU-12.01, about $38 billion over FY2030-39, with grants at or below 80% of area median income (`DEC-2026-11-09-21`, `-25`); the down payment help stays HOU-10.01, up to 140% of area median income or 160% in high-cost areas (`DEC-2026-11-09-19`, `-24`). Part 3 gives the repair fund a rural door; Part 2 makes USDA's loans work with the down payment help. The FY2040 gap still goes to the financing settlement and housing is not trimmed (`DEC-2026-11-09-09`). The ROAD to Housing Act's funding rules and the FY2029 bridge (`road-funding.md`; Espen said yes to all four, recorded in PR #175 once Espen labels it) are not reopened; the 2027-28 asks for USDA programs stay in that map.

## Why it matters

- **USDA reaches rural families other channels miss, but it is small and shrinking.** Its direct home loans peaked at nearly 133,000 in FY1976 (Congressional Research Service) [EV-HOU-778]; in FY2025 it made 2,756 [EV-HOU-771], and in FY2026 2,115 [EV-HOU-700]. Its repair grants reached about 3,000 owners in 2025, for nearly $26 million (USDA) [EV-HOU-772] (count: rural delivery note, section 1.3).
- **The staff that makes it local is a third smaller than a year ago.** USDA's own budget notes say Rural Development "is at historically low staffing levels compared to its loan and grant portfolio": 4,455 full-time staff in FY2025, 3,058 in FY2026, with a plan to keep the lower level and end some lending work [EV-HOU-777]. Money is going unspent: on the last day of FY2026, House members warned that about $232 million of the $1 billion for direct home loans could expire [EV-HOU-780]; advocates later counted about $180 million that did (rural delivery note, "In brief", item 2). USDA lent $571 million [EV-HOU-700].
- **The rural rental stock is about to drain away.** USDA's own data, as GAO reported, suggest over 90% of its rural rental properties, about 13,000 properties with 407,000 apartments, could leave the program between 2028 and 2050; GAO found the estimating tool lacked controls, so the figure is rough [EV-HOU-783]. What is measured: about 60% of properties whose mortgages matured in 2014-17 left (GAO) [EV-HOU-785]. Rent help for the 212,630 apartments that get it goes to tenants whose average income is $14,342 (USDA) [EV-HOU-773].
- **The rules shut many people out.** Repair grants go only to owners 62 and over with very low incomes, $10,000 in a lifetime [EV-HOU-615] [EV-HOU-616]. In February 2026 USDA cut the direct loan limit from 80% to 60% of FHA's area limits and added new approval layers (House members' letter) [EV-HOU-779].

## What the evidence says, in short

**The records are strong; the causal evidence is thin.** USDA's loan and grant counts are good records. No study measures what Section 502, 504 or 523 add compared with no program; states with more Section 502 loans have more Black and Hispanic owners, a correlation (Petach, 2024) [EV-HOU-702]. Nearly a fifth of direct loans were behind on payments in March 2025 (ProPublica and the Bangor Daily News) [EV-HOU-701]. Local trust is plausible but has never been measured for Rural Development itself (rural delivery note, section 2.3). So this memo recommends scaling what has take-up and simple rules, with published speed and results by state so your view that local delivery works better can be checked.

**Scaling is mainly a staffing problem, not a money problem.** Several USDA loans cost the government little: guaranteed home loans earn more in fees than they lose, and the direct loans cost about 13 cents per dollar lent (OMB's FY2026 subsidy rates; rural delivery note, section 1.2). What binds is people to take applications, a single-family loan system that "is a commercial mainframe product" (USDA Inspector General) [EV-HOU-782], and the administration's plan to keep staff at 2026's level [EV-HOU-777].

**Much of the narrowness is USDA's own rule, not the law.** The statute lets USDA make repair grants to any "eligible very low-income applicant" and sets no age limit [EV-HOU-760]; the age-62 rule is in USDA's regulation [EV-HOU-761]. The 60% loan limit, the packaging-fee cap and the new approval layers are handbook changes [EV-HOU-779]. A 2029 administration can undo these without Congress (see "Day one").

## Part 1: Rural Development's staff, offices and systems

Every other part needs this one: in FY2025-26 Congress provided money that the agency, short of staff, did not use (rural delivery note, section 3.1) [EV-HOU-780].

**A. Keep the current plan.** No housing money for USDA's staff; its salaries stay in regular Agriculture appropriations.
- *For people:* a family in rural Mississippi waits months for a loan officer, or finds the county office closed.
- *Cost or risk:* none added. Parts 2-5 would mostly go unspent.
- *Burden:* none new, but long waits are themselves the burden.

**B. Rebuild the field staff to about 5,000 and keep decisions local (recommended).**
- **Staff.** About 2,000 more people over FY2030-32, back to about FY2012's level, mostly housing loan and repair specialists in state and area offices, plus engineers and inspectors (judgment, from the rural delivery note, section 4.1).
- **Keep lending local.** Applications are taken and decided in state and area offices, reversing the June 2026 plan to centralize origination (rural delivery note, section 1.1). Central offices keep servicing support and systems.
- **One modern loan system** with online applications, replacing the mainframe [EV-HOU-782], built in stages and starting with single-family housing, the busiest line.
- **Nonprofit packagers paid fairly.** Local nonprofits that help families assemble applications get a packaging fee that covers their cost, reversing the 2026 cut [EV-HOU-779].
- **A review, not a rule.** USDA publishes each quarter, by state, how long decisions take and how satisfied applicants are. The ROAD to Housing Act already asks for 90-day decisions on Sections 502 and 504 and yearly timeliness reports [EV-HOU-763]; a state office that runs past 90 days for two quarters gets a published review and more staff, not a penalty.
- *For people:* the same family finds an open office with a loan officer who knows the county, and an answer within 90 days.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.4 billion a year once staffed: about $0.3 billion for 2,000 positions at about $150,000 each with benefits and offices, and about $0.1-0.2 billion a year for five years for the new system (judgment). About $3.5 billion over FY2030-39 ($2.5-5 billion). If written as a ten-year R2 appropriation, like the Inflation Reduction Act's money through USDA's utilities service (rural delivery note, section 1.4), it is time-limited; keeping the staff after FY2039 would add about $0.3-0.4 billion a year to FY2040, which goes to the financing settlement with the rest of housing's gap (`DEC-2026-11-09-09`). Hiring takes two to three years; system replacements often stall.
- *Burden:* none for applicants; it removes waits.

**C. B, at the pre-2012 level of about 6,000.** About 3,000 more people.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.5-0.6 billion a year, about $5 billion over FY2030-39 (judgment). Faster scale-up of Parts 2-5, but harder to hire well in three years.

**D. Route rural housing through HUD's grantees instead.** Rural buyers and owners use HOU-10.01 (state housing finance agencies) and HOU-12.01 (state and local CDBG and HOME grantees); USDA programs stay as they are.
- *For people:* families in places with a capable county or state program are served; in places without one, they are not.
- *Cost or risk:* no new money. It gives up the local channel you value, and HUD's state CDBG programs reach small towns through the same thin local governments.

**Recommendation: B.** The rural delivery note's main finding is that staff, not money, limits the programs [EV-HOU-777] [EV-HOU-780], and B buys back the local, staffed model at modest cost. C is right if you want Parts 2-5 at the high end quickly; the staff count is judgment.

## Part 2: buying a home (Section 502)

USDA makes direct mortgages to low-income rural buyers who cannot get credit elsewhere, with payment help that can bring the rate to 1% and no down payment (Congressional Research Service) [EV-HOU-599], and guarantees private lenders' loans for buyers up to 115% of area median income [EV-HOU-766]. In FY2025 it guaranteed 41,012 loans worth $8.4 billion [EV-HOU-771]. Memo 9 chose down payment help through state agencies (HOU-10.01) and left growing Section 502 to this memo.

**A. Keep the current plan.** Section 502 stays at about $1 billion of direct loans a year [EV-HOU-781]; rural buyers also use HOU-10.01.
- *For people:* a home health aide in rural Georgia earning 70% of the area's median income qualifies, but the loan limit no longer covers the homes for sale in her county [EV-HOU-779].
- *Cost or risk:* none added.

**B. Direct loans at scale.** Grow direct lending from about 2,000-3,000 loans a year to about 15,000 by FY2033: about $4 billion of loans a year (judgment, at about $270,000 a loan, near FY2026's average [EV-HOU-700]). With it:
- the 80% loan limit restored, so the program works where homes cost more [EV-HOU-779];
- servicing that acts early on missed payments, using the ROAD to Housing Act's power to stretch a loan to 40 years [EV-HOU-764], because nearly a fifth of loans are behind [EV-HOU-701];
- HOU-10.01's down payment help stackable with both Section 502 loans, under the plain stacking rule you already chose (`DEC-2026-11-09-23`).
- *Cost or risk:* about $3.5 billion of credit subsidy over FY2030-39 ($2.5-5 billion), at about 13% (judgment, from OMB's rate; rural delivery note, section 1.2), written as a FY2030-39 loan level, so time-limited. Higher delinquency would raise the rate when OMB re-estimates it. It needs Part 1's staff.
- *Burden:* the statute's "credit elsewhere" test [EV-HOU-765] stays, checked from the credit report USDA already pulls rather than lender denial letters (judgment).

**C. B, plus broader eligibility (recommended).**
- **Direct loans to 100% of area median income.** Today they stop at 80% [EV-HOU-766]. Payment help already shrinks as income rises, so a buyer at 95% gets the loan and little or no payment help (judgment). This is one changed number, not a new test. It needs the statute.
- **Guaranteed loans to HOU-10.01's limits:** 140% of area median income, 160% in high-cost areas, instead of 115% [EV-HOU-766], so a rural buyer who qualifies for the plan's down payment help also qualifies for the plan's rural mortgage. One number across the plan's buyer programs (design lens).
- **A first-generation priority** where requests exceed money, by the buyer's signed statement, as in HOU-10.01.
- *For people:* a young teacher couple in a small Montana town earning 130% of median gets a guaranteed loan with HOU-10.01's help; a family at 95% gets a direct loan.
- *Cost or risk:* the guaranteed loans earn more in fees than they cost (rural delivery note, section 1.2), so the wider limit adds little cost and could save a little. The direct loans to 100% add about $0.1-0.3 billion over the decade (judgment), since higher-income buyers need less payment help. The risk is crowding: with a fixed loan level, moderate-income buyers compete with the poorest ones. B's larger level and the first-generation priority limit that; a review, not a cap, watches the income mix.
- *Burden:* the same as B.

**D. A rural public lender at 1970s scale.** 50,000 direct loans a year or more.
- *Cost or risk:* about $1.5-2 billion a year of subsidy (judgment), more staff than the agency could hire in a decade, and a delinquency problem multiplied.

**Recommendation: C.** It answers your "broader eligibility" ask with two plain numbers, puts the plan's buyer help on one income line, and grows the direct loans as far as Part 1's staff can carry them. The 15,000-loan target, the 100% limit and the costs are judgment; no causal study measures what the loans add [EV-HOU-702].

## Part 3: repairs (Section 504), the rural door into the repair fund

HOU-12.01 runs through the states, cities and urban counties that receive CDBG and HOME, with a tribal share. In small towns the grantee is usually the state's CDBG office, which works through local governments with little staff. USDA's repair program reached about 3,000 owners with grants and about 1,200 with loans in 2025 (rural delivery note, section 1.3) [EV-HOU-772]. Its limits:
- **Age.** Grants only at 62 and over; that is USDA's rule, not the statute [EV-HOU-760] [EV-HOU-761].
- **Income.** Grants only at or below 50% of area median income (statute) [EV-HOU-760]; the ROAD to Housing Act opened loans, not grants, to owners up to 80% [EV-HOU-762].
- **Amounts and paperwork.** $10,000 in a lifetime [EV-HOU-616]; an assets test ($20,000 for older owners, $15,000 for others), a "credit elsewhere" test, and repayment if the home is sold within three years, all USDA rules [EV-HOU-761]; a lien above $15,000 since the ROAD Act [EV-HOU-762]. USDA itself says its old $7,500 limit "discourages small repairs" [EV-HOU-772].
- **Another door already exists.** USDA's Section 533 grants go to nonprofits, tribes and local governments that repair homes of owners up to 80% of area median income, with no age limit [EV-HOU-768].

**A. Keep the current plan.** HOU-12.01 reaches rural owners through state CDBG offices; Section 504 continues as it is.
- *For people:* a 45-year-old disabled owner in rural Kentucky with a failing roof gets nothing from USDA because she is under 62, and waits for a state program to reach her county.
- *Cost or risk:* none added.
- *Burden:* two programs, two sets of rules.

**B. One repair program with two doors (recommended).**
- **The rule is the repair fund's.** In rural areas, owners can apply at a USDA office or a Section 533 grantee, or at the state or local HOU-12.01 grantee. Either door gives the same help on the same terms: grants at or below 80% of area median income, zero-interest loans repaid at sale from 80% to 120%, up to $25,000 a home (plus $15,000 for lead or accessibility), income by signed statement or benefit enrollment, no credit score, **no age test**, no assets test (HOU-12.01's decided rules).
- **The money.** A rural share of HOU-12.01 goes to USDA, which uses its own offices and Section 533 grantees. About a sixth, about $6 billion of the $38 billion (judgment: the register has no count of low-income owners in USDA-eligible areas). The state keeps the rest of its rural share and may hand more to USDA. **No new money.**
- **Section 504 stays,** opened by USDA's own rule to owners of any age, for the very low-income owners it already serves, so nothing is lost if the fund ends after FY2039.
- **The older-adult track** (HOU-12.04, the CAPABLE model) runs through the same doors.
- *For people:* the Kentucky owner walks into the USDA office in the county seat, signs a statement, and gets her roof fixed that season.
- *Cost or risk:* no added cost. USDA needs Part 1's staff and inspectors; without them, its share sits unspent. The sixth is a guess that should be set from data in section 4.
- *Burden:* it removes four tests (age, assets, credit, three-year repayment) and lets owners use whichever office is closest.

**C. B, plus extra money for rural repairs on top of HOU-12.01.** For example $0.5 billion a year more through USDA.
- *Cost or risk:* about $5 billion over FY2030-39 (judgment). It reaches more rural homes, but adds money the decided $38 billion did not include, and the bottleneck is staff and contractors, not money.

**D. Fix Section 504 alone.** Drop the age rule, raise grants to $15,000-25,000, open grants to 80% of area median income, and fund it at about $0.3 billion a year, separate from HOU-12.01.
- *Cost or risk:* about $3 billion over FY2030-39 (judgment). Two repair programs with different rules for the same rural owners.
- *Burden:* owners must learn which program fits them.

**Recommendation: B.** It answers "not just buyers over 62" directly: the age, assets and credit tests go, and rural owners get the repair fund's terms at a USDA office. It costs nothing new, because it routes money you already decided through the channel you trust. C is the choice if Part 1's staff arrive and the rural share runs out.

## Part 4: rural rental homes (Sections 515 and 521)

About 12,000 USDA-financed rental properties remain (rural delivery note, section 3.4). Until 2024, rent help ended when a mortgage was paid off. Appropriations acts let USDA keep paying it for up to 5,000 units in FY2026 [EV-HOU-781], and the ROAD to Housing Act made that and the preservation program permanent and widened rural vouchers to every low-income tenant of a paid-off property [EV-HOU-239] [EV-HOU-240]. In 2025, 547 of the 911 units eligible to leave stayed [EV-HOU-775]. USDA projects preservation needs above $30 billion through 2050, about $1.2 billion a year, against $8 million spent on Section 515 preservation in 2025 [EV-HOU-774] [EV-HOU-784]. The heaviest maturities fall inside the plan's window [EV-HOU-783].

**A. Keep the current plan.** The ROAD Act's permanent tools at today's funding: about $30 million for preservation, rent help under the appropriations cap [EV-HOU-756].
- *For people:* in a 24-unit building in rural Iowa, a retired cook on $14,000 a year loses her rent help when the mortgage is paid off, or the building is sold and rents rise.
- *Cost or risk:* none added; USDA's estimate, as GAO reported, says most of the stock could leave [EV-HOU-783], and most properties with maturing mortgages already have [EV-HOU-785].

**B. Rent help follows every maturing mortgage, and a preservation fund (recommended).**
- **Automatic offer.** A year before a mortgage matures, USDA offers the owner continued rent help, with no unit cap. If the owner leaves anyway, tenants get rural vouchers, as the ROAD Act allows [EV-HOU-240].
- **A preservation and transfer fund,** $0.5 billion a year over FY2030-39, for repairs and for sales to nonprofit, public and tenant buyers, who are less likely to convert to market rents (An, 2026; rural delivery note, section 2.1). HOU-3C's revolving funds and loans can also buy and preserve these buildings, already an eligible use.
- *For people:* the retired cook keeps her apartment and her rent; the building is sold to a regional nonprofit that fixes the roof.
- *Cost or risk:* the fund is about $5 billion over FY2030-39 ($3-8 billion), time-limited, enough for perhaps 60,000 apartments at USDA's average need per unit (judgment, from [EV-HOU-774]). Keeping rent help on maturing buildings costs about $7,700 a unit a year [EV-HOU-773], but against the budget baseline, which carries today's rental assistance forward, it is roughly no new cost (judgment). Rural vouchers for tenants who lose their building: about $0.06 billion a year, the placeholder in `road-funding.md`, permanent. Deals need staff who can close them (Part 1).
- *Burden:* low; USDA starts the offer, not the owner or tenant.

**C. B, at USDA's full estimate.** $1.2 billion a year for the fund [EV-HOU-774].
- *Cost or risk:* about $12 billion over FY2030-39. It matches the agency's projection, which no independent estimate checks; with today's staff and nonprofit buyers, much of it could go unspent in the early years.

**D. Move the stock to HUD.** Convert USDA rent help to HUD project-based contracts and let HUD manage the buildings.
- *Cost or risk:* one rental help system, but it gives up USDA's local servicing and would be a large transition for 12,000 small properties.
- *Burden:* heavy for owners and tenants during the move.

**Recommendation: B.** It turns the ROAD Act's permanent tools into a default that keeps people housed, at little cost against the baseline, and funds preservation at a level the agency can spend once staffed. The fund size is judgment; it should rise toward C if USDA spends it well.

## Part 5: self-help and farmworker housing

**Self-help (Section 523).** Nonprofits guide groups of low-income families who build their own homes with their own labor, financed with Section 502 direct loans [EV-HOU-770]. In 2025 USDA made 29 grants for $22.8 million [EV-HOU-776]. The 2026 loan limit falls hardest on these families, because their labor makes the home worth more than the loan (rural delivery note, section 1.3). No recent data show cost per home or results.

**Farmworker housing (Sections 514 and 516).** Loans and grants for housing farmworkers and their families, including retired and disabled former farmworkers, wherever the need is, not only in rural areas [EV-HOU-769]. In 2025, $32.7 million went to 11 states [EV-HOU-776]. The statute limits occupants to citizens, permanent residents and people legally admitted and authorized to work in agriculture [EV-HOU-769].

**A. Keep the current plan.** About $25 million for self-help and about $21 million for farmworker housing a year [EV-HOU-756].

**B. Grow both (recommended).**
- Self-help to about $100 million a year, four times today, with the 80% loan limit restored (Part 2) and the family's labor counted toward the home's value.
- Farmworker housing loans and grants to about $200 million a year, with rent help through Section 521 (judgment).
- *For people:* twelve families in rural New Mexico build their own homes together over a year, each with a 502 loan; a grower cooperative in Washington builds 40 family apartments for year-round workers.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.25 billion a year more, about $2.5 billion over FY2030-39, time-limited (judgment). Nonprofit capacity limits how fast either grows; the evidence on both is old or missing.
- *Burden:* low; the nonprofits carry it.

**C. Fold both into HOU-3C.** The state revolving funds finance self-help and farmworker housing among their other uses.
- *Cost or risk:* no new money, but it loses the specialized nonprofit network and USDA's rent help for farmworker homes.

**Recommendation: B.** Both programs are small, simple and run by nonprofits that already know how, and B is the cheapest way to add owners and family housing in places with few other builders. The amounts are judgment.

**A separate question: who may live in farmworker housing.** The immigration-status limit [EV-HOU-769] keeps many farmworker families out of the housing built for them; the register has no count of how many. Dropping it would reach more of the workforce, and it is a question about immigration policy that the plan's other dossiers have not weighed. **This memo recommends leaving it to that work,** and asks you whether you want it weighed now.

## Part 6: which places count as rural

For housing, a rural area is a place of up to 2,500 people; up to 10,000 if "rural in character"; up to 20,000 outside a metro area with "a serious lack of mortgage credit"; and places that lost eligibility after a census keep it up to 35,000 **only until data from the 2030 census arrive** [EV-HOU-767]. Rural Development uses seven definitions across its programs, from 5,000 to 50,000 people (Congressional Research Service; rural delivery note, section 1.6). So in the early 2030s, inside the plan's window, growing towns could lose eligibility unless Congress renews the grandfather clause, as it has after each census [EV-HOU-767].

**A. Keep the current plan.** Congress renews the grandfather clause after the 2030 census, as before.
- *Cost or risk:* none added; the renewal can slip, and staff keep running "rural in character" and mortgage-credit reviews.
- *Burden:* each town checked against a map that changes each decade.

**B. One plain rural test for housing, up to 35,000 (recommended).** Any place up to 35,000 people outside an urbanized core counts, using one published map; the "rural in character" and "lack of mortgage credit" judgments go, and every place eligible today stays eligible.
- *For people:* a town of 18,000 in Arkansas that grew past a line does not lose its USDA office's loans the year the census lands.
- *Cost or risk:* no money by itself; it spreads fixed program money over somewhat more places, which Part 1's staff and Part 2's larger loan level absorb. The register has no count of the people it adds.
- *Burden:* lower: one map, no reviews or appeals.

**C. One test for all of Rural Development.** One limit (for example 50,000) for housing, water, broadband, power and business programs.
- *Cost or risk:* simplest for towns, but it thins water and broadband money for the smallest places, and those programs belong to other dossiers. Better proposed to them, through the rural delivery note.

**D. Widen housing to 50,000.** Match the business programs.
- *Cost or risk:* reaches small cities that HUD's grantees and FHA already serve; USDA's guaranteed loans would compete with FHA there, and staff would be spread thinner.

**Recommendation: B.** It keeps every place now served, ends the decade-by-decade cliff and the judgment calls, and asks staff to check one map. C is worth raising with the other dossiers; it is not housing's to decide.

## Day one: what USDA can do in 2029 without Congress

All are rule or handbook changes, at no budget cost, that apply the design lens (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`):
1. Drop the age-62 limit on repair grants, the assets test and the three-year repayment rule [EV-HOU-760] [EV-HOU-761].
2. Restore the 80% loan limit, a fair packaging fee and single approval in state offices for direct home loans [EV-HOU-779].
3. Stop centralizing loan origination; keep applications in state and area offices (rural delivery note, section 1.1).
4. Offer continued rent help before every maturing mortgage, up to the appropriations limit [EV-HOU-781].
5. Publish decision times and applicant satisfaction by state each quarter [EV-HOU-763].

Recommended as a package for 2029, but you can say yes or no to each.

## Questions for Espen

Each can be answered yes, no, or with a different option.
1. **USDA's local staff (Part 1).** Should the plan pay to rebuild Rural Development's local offices to about 5,000 people (about $0.4 billion a year), keep loan decisions in those local offices, and replace the old loan computer system? (Recommended: yes, option B.)
2. **Home loans (Part 2).** Should USDA's direct home loans grow about six-fold, and be open to families up to 100% of their area's median income (today 80%), with its guaranteed loans open to the same 140% and 160% limits as the plan's down payment help? (Recommended: yes, option C.)
3. **Repairs (Part 3).** Should rural owners be able to get the plan's repair fund at a USDA office, on the fund's simple terms, with no age-62 rule, using about a sixth of the fund's money and no new money? (Recommended: yes, option B.)
4. **Rural apartments (Part 4).** Should USDA automatically keep rent help going when a rural apartment building's mortgage is paid off, plus a $0.5 billion a year fund to repair and sell these buildings to nonprofits? (Recommended: yes, option B.)
5. **Self-help and farmworker housing (Part 5).** Should both grow (to about $100 million and $200 million a year)? And do you want this memo to weigh now whether farmworker housing should be open to farmworker families regardless of immigration status, or leave that to later immigration work? (Recommended: grow both; leave the status question for later.)
6. **What counts as rural (Part 6).** Should housing use one simple test, any place up to 35,000 people outside a city, keeping every place eligible today? (Recommended: yes, option B.)
7. **Day one.** Should the plan commit to the five rule changes USDA can make in 2029 without Congress? (Recommended: yes.)
