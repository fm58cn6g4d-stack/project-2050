# Memo 6: Emergency rent help

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 6 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

## The question

The plan has an $85 billion one-time emergency housing package for 2030-31 (HOU-1A). Should emergency help be a one-time package, or a standing fund that is always there when a renter hits a crisis?

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** HOU-1A is the plan as filed (`chosen`). The recommendation would replace it with a standing fund and a smaller one-time start.

## Why it matters

Small, fast help keeps people housed.
- In Chicago, people who called a help line when money happened to be available were 76% less likely to enter a shelter (Evans, Sullivan and Wallskog, Chicago study in *Science*, 2016) [EV-HOU-108].
- In a California trial, about $2,000 of aid made households 81% less likely to become homeless within six months (Phillips and Sullivan, Santa Clara County trial, 2023) [EV-HOU-109].
- In 2025, 23% of renters fell behind on rent at some point (Federal Reserve, household well-being survey, 2026) [EV-HOU-008].

## The options

**A. One-time $85 billion (as now planned).**
- Rent help plus money to fill gaps in building projects, spent in 2030-31.
- *For people:* a big wave of help in two years, then nothing.
- *Risk:*
  - The last national program spent only about 6% of its first round in its first months (Congressional Research Service, Emergency Rental Assistance report, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].
  - Rents have cooled since, so the need in 2030 is uncertain (Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, 2026) [EV-HOU-068].
  - It never measured how much money went to the wrong people (Treasury Inspector General, 2024 and 2025) [EV-HOU-154] [EV-HOU-182].

**B. One-time $40 billion, rent help only.**
- *For people:* the same wave, smaller.
- *Risk:* the same start-up problems, at lower cost (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

**C. A standing emergency-rent fund, plus a smaller one-time start (recommended).**
- A permanent fund gives one-time help to renters about to lose their homes: a month or two of rent, a deposit, legal help.
- Simple rules: renters can state their own income and hardship; landlords can apply; payment errors are measured from the first year. In the last program, spending doubled in the month after renters were allowed to state their own income (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].
- The building-gap money moves into the construction funds you already chose.
- *For people:* for example, a cook whose hours are cut in a recession in 2034 can get back rent paid before an eviction, not only someone who happened to struggle in 2030. Help like this kept families out of shelters in New York City's Homebase trial (Rolston, Geyer and Locke, Abt Associates, 2013) [EV-HOU-110].
- *Cost or risk:*
  - Permanent spending, so it must be paid for from 2040 under the fiscal rule. It is likely small next to rental help; section 6 sizes it.
  - Across a whole city, the effect is smaller than in trials: Homebase cut family shelter entries by 5% to 11% (Goodman, Messeri and O'Flaherty, 2016) [EV-HOU-111].
  - It is a new option, not yet in the agenda.

## Recommendation

**C.** It rests on three findings:
1. Prevention works for the people it reaches (Evans, Sullivan and Wallskog, 2016; Phillips and Sullivan, 2023) [EV-HOU-108] [EV-HOU-109].
2. The last one-time national program was slow to start (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].
3. Its value depends on reaching the right people. In Chicago it cost about $10,300 for each case of homelessness prevented, and the researchers expect much less with better targeting (Evans, Sullivan and Wallskog, 2016) [EV-HOU-108].

In this dossier's reading, many people who get emergency aid would have stayed housed anyway, so the fund's rules must aim it at those most at risk.

A standing fund is there when need rises, and it avoids rebuilding a program from scratch each crisis; that part is judgment. The evidence on the last national program is split:
- one evaluation credits it with fewer evictions (UC Berkeley and University of Pennsylvania researchers for HUD, 2026) [EV-HOU-152];
- lottery studies found it helped people pay rent but did little to keep them from moving (Collinson and others, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2025) [EV-HOU-153].

That argues for building and testing a lasting program carefully.
