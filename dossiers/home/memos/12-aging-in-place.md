# Memo 12: Aging in place with dignity

Role: decision memo for Espen, part 2 of the section 3 addendum (`../3-design.md`, "Addendum, part 2: repair and renovation; aging in place"). Status: awaiting Espen's decision; four parts, each decided on its own. The research is in `../research-repair-aging.md`, sections 6 to 9. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Costs and counts marked "judgment" are this dossier's estimates, not official scores.

## The question

You asked on November 9 for policies to help older people stay in their homes as they age, if they want to, when they are at financial risk of losing them: an "age with dignity" frame. The plan has nothing aimed at this today. Its rental help reaches older renters only if they are homeless (stage 1) or disabled (stage 3, from 2038), and you decided to reconsider older renters who are not disabled once the care program is scoped (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`). This memo does not reopen that decision. It asks four other questions. **Decide each part on its own.**
- **Part 1.** Should the plan help older owners with property taxes?
- **Part 2.** Should the plan pay for home repairs and changes that let older people stay safely, and how does that meet the care program?
- **Part 3.** Should the plan protect older owners' equity: tangled titles, reverse mortgages and tax foreclosure?
- **Part 4.** What should the plan do for older renters' homes, short of the rental guarantee question you have already set aside?

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** Nothing here reverses an earlier decision. Each part adds a program or a use of money already in the plan. The plan starts in 2029; new help would begin in 2030.

## Why it matters

- **Most older people want to stay put.** 75 percent of adults 50 and older want to stay in their current home as long as possible (AARP, 2024) [EV-HOU-699].
- **Housing costs are already heavy for them.** In 2023, 12.4 million older households, 34 percent, paid more than 30 percent of income for housing; 58 percent of older renters did (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2025) [EV-HOU-671]. In 2024 owners 65 and over had the highest cost-burden rate of any age group, 28 percent, and property taxes rose 31 percent from 2019 to 2025 (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2026) [EV-HOU-670].
- **Taxes push older owners out.** A $100 rise in annual property tax raised the two-year moving rate of owners over 50 by about 8 percent (Shan, Federal Reserve working paper, 2008) [EV-HOU-681].
- **Falls end independence.** 14 million older adults reported a fall in 2020; falls killed 38,742 in 2021 and cost about $50 billion a year in medical care (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) [EV-HOU-690].
- **Older renters with the worst needs are many and mostly unhelped.** 2.46 million older renter households had worst case needs in 2023, and fewer than 38.8 percent of very low-income older households got any rental help (HUD, 2026) [EV-HOU-672].

## Part 1: property taxes

**A. Keep the current plan.** No federal role. States run their own relief: 29 states and the District of Columbia have circuit breakers, which limit tax as a share of income, with average benefits of $100 to $2,000 (Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy, 2026) [EV-HOU-682]. Some states let older owners defer taxes until sale.
- *For people:* a widow on Social Security in a state without relief faces each reassessment alone.
- *Cost or risk:* none added. Relief stays uneven by state.
- *Burden:* none new.

**B. A federal match for state relief that meets simple standards (recommended).**
- The federal government pays half of what a state spends on property-tax relief for owners 65 and over (and owners with disabilities) with incomes up to about 80 percent of the area median, when the relief meets three standards:
  1. **Relief by income:** tax above a share of income the state sets (say 4-6 percent) is credited back, as a circuit breaker does.
  2. **A deferral option for the rest:** any older owner may defer the rest of the bill, as a lien repaid at sale or death at a low rate, with no yearly reapplication.
  3. **Records the state already holds:** age and income from state tax or benefit records, so most owners are enrolled without filling in a form. Owners who do not file can sign a one-page statement.
- Renters 65 and over in the same states can get the share of rent that stands for property tax, as some state circuit breakers already allow (the register does not yet count which).
- *For people:* a retired bus driver in Ohio whose bill jumps after a reassessment gets the part above 5 percent of income credited back automatically, and can defer the rest instead of selling.
- *Cost or risk:* about $1-4 billion a year in federal match once states join, $8-35 billion over 2030-39 as states join (judgment: roughly 2-4 million older owners at $500-1,000 each of federal share). It is permanent, so it adds to the FY2040 gap that already goes to the financing settlement (`../6-cost.md`; `DEC-2026-11-09-09`). Some relief goes to owners who would have stayed anyway; the moving effect is an upper bound [EV-HOU-681]. Federal money can let states cut their own spending; the match is paid only on spending above each state's 2028 level to limit that (judgment).
- *Burden:* light for owners, if states use their records. California shows what happens otherwise: a deferral program with yearly requalification had about 1,000 participants against more than a million eligible (California Legislative Analyst's Office, 2018) [EV-HOU-683].

**C. A federally backed deferral only.**
- A federal loan fund lets every state offer older owners low-cost tax deferral, repaid at sale, at little budget cost because the loans are repaid.
- *For people:* the same owner keeps her home but her heirs inherit less.
- *Cost or risk:* small, perhaps $0.1-0.3 billion a year of subsidy cost (judgment). It reaches few people: owners avoid liens on homes they hope to pass on, as California's take-up shows [EV-HOU-683].
- *Burden:* a lien and a loan agreement.

**D. A federal refundable property-tax credit for older owners, paid by the IRS.**
- *For people:* the same relief in every state.
- *Cost or risk:* the most expensive, perhaps $4-8 billion a year (judgment), and it pays toward high tax bills in rich places as well as poor ones unless income-capped. The IRS does not know most households' property tax, so it adds a new item to check.
- *Burden:* a tax filing for owners who do not now file.

**Recommendation: B.** Property taxes are a proven reason older owners move [EV-HOU-681]; states already run the machinery [EV-HOU-682]; and the match can reward the low-burden designs, automatic enrollment and no yearly requalification, that decide whether relief reaches people [EV-HOU-683]. The share-of-income threshold, the income line and the cost are judgment. If you want to add nothing permanent, C is the fallback.

## Part 2: repairs and home changes for older people, and the care program

**A. Keep the current plan.** HUD's small Older Adults Home Modification grants continue: more than $109 million to 67 grantees in January 2025, about $7,000 per family in a 2024 round (HUD, 2024 and 2025) [EV-HOU-689] [EV-HOU-673]. Medicaid's home-care waivers can pay for some changes, but almost 650,000 people wait for them, 34 months on average (KFF, 2026) [EV-HOU-691].
- *Cost or risk:* none added. Help stays a lottery of local grants.
- *Burden:* none new.

**B. An older-adult track in memo 11's repair fund, built on CAPABLE (recommended).**
- Owners and renters 65 and over, or with a disability, at or below 80 percent of area median income, can get a home assessment by an occupational therapist and repairs and changes: grab bars, rails, ramps, a first-floor bathroom, lighting, plus the system repairs memo 11 pays for.
- For people with daily-living limits, the CAPABLE model: a nurse and an occupational therapist visit, and a handyman does up to a few thousand dollars of work. In a randomized trial of 300 low-income older adults, it cut disability scores 30 percent by the program's end, at $2,825 a person (Szanton and others, *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2019) [EV-HOU-686]. The effect faded by 12 months, and Medicare's savings estimate rests on a nonrandomized evaluation (Journal of the American Geriatrics Society, 2021) [EV-HOU-688] [EV-HOU-687].
- Renters get the same help with the landlord's written consent; the landlord may not raise rent for it.
- **Where it meets care.** The repair fund pays for the home. Whether Medicare or Medicaid pays for CAPABLE's nurse and therapist visits, and how Medicaid home care grows, is the care program's decision (HLT-4). The fund sends people to Medicaid home care where they qualify, and the care dossier can later move CAPABLE's clinical part into Medicare.
- *For people:* a 78-year-old with arthritis in Detroit gets a railing, a raised toilet and a fixed furnace, and a therapist shows her how to get in and out of the tub safely.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.3-1.6 billion a year inside memo 11's fund, reaching about 100,000-200,000 older households a year at $3,000-8,000 each (judgment). Falls prevented and nursing-home stays delayed are likely but not well measured.
- *Burden:* light: the same self-attested income as memo 11; an assessment visit.

**C. Make CAPABLE a Medicare benefit now.**
- *For people:* every Medicare beneficiary with daily-living limits could get it.
- *Cost or risk:* the savings evidence is not yet strong enough to score [EV-HOU-688]; and Medicare benefits are the care dossier's to design.
- *Burden:* Medicare enrollment of providers.

**Recommendation: B.** It pays for the part that is plainly housing, the repairs and changes, through one front door, and leaves the clinical benefit to the care program, which is where Medicare and Medicaid are decided. The trial evidence supports the model [EV-HOU-686]; its lasting effect and its savings are uncertain, so the fund should measure falls and moves to nursing homes from the start (judgment).

## Part 3: protecting older owners' equity

**A. Keep the current plan.** Current law helps in three ways:
- the Supreme Court held in 2023 that a county may not keep the equity left after a tax sale (Tyler v. Hennepin County) [EV-HOU-677], though it does not stop the sale itself;
- FHA's 2023 rules let reverse-mortgage servicers wait while tax and insurance arrears are $5,000 or less, and allow repayment plans of up to five years (HUD, 2023) [EV-HOU-680];
- 24 states, the Virgin Islands and the District of Columbia have adopted the uniform law protecting heirs from forced partition sales (American Bar Association, 2025) [EV-HOU-685].
- *Cost or risk:* none added.

**B. A low-cost equity protection package (recommended).**
- **Legal help for titles and wills.** Grants to legal aid and nonprofits to clear tangled titles (homes held by heirs without a deed in their name), write simple wills and deeds that pass at death, and defend tax foreclosures. Philadelphia alone had at least 10,407 tangled titles worth over $1.1 billion; clearing one costs about $9,200 (Pew Charitable Trusts, 2021) [EV-HOU-684]. A tangled title blocks repair grants, tax relief and insurance claims.
- **Reverse mortgages.** Write FHA's 2023 relief into law, so a later administration cannot drop it. Defaults, mostly unpaid taxes and insurance, rose from 2 percent of reverse-mortgage endings in 2014 to 18 percent in 2018, and only about 22 percent of borrowers behind on those charges got a repayment plan (Government Accountability Office, 2019) [EV-HOU-678]. Part 1's deferral would cover the taxes.
- **Before any tax sale of an older owner's home,** a state taking Part 1's match must first offer a payment plan or deferral. This is a condition on new money, not a federal rule for every state.
- *For people:* a grandson who inherited his grandmother's row house in Philadelphia gets the deed into his name for free, then qualifies for a repair grant.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.1-0.3 billion a year (judgment). The reverse-mortgage book is healthy today (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-679], so making the relief permanent is a small risk to FHA's fund.
- *Burden:* none for owners; legal aid does the work.

**C. Federal standards for every state's tax foreclosures.**
- *For people:* the same protections everywhere.
- *Cost or risk:* property tax is state law; a federal mandate invites a legal fight and the evidence does not show it is needed beyond Tyler [EV-HOU-677].
- *Burden:* new federal oversight of county tax offices.

**Recommendation: B.** It is cheap, uses lawyers rather than new rules, and fixes the paperwork problems that stop older owners using every other kind of help. The cost is judgment.

## Part 4: older renters' homes and services

The question of rental help for older renters who are not disabled waits on the care program (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`). This part is about buildings and services, which do not.

**A. Keep the current plan.** HUD's Section 202 homes for older renters keep their rent contracts, about 118,000 households, and about 1,500 service coordinators; no new Section 202 homes have been funded since 2012 (HUD, 2026) [EV-HOU-672]. The 2026 budget proposed ending the program, and Congress kept it (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-674]. HOU-3C's construction and acquisition money can already build homes for older renters, but nothing points it there.
- *Cost or risk:* none added. The waiting lists stay.

**B. Restart Section 202 construction.** About $1 billion a year for 2030-39, building perhaps 4,000-6,000 homes a year with services (judgment).
- *For people:* new senior apartments with a coordinator on site.
- *Cost or risk:* $10 billion over ten years, plus the rent contracts that follow, which are permanent. The ROAD to Housing Act asks GAO to study 202's capital advances within a year [EV-HOU-676]; the study should come first.
- *Burden:* HUD's 202 process is slow and separate from the plan's other construction money.

**C. Point HOU-3C at older renters and fund service coordinators (recommended).**
- A share of HOU-3C's state grants and federal loans goes to homes for older and disabled renters, set by each state's need (no federal quota), with HUD publishing how many homes each state builds or preserves for them.
- Every federally assisted building where most tenants are 62 or over can get a service coordinator, who links tenants to home care, meals and benefits. Vermont's housing-based care coordination slowed Medicare spending growth by about $1,100 a person a year in one group, though not across all sites (HHS, 2019) [EV-HOU-698].
- *For people:* a 70-year-old on a $16,000 income in a senior building gets help applying for Medicaid home care before a fall forces a move.
- *Cost or risk:* the homes come from HOU-3C's existing amounts, so they cost nothing new but displace other uses. Coordinators cost about $0.1-0.2 billion a year more (judgment, from HUD's $122 million for about 1,500 grants [EV-HOU-672]).
- *Burden:* none for tenants.

**Recommendation: C.** It builds for older renters without a separate, slower program, and the coordinators are cheap and link housing to the care program. If GAO's study shows Section 202's capital model is the faster route, B can follow. The share of HOU-3C and the coordinator cost are judgment.

## Smaller points, recommended without a decision of their own

These ride on memo 10 and memo 11; say no to any.
- **Accessory units for older owners.** Memo 10's zoning reward already counts accessory-unit reform. FHA's Title I loans can now finance an accessory unit (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-220]; most California owners who built one used their own cash, at a median $150,000 (Terner Center, 2022) [EV-HOU-675]. The plan should let memo 11's deferred loans pay for a unit an older owner builds for a caregiver or a tenant.
- **Home sharing.** Small grants to match older owners with tenants who pay low rent and help around the house, as HomeShare Vermont does for 364 people on about $0.7 million a year (HomeShare Vermont, 2026) [EV-HOU-692]. Under $50 million a year nationally (judgment).
