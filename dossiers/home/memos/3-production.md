# Memo 3: How the plan builds homes

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 3 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). It also answers the open call on the federal builder of last resort (HOU-9A, 9B or 9C). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

**Part 3 of this memo decides the form and size of the construction money (HOU-3C): about $72 billion of grants to state revolving funds and $300 billion of federal loans.** On October 30 you made it a working choice rather than a settled one (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`), so keeping it as filed is one option there, weighed like the others. The recommendation keeps both channels and both amounts and adds one use: buying and preserving existing buildings.

## The question

Public housing repair and the social housing pilot stay as chosen. This memo has **three parts. Decide each on its own.**
- **Part 1.** Should the plan keep all three housing tax credits, and should every home it pays for stay affordable for good?
- **Part 2.** Should it create a federal builder for places where states don't build?
- **Part 3.** Is the construction money the right size and shape?

The plan starts in 2029; its building money flows from 2030.

## Current law: the ROAD to Housing Act

The ROAD to Housing Act, law since July 11, 2026, **does not change the options in this memo, and changes one recommendation only slightly** (part 1: the middle-income credit is postponed until a federal report arrives, not dropped). It adds no construction money and does not touch the housing tax credits (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-201] [EV-HOU-202]. What it does change is the starting point (`../road-act.md`):
- Community development grants may now pay for new affordable homes, up to a fifth of a grant, and the HOME program is renewed with fewer deadlines, though with no set amount (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-215] [EV-HOU-226] [EV-HOU-227].
- Banks may put more money into tax-credit projects: up to 20% of their capital instead of 15% (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-241].
- Public housing can keep converting to other kinds of rent help with no end date, and the limit on conversions since 2012 rises from 455,000 to 555,000 homes (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-231]. The repair money you chose (HOU-5) works on a shrinking program, and section 4 must say how it treats homes that convert.
- Congress's auditors (GAO) must report by July 2027 on housing for middle-income households (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-242]. Under the recommendation, the middle-income credit is put off until that report is in, not dropped.
- Cities and counties must list the empty land they own (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-214], which helps find sites for public building.

## Why it matters

- **Tax credits partly pay for homes that would have been built anyway.**
  - One subsidized home adds a third to a half of a home to the market (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114].
  - One careful study finds nearly all of it is offset (Eriksen and Rosenthal, 2010) [EV-HOU-115].
- **Affordability runs out.** About a third of today's 2.5 million tax-credit homes are set to leave their rent limits by 2035 (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].
- **New state programs start slowly.** Only about 6% of the first round of pandemic rent aid was spent by May 2021 (Congressional Research Service, Emergency Rental Assistance report, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

## Part 1: tax credits and lasting affordability

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** The plan as filed (HOU-2A, which is `chosen`) has three tax credits:
- a bigger Low-Income Housing Tax Credit with a boost for the poorest renters;
- a new credit for middle-income rentals;
- a credit for repairing homes for sale in struggling neighborhoods.

The recommendation would change that.

**A. Keep all three credits (as chosen).** Affordability rules as under current law.
- *For people:* more homes for middle-income renters, though many would have been built anyway. Tax-credit building crowds out private building in gentrifying areas, though not in stable or declining ones (Baum-Snow and Marion, 2009) [EV-HOU-116].
- *Risk:* the middle-income credit is a permanent tax cost that may buy few extra homes. Affordability keeps expiring (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].

**B. Narrower credits, permanent affordability (recommended).**
- Keep the bigger tax credit with the boost for the poorest renters. Postpone the middle-income credit until GAO's report on middle-income housing, due July 2027, before the plan starts (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-242]; waiting for that report is this dossier's judgment; drop or postpone the neighborhood repair credit.
- Every home the plan builds or buys stays affordable permanently. New tax-credit buildings give a nonprofit the right to buy when the rent limits end.
- *For people:* for example, a home-care worker renting in a new nonprofit building keeps an affordable rent decades from now. Today's tax-credit tenants can lose theirs when the rent limits expire (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].
- *Risk:* the middle-income credit is the one housing benefit that reaches middle-to-upper-middle earners, and the fairness test is not yet met for that group (`charter/PROGRESS.md`). The household model must confirm that group still comes out ahead.

**C. Keep all three credits, and make affordability permanent.**
- *For people:* as A, but homes stay affordable.
- *Risk:* keeps the permanent cost of credits that may buy few extra homes (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005; Eriksen and Rosenthal, 2010) [EV-HOU-114] [EV-HOU-115].

**Recommendation: B.** It rests on three findings:
1. Studies typically find tenant help more cost-effective than building subsidies (Congressional Research Service, *The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit: Policy Issues*, 2019) [EV-HOU-112].
2. Tax credits partly replace building that would have happened anyway (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005; Eriksen and Rosenthal, 2010) [EV-HOU-114] [EV-HOU-115].
3. About a third of tax-credit homes are set to leave their rent limits by 2035 (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].

Building money should go where it adds homes the market won't, and keep them affordable.

## Part 2: a federal builder of last resort

This is the open choice already on your list (HOU-9A, 9B or 9C).

**C. No federal builder (HOU-9C).** Push harder on zoning conditions instead.
- *Risk:* nothing steps in where states don't build. New state programs can stall: more than $3.1 billion of pandemic rent aid was taken back from grantees who could not spend it (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

**B. Wait for the pilot (HOU-9B).** If the social housing pilot works, its sponsor becomes the federal builder.
- *Cost or risk:* cheapest, but slowest: the builder would not exist until the pilot proves out, likely years in. This is judgment; the register has no evidence on how long such a pilot takes.

**A-small. A small builder from 2029 (recommended).** Authorize a federal builder in 2029-30 with a small start. It builds on federal land, and only in tight markets where state programs fall short.
- *For people:* in a city where the state program stalls, homes still get built.
- *Cost or risk:* a builder's cost includes development, staff, and the risk that some projects don't pay back. Section 6 prices it.

**A-full. A federal builder at full scale (HOU-9A as written).** A-small, scaling up in 2033-36.
- *Cost or risk:* the most expensive option and the hardest to run well. The U.S. has little social housing to build on: 2-10% of homes against more than 20% in Austria (OECD Affordable Housing Database, 2024) [EV-HOU-161].

**Recommendation: A-small.** It rests on two things:
1. Your October 26 direction, which asked for "a lawful federal sponsor/backstop where state participation fails".
2. How slowly new state programs start (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

**The evidence that a federal builder works is thin.** The register has no independent evaluation of a U.S. public developer. The legacy case for it comes from a scenario model (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-9). A small builder authorized in 2029 means the backstop exists before it is needed. B is the cheaper choice if you would rather wait for the pilot.

## Part 3: the construction money (HOU-3C)

**What you chose, and what this weighs.** The plan as filed has two channels. Together they put about $386 billion behind building over 2030-39, counting the states' 20% match, but only about $75 billion counts against the budget, because loans that are repaid count only for their expected losses (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-3; that rate is an estimate until the Congressional Budget Office scores it).
- **Grants** of about $72 billion to a revolving fund in each state, starting at $3 billion in 2030 and rising to $8 billion a year from 2032. At least 30% goes out as grants or forgivable loans for homes for the poorest renters, whose rents cannot repay a loan.
- **Federal loans** of $300 billion, made through state housing agencies, for mixed-income and social housing, public housing authorities' new building, and nonprofit and tax-credit projects.

**A. Keep it as filed.**
- *For people:* new homes at a range of incomes, with the grant share reaching the poorest.
- *How it fits the plan:* loans pay for homes whose rents can repay them. With memo 1's attached rent help, that includes homes for the poorest renters: tax-credit buildings reach them mainly when rent help comes with the home (O'Regan and Horn, NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].

**B. Keep both channels and amounts, and let them buy and preserve existing buildings (recommended).**
- As A, but public and nonprofit owners can also use the money to buy existing rental buildings and single-family homes, including tax-credit buildings whose rent limits are ending, and keep them affordable for good (part 1).
- *For people:* for example, tenants in a tax-credit building whose rent limits end in 2032 keep their rents when a nonprofit buys it. About a third of today's 2.5 million tax-credit homes are set to leave their rent limits by 2035 (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].
- *How it fits the plan:* a bought building can house families years before a new one is finished, so memo 1's attached help has homes to go to from the early 2030s. It also gives your October 26 direction's "acquisition" a funded channel.
- *What the evidence doesn't show yet:* the register has no study of what buying buildings costs or achieves. That part rests on your direction and on judgment.

**C. More grants, fewer loans.**
- For example, an even split: $125 billion of grants and $125 billion of loans (option HOU-3D in the plan).
- *For people:* more homes for the poorest renters without needing attached rent help.
- *How it fits the plan:* it suits memo 1's option A (vouchers everywhere) better than B does.
- *Cost or risk:* about $65 billion more against the budget. And $12.5 billion a year of grants is close to what the long-running water revolving funds take in, about $13 billion a year (plan's figure from the Congressional Research Service, not yet in the register), while most states have no housing fund yet. New state programs start slowly (Congressional Research Service, Emergency Rental Assistance report, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

**D. Less building, more rent help.**
- Move some or all of the grant money into vouchers.
- *For people:* the $72 billion of grants would pay for about 370,000 households' rent for a decade (this dossier's arithmetic, at memo 2's cost per voucher). Cutting the loans frees little, because they barely count against the budget: about $15 billion, enough for about 77,000 households for a decade.
- *Evidence for it:* rent help is typically more cost-effective than building subsidies (Congressional Research Service, *The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit: Policy Issues*, 2019) [EV-HOU-112], and subsidized building partly replaces building that would happen anyway (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114].
- *Risk:* fewer homes where they are scarcest. There are only 38 affordable and available homes for every 100 of the poorest renter households (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-024], and vouchers often go unused where homes are scarce (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, Institute for Research on Poverty, 2024) [EV-HOU-092].

**Recommendation: B.** It keeps the construction money as you chose it, and that part of the plan holds up on three findings:
1. With rent help attached, loans can pay for homes for the poorest renters (NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].
2. The slow grant start matches how slowly new state programs begin (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].
3. Moving the grants into rent help would buy relatively little and give up homes in the places where vouchers fail (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, 2024) [EV-HOU-092].

It adds buying and preserving buildings because your October 26 direction names it and expiring rent limits are a measured loss (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119]. If you choose vouchers everywhere in memo 1, C becomes the better fit.

## If you decide

Part 1 B sets HOU-2B over HOU-2A and adds the permanent-affordability rule. Part 2 sets HOU-9A, 9B or 9C. Part 3 B keeps HOU-3C's amounts and adds buying and preserving buildings as eligible uses. Section 4 writes them; section 6 prices them.
