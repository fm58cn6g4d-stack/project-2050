# Memo 3: How the plan builds homes

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 3 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). It also answers the open call on the federal builder of last resort (HOU-9A, 9B or 9C). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides.

## The question

You already chose the construction money: grants to state revolving funds and federal loans (HOU-3C). Public housing repair and the social housing pilot also stay as chosen. This memo has **two parts. Decide each on its own.**
- **Part 1.** Should the plan keep all three housing tax credits, and should every home it pays for stay affordable for good?
- **Part 2.** Should it create a federal builder for places where states don't build?

## Why it matters

- **Tax credits partly pay for homes that would have been built anyway.** One subsidized home adds a third to a half of a home to the market [EV-HOU-114]; one careful study finds nearly all of it is offset [EV-HOU-115].
- **Affordability runs out.** About a third of today's 2.5 million tax-credit homes are set to leave their rent limits by 2035 [EV-HOU-119].
- **New state programs start slowly.** Only about 6% of the first round of pandemic rent aid was spent by May 2021 [EV-HOU-150].

## Part 1: tax credits and lasting affordability

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** The plan as filed (HOU-2A, which is `chosen`) has three tax credits: a bigger Low-Income Housing Tax Credit with a boost for the poorest renters, a new credit for middle-income rentals, and a credit for repairing homes for sale in struggling neighborhoods. The recommendation would change that.

**A. Keep all three credits (as chosen).** Affordability rules as under current law.
- *For people:* more homes for middle-income renters, though many would have been built anyway.
- *Risk:* the middle-income credit is a permanent tax cost that may buy few extra homes, and affordability keeps expiring.

**B. Narrower credits, permanent affordability (recommended).**
- Keep the bigger tax credit with the boost for the poorest renters. Drop or postpone the other two.
- Every home the plan builds or buys stays affordable permanently. New tax-credit buildings give a nonprofit the right to buy when the rent limits end.
- *For people:* for example, a home-care worker renting in a new nonprofit building keeps an affordable rent decades from now. Today's tax-credit tenants can lose theirs when the rent limits expire.
- *Risk:* the middle-income credit is the one housing benefit that reaches middle-to-upper-middle earners, and the fairness test is not yet met for that group (`charter/PROGRESS.md`). The household model must confirm that group still comes out ahead.

**C. Keep all three credits, and make affordability permanent.**
- *For people:* as A, but homes stay affordable.
- *Risk:* keeps the permanent cost of credits that may buy few extra homes.

**Recommendation: B.** Tenant help is usually more cost-effective than building subsidies [EV-HOU-112], so building money should go where it adds homes the market won't, and keep them affordable.

## Part 2: a federal builder of last resort

This is the open choice already on your list (HOU-9A, 9B or 9C).

**C. No federal builder (HOU-9C).** Push harder on zoning conditions instead.
- *Risk:* nothing steps in where states don't build.

**B. Wait for the pilot (HOU-9B).** If the social housing pilot works, its sponsor becomes the federal builder.
- *Cost or risk:* cheapest, but slowest: the builder would not exist until the pilot proves out, likely years in.

**A-small. A small builder now (recommended).** Authorize a federal builder in 2029-30 with a small start. It builds on federal land, and only in tight markets where state programs fall short.
- *For people:* in a city where the state program stalls, homes still get built.
- *Cost or risk:* a builder's cost includes development, staff, and the risk that some projects don't pay back. Section 6 prices it.

**A-full. A federal builder at full scale (HOU-9A as written).** A-small, scaling up in 2033-36.
- *Cost or risk:* the most expensive option and the hardest to run well. The U.S. has little social housing to build on: 2-10% of homes against more than 20% in Austria [EV-HOU-161].

**Recommendation: A-small.** This rests on your October 26 direction, which asked for "a lawful federal sponsor/backstop where state participation fails", and on how slowly new state programs start. **The evidence that a federal builder works is thin:** the register has no independent evaluation of a U.S. public developer, and the legacy case for it comes from a scenario model (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-9). A small builder now means the backstop exists before it is needed. B is the cheaper choice if you would rather wait for the pilot.

## If you decide

Part 1 B sets HOU-2B over HOU-2A and adds the permanent-affordability rule. Part 2 sets HOU-9A, 9B or 9C. Section 4 writes them; section 6 prices them.
