# Memo 3: How the plan builds homes

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 3 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). It also answers the open call on the federal builder of last resort (HOU-9A, 9B or 9C). Status: parts 2 (A-small) and 3 (B) decided by Espen on November 9, 2026 (recorded in PR #135); part 1 re-opened by Espen November 9, 2026, awaiting decision, and revised here. Nothing in part 1 changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

**Part 3 of this memo decides the form and size of the construction money (HOU-3C): about $72 billion of grants to state revolving funds and $300 billion of federal loans.** On October 30 you made it a working choice rather than a settled one (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`), so keeping it as filed is one option there, weighed like the others. The recommendation keeps both channels and both amounts and adds one use: buying and preserving existing buildings.

## The question

Public housing repair and the social housing pilot stay as chosen. This memo has **three parts. Decide each on its own.**
- **Part 1.** Should the plan keep all three housing tax credits, and should every home it pays for stay affordable for good?
- **Part 2.** Should it create a federal builder for places where states don't build?
- **Part 3.** Is the construction money the right size and shape?

The plan starts in 2029; its building money flows from 2030.

## Current law: the ROAD to Housing Act

The ROAD to Housing Act, law since July 11, 2026, **does not change the options in this memo.** Its middle-income housing study, due July 2027, is read before any credit is written (part 1). It adds no construction money and does not touch the housing tax credits (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-201] [EV-HOU-202]. What it does change is the starting point (`../road-act.md`):
- Community development grants may now pay for new affordable homes, up to a fifth of a grant, and the HOME program is renewed with fewer deadlines, though with no set amount (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-215] [EV-HOU-226] [EV-HOU-227].
- Banks may put more money into tax-credit projects: up to 20% of their capital instead of 15% (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-241].
- Public housing can keep converting to other kinds of rent help with no end date, and the limit on conversions since 2012 rises from 455,000 to 555,000 homes (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-231]. The repair money you chose (HOU-5) works on a shrinking program, and section 4 must say how it treats homes that convert.
- Congress's auditors (GAO) must report by July 2027 on housing for middle-income households (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-242]. Part 1 reads it before writing any middle-income credit.
- Cities and counties must list the empty land they own (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-214], which helps find sites for public building.

## Why it matters

- **Tax credits partly pay for homes that would have been built anyway.**
  - One subsidized home adds a third to a half of a home to the market (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114].
  - One careful study finds nearly all of it is offset (Eriksen and Rosenthal, 2010) [EV-HOU-115].
- **Affordability runs out.** About a third of today's 2.5 million tax-credit homes are set to leave their rent limits by 2035 (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].
- **New state programs start slowly.** Only about 6% of the first round of pandemic rent aid was spent by May 2021 (Congressional Research Service, Emergency Rental Assistance report, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

## Part 1: tax credits and lasting affordability

**Status: re-opened by Espen November 9, 2026, awaiting decision.** You were inclined to do "a bit of all three options (A, B, C)" or to ask for more research on federal policy. This part is that research, revised; the detail is in `../memo-3-part1-research.md`. Your view was not counted as evidence either way.

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** The plan as filed (HOU-2A, which is `chosen`) has three tax credits:
- a bigger Low-Income Housing Tax Credit with a boost for the poorest renters;
- a new credit for middle-income rentals;
- a credit for building or repairing homes for sale in struggling neighborhoods.

**The law in 2029.** The 2025 tax law permanently raised the low-income credit by 12% and made bond-financed projects easier to qualify; nothing since has changed the credit (Joint Committee on Taxation, 2025) [EV-HOU-260]. It cost about $15.7 billion over ten years (Joint Committee on Taxation, 2025) [EV-HOU-261]. It did not add the boost for the poorest renters, a middle-income credit or the neighborhood credit.

**The bills behind each credit.** All three have bipartisan bills in Congress now:
- *Bigger low-income credit:* the Affordable Housing Credit Improvement Act (S. 1515 and H.R. 2725; Sen. Todd Young, R-IN, with Sens. Cantwell, Wyden and Blackburn). It raises allocations further and adds a 50% boost for buildings that set aside a fifth of their homes for the poorest renters (bill text, 2025) [EV-HOU-262] [EV-HOU-263].
- *Middle-income credit:* the Workforce Housing Tax Credit Act (H.R. 8626, 2026; Rep. Jimmy Panetta, D-CA, with Rep. Mike Carey, R-OH). Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR) has proposed it since 2018 and put it in his DASH Act, the broadest progressive housing bill. Most homes in a building must rent to households at or below the area's median income; the credit is $1 per resident a year (bill text, 2026) [EV-HOU-264] [EV-HOU-266]. Builders, landlords and realtors back it; the backers estimate 344,000 homes (Rep. Panetta's office, citing Novogradac, 2026) [EV-HOU-267]. The National Low Income Housing Coalition opposes it as "wasteful and misguided" (NLIHC, 2023) [EV-HOU-282].
- *Neighborhood credit:* the Neighborhood Homes Investment Act (S. 1686 and H.R. 2854; Sen. Young and Rep. Mike Kelly, R-PA, with many Democrats). It pays the gap between what a home costs to build or fix and what it sells for, for buyers up to 140% of median income (bill text, 2025) [EV-HOU-268] [EV-HOU-269].
- None has an official cost estimate for its current version.

**What the evidence says about each.**
- *Bigger low-income credit.* Nationally, about eight in ten credit homes replace homes that would have been built anyway, so each added home costs about $1 million (Soltas, MIT working paper, 2024) [EV-HOU-275]; the older studies agree (Eriksen and Rosenthal, 2010) [EV-HOU-115]. But in dense places the cost per added home is about half that (Soltas, 2024) [EV-HOU-277]. The boost for the poorest renters is the best-supported piece, because that is where rent limits actually bite (NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].
- *Middle-income credit.* In most of the country a rent cap at 80% of median income is above what typical homes already rent for, so it would lower no one's rent: that holds in all but five counties (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-272]. State and local versions show the leak: California's middle-income deals capped some homes "effectively at market rates" (California public-finance advisers, 2021) [EV-HOU-280], and in Seattle only about half the tax break came back as lower rent (University of Washington, 2024) [EV-HOU-281]. **But in costly cities the market is not building for these renters at all**: in three California markets, rents a median earner can pay are close to market rents, yet no single policy change makes such buildings pencil (Terner Center, 2024) [EV-HOU-279]. There a credit would pay for homes that would not otherwise be built, rather than for lower rents. Harvard's Joint Center, CRS and the Terner Center all point the same way: such a credit has a case only in high-cost places, kept apart from the money for the poorest (Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2024; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-283] [EV-HOU-274]. No middle-income credit has ever been evaluated.
- *Neighborhood credit.* The best evidence of the three: in a Missouri credit given out by lottery, winners built nearly two-thirds more, with less crowding out in declining neighborhoods, and about half of each credit dollar showed up in the home's value (Clarke and Freedman, working paper, 2018) [EV-HOU-284]. CRS warns that a gap-filling credit can let costs drift up (Congressional Research Service, 2021) [EV-HOU-271].

**A. Keep all three credits (as chosen).** Affordability rules as under current law.
- *For people:* more middle-income rentals and neighborhood homes, though many would have been built anyway (Soltas, 2024) [EV-HOU-275].
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.15 trillion over ten years (legacy estimate, `legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-2). The middle-income credit as filed costs about $20 billion over 2030-39 and about $5 billion a year once mature, mostly where its cap does not bite (this dossier's arithmetic from the bill) [EV-HOU-266] [EV-HOU-272]. Affordability keeps expiring (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].

**B. Narrower credits, permanent affordability (recommended before this research).**
- Keep the bigger low-income credit with the boost for the poorest renters. Postpone the middle-income credit until GAO's middle-income housing report, due July 2027 (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-242]. Drop or postpone the neighborhood credit.
- Every home the plan builds or buys stays affordable permanently; new tax-credit buildings give a nonprofit the right to buy when the rent limits end.
- *For people:* for example, a home-care worker renting in a new nonprofit building keeps an affordable rent decades from now.
- *Cost or risk:* the cheapest, about $0.08-0.12 trillion (legacy estimate, HOU-2B). It drops or postpones the neighborhood credit, which has the best evidence of added building [EV-HOU-284], and the only credits that reach middle earners, which the household model must check (`charter/PROGRESS.md`, D3).

**C. Keep all three credits, and make affordability permanent.**
- *For people:* as A, but homes stay affordable.
- *Cost or risk:* as A; the middle-income credit still pays mostly where it buys little [EV-HOU-272] [EV-HOU-280].

**D. A bit of all three, each where it adds homes (recommended).**
- *Low-income credit:* the bigger credit with the boost for the poorest renters, as in B.
- *Middle-income credit, narrowed:*
  - only in high-cost areas, such as the ones the low-income credit already marks as hard to build in (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-274];
  - rents at least 10% below a checked market rent for comparable new homes;
  - homes stay affordable for good;
  - first call for places that win memo 4's zoning reward;
  - five years of awards (2030-34), part given by lottery so it can be evaluated, renewed only if it adds homes;
  - states with no eligible places move their share to the low-income credit (bill text, 2026) [EV-HOU-266].
- *Neighborhood credit:* as the bill writes it, for 2030-34, in the struggling neighborhoods it targets, with an evaluation, renewed if homes get built at a reasonable cost.
- *Permanent affordability* for every rental home the plan pays for, as in B and C.
- *For people:* for example, a teacher in a costly city rents in a new building where some homes are held below market for good; in a struggling neighborhood a family buys a rebuilt house at a price it can carry.
- *Cost or risk:* about $0.10-0.14 trillion over 2030-39: the low-income part as in B, about $7 billion for the middle-income trial and about $15 billion for the neighborhood credit (this dossier's arithmetic from the bills and CRS's method) [EV-HOU-269] [EV-HOU-270]. The narrow credit is harder to run (state agencies must check market rents) and builds less than the bill would. It is still a bet: the evidence supports where to aim it, not yet that it works.

**Recommendation: D.** It rests on three findings:
1. Credits add the most homes where the market would not build them, in dense places and declining neighborhoods, and the least elsewhere (Soltas, 2024; Baum-Snow and Marion, 2009; Clarke and Freedman, 2018) [EV-HOU-277] [EV-HOU-116] [EV-HOU-284].
2. A middle-income cap buys nothing where it sits above market rents, which is most places, but in costly cities middle-income buildings are not being built at all, so a credit there can add homes (Congressional Research Service, 2026; Terner Center, 2024) [EV-HOU-272] [EV-HOU-279].
3. Independent researchers who have studied middle-income programs recommend exactly this narrowing: high-cost places only, without taking from the poorest (Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2024; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-283] [EV-HOU-274].

**What changed from the earlier recommendation, B.** B postponed the middle-income credit and dropped the neighborhood credit. The new evidence supports a narrow middle-income credit as a trial, and it gives the neighborhood credit the strongest added-building result of the three, so D keeps both, small and tested. If you want the leanest plan, B is still the cheapest, and the research finds no case for the middle-income credit as filed. GAO's middle-income report (July 2027) [EV-HOU-242] is read before section 4 writes any credit, under any option.

## Part 2: a federal builder of last resort

This is the open choice already on your list (HOU-9A, 9B or 9C).

**C. No federal builder (HOU-9C).** Push harder on zoning conditions instead.
- *Risk:* nothing steps in where states don't build. New state programs can stall: more than $3.1 billion of pandemic rent aid was taken back from grantees who could not spend it (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

**B. Wait for the pilot (HOU-9B).** If the social housing pilot works, its sponsor becomes the federal builder.
- *Cost or risk:* cheapest, but slowest: the builder would not exist until the pilot proves out, likely years in. This is judgment; the register has no evidence on how long such a pilot takes.

**A-small. A small builder from 2029 (recommended).** Authorize a federal builder in 2029-30 with a small start. It builds on federal land, and only in tight markets where state programs fall short.
- *For people:* in a city where the state program stalls, homes still get built.
- *Cost or risk:* a builder's cost includes development, staff, and the risk that some projects don't pay back. Section 6 prices it.

**A-full. A federal builder at full scale (HOU-9A as written).** A-small, scaling up in 2033-36.
- *Cost or risk:* the most expensive option and the hardest to run well. The U.S. has little social housing to build on: 2-10% of homes against more than 20% in Austria (OECD Affordable Housing Database, 2024) [EV-HOU-161].

**Recommendation: A-small.** It rests on two things:
1. Your October 26 direction, which asked for "a lawful federal sponsor/backstop where state participation fails".
2. How slowly new state programs start (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

**The evidence that a federal builder works is thin.** The register has no independent evaluation of a U.S. public developer. The legacy case for it comes from a scenario model (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-9). A small builder authorized in 2029 means the backstop exists before it is needed. B is the cheaper choice if you would rather wait for the pilot.

## Part 3: the construction money (HOU-3C)

**What you chose, and what this weighs.** The plan as filed has two channels. Together they put about $386 billion behind building over 2030-39, counting the states' 20% match, but only about $75 billion counts against the budget, because loans that are repaid count only for their expected losses (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-3; that rate is an estimate until the Congressional Budget Office scores it).
- **Grants** of about $72 billion to a revolving fund in each state, starting at $3 billion in 2030 and rising to $8 billion a year from 2032. At least 30% goes out as grants or forgivable loans for homes for the poorest renters, whose rents cannot repay a loan.
- **Federal loans** of $300 billion, made through state housing agencies, for mixed-income and social housing, public housing authorities' new building, and nonprofit and tax-credit projects.

**A. Keep it as filed.**
- *For people:* new homes at a range of incomes, with the grant share reaching the poorest.
- *How it fits the plan:* loans pay for homes whose rents can repay them. With memo 1's attached rent help, that includes homes for the poorest renters: tax-credit buildings reach them mainly when rent help comes with the home (O'Regan and Horn, NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].

**B. Keep both channels and amounts, and let them buy and preserve existing buildings (recommended).**
- As A, but public and nonprofit owners can also use the money to buy existing rental buildings and single-family homes, including tax-credit buildings whose rent limits are ending, and keep them affordable for good (part 1).
- *For people:* for example, tenants in a tax-credit building whose rent limits end in 2032 keep their rents when a nonprofit buys it. About a third of today's 2.5 million tax-credit homes are set to leave their rent limits by 2035 (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119].
- *How it fits the plan:* a bought building can house families years before a new one is finished, so memo 1's attached help has homes to go to from the early 2030s. It also gives your October 26 direction's "acquisition" a funded channel.
- *What the evidence doesn't show yet:* the register has no study of what buying buildings costs or achieves. That part rests on your direction and on judgment.

**C. More grants, fewer loans.**
- For example, an even split: $125 billion of grants and $125 billion of loans (option HOU-3D in the plan).
- *For people:* more homes for the poorest renters without needing attached rent help.
- *How it fits the plan:* it suits memo 1's option A (vouchers everywhere) better than B does.
- *Cost or risk:* about $65 billion more against the budget. And $12.5 billion a year of grants is close to what the long-running water revolving funds take in, about $13 billion a year (plan's figure from the Congressional Research Service, not yet in the register), while most states have no housing fund yet. New state programs start slowly (Congressional Research Service, Emergency Rental Assistance report, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].

**D. Less building, more rent help.**
- Move some or all of the grant money into vouchers.
- *For people:* the $72 billion of grants would pay for about 370,000 households' rent for a decade (this dossier's arithmetic, at memo 2's cost per voucher). Cutting the loans frees little, because they barely count against the budget: about $15 billion, enough for about 77,000 households for a decade.
- *Evidence for it:* rent help is typically more cost-effective than building subsidies (Congressional Research Service, *The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit: Policy Issues*, 2019) [EV-HOU-112], and subsidized building partly replaces building that would happen anyway (Sinai and Waldfogel, 2005) [EV-HOU-114].
- *Risk:* fewer homes where they are scarcest. There are only 38 affordable and available homes for every 100 of the poorest renter households (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-024], and vouchers often go unused where homes are scarce (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, Institute for Research on Poverty, 2024) [EV-HOU-092].

**Recommendation: B.** It keeps the construction money as you chose it, and that part of the plan holds up on three findings:
1. With rent help attached, loans can pay for homes for the poorest renters (NYU Furman Center, 2012) [EV-HOU-118].
2. The slow grant start matches how slowly new state programs begin (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-150].
3. Moving the grants into rent help would buy relatively little and give up homes in the places where vouchers fail (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, 2024) [EV-HOU-092].

It adds buying and preserving buildings because your October 26 direction names it and expiring rent limits are a measured loss (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, 2025) [EV-HOU-119]. If you choose vouchers everywhere in memo 1, C becomes the better fit.
