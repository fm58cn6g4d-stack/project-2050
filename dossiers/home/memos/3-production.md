# Memo 3: How the plan builds homes

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 3 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). It also answers the open call on the federal builder of last resort (HOU-9A, 9B or 9C). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides.

## The question

You already chose the construction money: grants to state revolving funds and federal loans (HOU-3C). Three questions remain. Should the plan keep all three housing tax credits? Should every home it pays for stay affordable for good? And should it create a federal builder for places where states don't build?

## Why it matters

- **Tax credits partly pay for homes that would have been built anyway.** One subsidized home adds a third to a half of a home to the market [EV-HOU-114]; one careful study finds nearly all of it is offset [EV-HOU-115].
- **Affordability runs out.** About a third of today's 2.5 million tax-credit homes are set to leave their rent limits by 2035 [EV-HOU-119].
- **New state programs start slowly.** Only about 6% of the first round of pandemic rent aid was spent by May 2021 [EV-HOU-150].

## The options

**A. As now planned.**
- All three tax credits: a bigger Low-Income Housing Tax Credit with a boost for the poorest renters, a new credit for middle-income rentals, and a credit for repairing homes for sale in struggling neighborhoods. No federal builder.
- *For people:* more homes for middle-income renters, though many would have been built anyway.
- *Risk:* the middle-income credit is a permanent tax cost that may buy few extra homes. Nothing steps in if states don't build.

**B. Lending and lasting affordability first (recommended).**
- Keep the tax-credit increase with the boost for the poorest renters. Drop or postpone the middle-income credit.
- Every home the plan builds or buys stays affordable permanently.
- Create a small federal builder now. It builds on federal land, and only in tight markets where state programs fall short.
- *For people:* for example, a home-care worker renting in a new nonprofit building keeps an affordable rent decades from now. Today's tax-credit tenants can lose theirs when the rent limits expire.
- *Cost or risk:* lower permanent tax cost than A. The builder's loans are repaid, so its real cost is staff and operations. One risk to check: the middle-income credit is the one housing benefit that reaches middle-to-upper-middle earners, and the fairness test is not yet met for that group (`charter/PROGRESS.md`). The household model must confirm that group still comes out ahead.

**C. A federal builder at full scale.**
- B, plus a federal builder that grows large in 2033-36.
- *For people:* more homes in places where states won't build.
- *Cost or risk:* the most expensive option and the hardest to run well. The case for it comes from a scenario model, not real-world evidence (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-9). The U.S. has little social housing to build on: 2-10% of homes against more than 20% in Austria [EV-HOU-161].

## Recommendation

**B.** The evidence favors deep, lasting affordability over broad credits. Tenant help is usually more cost-effective than building subsidies [EV-HOU-112], so building money should go where it adds homes the market won't. A small builder authorized now means the backstop you asked for on October 26 exists before it is needed. It can grow later if the evidence supports it.
