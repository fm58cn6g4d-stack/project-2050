# Memo 4: What the zoning reward pays for

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 4 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). Status: awaiting Espen's decision; revised for the ROAD to Housing Act, now current law (`../road-act.md`). Nothing changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

## In short

The ROAD to Housing Act, law since July 11, 2026, already created two federal tools that reward places for building more housing. The plan's zoning fund (HOU-6) would duplicate them. **The recommendation changes:** instead of a separate fund, put the plan's money into the Act's reward fund, open it to states, pay for homes finished, and count lower-cost homes. Drop the plan's own condition on community development grants, since the Act already has one. Keep the condition on new transportation money.

## The question

The plan has a Housing Supply Fund (HOU-6) that rewards cities and states for zoning reform, plus conditions on new federal transportation and community development money. Now that the Act has its own reward fund and its own condition, should the plan build a separate program, or build on the Act's?

**What you already chose, and what this would change.** HOU-6's fund and conditions are in the plan as filed (`chosen`). The recommendation keeps HOU-6's money and its transportation condition, but replaces its separate fund and its community development condition with the Act's two tools.

## What the law already does

- **A reward fund (the "Innovation Fund").** Grants to cities, counties and tribes that have shown faster housing growth, at least 25 a year, from $250,000 to $10 million each (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-206]. The reforms it names are the plan's: apartments allowed by right, fewer parking rules, smaller lots, backyard cottages (same law) [EV-HOU-207].
  - It may spend $200 million a year from 2027 to 2031, but Congress has not yet provided any of it, and the fund ends in 2033 (Congressional Research Service, 2026; ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-202] [EV-HOU-206].
  - States cannot apply (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-206].
- **A shift in community development grants ("Build Now").** From 2029 to 2043, cities and counties whose building speeds up less than most lose 10% of their community development grant, and faster builders gain it; places growing 4% a year or more always get a share. Places with cheap housing, many empty apartments, recent disasters or no zoning power are left out (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-208]. The whole grant program was $3.3 billion in 2025 (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-209].
- **Smaller pieces.** Planning grants until 2031, ready-made building designs, guidance on single-stair apartment buildings, and national zoning guidelines (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-210] [EV-HOU-212] [EV-HOU-243] [EV-HOU-213]. None of it overrides local zoning (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-206].

## Why it matters

Some zoning reforms produce homes, and some mostly raise land prices.
- When Auckland, New Zealand, rezoned about three-quarters of its residential land, an estimated 21,808 extra homes were permitted in five years (Greenaway-McGrevy and Phillips, 2023) [EV-HOU-123].
- California's statewide rules on backyard cottages took yearly permits from 1,336 to 30,354 (California housing department, 2026) [EV-HOU-128].
- Chicago's narrower rezonings near transit raised land values but added no new permits in five years (Freemark, 2019) [EV-HOU-124].
- Across U.S. cities, reforms that loosened the rules went with about 0.8% more housing over three to nine years, mostly at higher rents (Stacy and others, Urban Institute, 2023) [EV-HOU-177].

The law already pays for growth, as the evidence favors. Its gaps are where the evidence points next:
- **No money yet**, and awards of at most $10 million (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-206].
- **No role for states**, though the broad reforms above were state- or region-wide (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-206].
- **No count of lower-cost homes**, where reforms have done least (Urban Institute, 2023) [EV-HOU-177]. Applicants "may" report who the new homes serve (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-207].
- **Below 4% growth a year, Build Now rewards speeding up rather than building a lot** (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-244]. In this dossier's reading, a place that builds steadily at under 4% a year can lose money while one recovering from a slump gains. Nobody has evaluated it yet.
- **Build Now reaches only big cities and urban counties, and it cuts their existing grants** (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-208]. Smaller places get their community development money through the state, which Build Now does not touch.

## The options

**A. Keep a separate fund (as chosen).**
- The plan's own fund rewards reforms, scaled to homes permitted, plus conditions on new transportation and community development money.
- *For it:* it needs no change to who can apply for an existing program, its money was set to ride the budget bill, and its condition reaches new community development money everywhere, including the state-run part for smaller places that Build Now leaves out (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-6).
- *Risk:* two federal reward programs and two growth tests on the same community development grant, for the same reforms. Cities would answer to both.

**B. Pay for results through the law's tools (recommended).**
- The plan's money goes into the Act's reward fund, which is changed so that:
  - states and regions can compete, with larger awards for statewide reform;
  - money follows homes finished;
  - lower-cost homes are counted and rewarded separately;
  - the $10 million cap rises and the 2033 end date goes.
- The Act's community development shift stays as the only test on that grant. The plan studies it and proposes a fix if it punishes steady builders.
- The condition on new transportation money stays; the Act has nothing like it.
- *For people:* for example, a state that legalizes small apartment buildings everywhere, and sees them built, can win a large award; today's law would pay only its cities, and only up to $10 million each.
- *Cost or risk:* about the same money as the plan's fund. Places with slow markets get less, even if they reform; Build Now, not the reward fund, exempts places with many empty apartments (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-208]. Dropping the plan's own condition leaves the state-run community development money for smaller places with no condition at all. Changing who can apply needs a change in the law, which may need a regular bill as well as the budget bill; that is judgment.

**C. Federal override.**
- Federal law overrides local permits for federally financed homes.
- *Risk:* legally risky and politically hard. This is judgment, not evidence from the register. Congress just chose rewards over mandates, and the Act says its grants override no local zoning (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-206]. The federal builder in memo 3 already covers building on federal land.

**D. Fund the law as written.**
- Provide the $200 million a year the Act allows, through 2031, and add nothing (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-206].
- *Cost or risk:* cheapest. No role for states, no count of lower-cost homes, and the reward ends in 2033.

## Recommendation

**B.** It rests on three findings:
1. Broad reforms are the ones followed by building: Auckland's with a measured effect (Greenaway-McGrevy and Phillips, 2023) [EV-HOU-123], California's by raw permit counts from the agency that runs it (California housing department, 2026) [EV-HOU-128].
2. A narrow reform in Chicago raised land values without building (Freemark, 2019) [EV-HOU-124].
3. Gains across U.S. cities were modest and mostly at higher rents (Urban Institute, 2023) [EV-HOU-177].

The law already rewards growth. The plan should add what it lacks (money, a role for states, a count of lower-cost homes) rather than build a second program beside it. That part is judgment: neither of the law's tools has been funded or evaluated. D is the cheaper fallback.
