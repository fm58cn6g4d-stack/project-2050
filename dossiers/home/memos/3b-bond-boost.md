# Memo 3b: should the bigger tax credit also boost bond-financed projects?

Role: decision memo for Espen, a follow-up to memo 3 part 1 (option D, `DEC-2026-11-09-08`) on one piece of provision HOU-2D.01. Status: decided by Espen on November 9, 2026: option (a), leave it out (`DEC-2026-11-09-17`): "b isn't cheap for 2k-10k extra homes-- i think we drop it". HOU-2D.01 does not include section 308. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Every cost and home count here is this dossier's estimate: no official score exists.

## The question

The bill behind HOU-2D.01, the Affordable Housing Credit Improvement Act, has a section 308. It would let state housing agencies give their 30% "basis boost" to bond-financed projects (the "4% credit" deals), not only to projects using the state's yearly credit allocation. Should HOU-2D.01 include it?

## What the law does now

- **Projects in designated qualified census tracts (poor tracts) and difficult development areas (high-cost areas) already get the boost, bond-financed or not** (U.S. Code, 2023 edition, unchanged on this point by the 2025 tax law) [EV-HOU-460]. Both are HUD designations, each capped at 20% of an area's population; neither is a density test.
- **Outside those designated areas, an agency may give the boost to a project that needs it, but not to a bond-financed one** (U.S. Code) [EV-HOU-461]. Section 308 removes that exclusion for bonds issued after 2025 (bill text, 2025) [EV-HOU-462]. So its effect is on bond-financed projects outside currently designated tracts and areas.
- **Bonds are easier to get now.** The 2025 tax law cut the share of a project that bonds must finance from 50% to 25% (Joint Committee on Taxation, 2025) [EV-HOU-260]. The same bond allowance now stretches over more projects, so the limit on bond deals is usually the leftover funding gap.
- **The plan already fills gaps two ways.** HOU-2D.01's boost for the poorest renters raises basis to 150% for the homes a building sets aside for them, at least a fifth (bill text, 2025) [EV-HOU-263]; those homes are outside the 30% boost, so under (b) the 30% reaches only the building's other homes. HOU-3C's grants and loans pay about $150,000-300,000 of gap per home (`4-specification.md`, HOU-3C.04).

## What it would buy

- **Per home:** about $33,000-41,000 more in credits over ten years. This is this dossier's arithmetic: the 4% credit is about 4% of eligible basis a year for ten years, and a 30% boost adds 1.2% of eligible basis a year. Tax-credit homes cost about $322,000-406,000 in total to develop in Washington State (Washington State Housing Finance Commission) [EV-HOU-412]. Eligible basis leaves out land and some other costs, so it is taken here as 85% of that, about $274,000-345,000 (assumption). Sold to investors at about 85 cents per credit dollar (assumption), the boost fills about $28,000-35,000 of a project's gap, roughly a tenth to a quarter of HOU-3C's typical gap.
- **The industry's estimate:** more than 175,000 more homes over ten years from all three of the bill's 30% boosts (Novogradac, 2025) [EV-HOU-444]. Novogradac is a tax-credit accounting firm with a stake. Its figure assumes other gap money grows to match, and here that money is largely HOU-3C's. So homes it counts would often have been financed by HOU-3C anyway.
- **Crowd-out:** about eight of every ten tax-credit homes replace homes that would have been built anyway (Soltas, MIT working paper, 2024) [EV-HOU-275]. The same study finds less replacement in denser tracts [EV-HOU-277], but designation is not a density test, and the register has no evidence on crowd-out in the places section 308 would newly reach. So this memo applies one judgment rate, two to five net new homes of every ten, to every option, and does not rank the options by crowd-out.

## The options

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **(a) Leave it out (as now)** | No change | None | None | None |
| **(b) Add it only for buildings using the poorest-renter boost** | Outside designated tracts and areas, the 30% boost reaches the other homes in a bond-financed building that sets a fifth aside for the poorest renters | About $0.4-1.6 billion | About 3,000-18,000 | About 700-9,000 |

| 5. Share of each building's eligible basis the boost reaches | 80% (the rest has the 150% boost) | 100% |
| 6. Added credit a year per boosted home: 1.2% of eligible basis | $3,284-4,141 | $3,284-4,141 |
| 7. Timing: awards 2030-37, in service two years later, credits for ten years | 36 cohort-years fall in FY2030-39 (8 + 7 + ... + 1) | same |
| 8. Boost cost in FY2030-39 (4 × 5 × 6 × 36) | $0.28-0.89 billion | $1.8-3.6 billion |
| 9. New projects the boost makes possible (assumption: 10-20% of boosted homes), each claiming the full 4% credit, $10,948-13,804 a year, over the same 36 cohort-years | $0.12-0.75 billion | $0.6-2.4 billion |
| **10. Revenue cost, FY2030-39 (8 + 9)** | **$0.4-1.6 billion** | **$2.4-6.0 billion** |

Homes financed use lifetime financing value, not the window's revenue:
- **Gap filled.** The boost's ten-year credits, sold at 85 cents, fill $28,000-35,000 per boosted home (times 80% under (b)). Over the eight award years that is $0.5-1.7 billion under (b) and $3.4-6.8 billion under (c).
- **Homes financed.** Half of boosted projects are assumed to use HOU-3C money. The gap the boost fills in those frees HOU-3C money for other homes at $150,000-300,000 each: about 900-5,600 under (b) and 5,600-22,500 under (c). Add the new projects from step 9 over eight award years, about 2,400-12,000 under (b) and 12,000-38,400 under (c).
- **Net new homes.** Two to five of every ten homes financed [EV-HOU-275].
- **Cost per net new home** varies widely with the assumptions, roughly $50,000 to $2.5 million. Most of (c)'s cost is a larger credit on projects that would have gone ahead anyway.

These steps replace an earlier, coarser version of the figures, which gave about $0.5-1 billion for (b) and $3-6 billion for (c). Espen decided on those figures; the decision stands either way.

**About "high-cost areas" in option (b):** bond-financed projects in designated difficult development areas and qualified census tracts already get the boost under current law [EV-HOU-460], so that half of (b) would add nothing. (b) is therefore written as the poorest-renter link alone.

## Weighed against the design lens

The lens prefers one plain test and local judgment over rules checked up front, and asks every rule to earn its place (`charter/STANDARDS.md`, `DEC-2026-11-09-13`).
- (c) is the simplest rule. Agencies already decide which projects need the boost for their state's yearly allocation; under (c) they would decide the same for bond-financed projects. But it costs the most, and most of its cost goes to projects that would have gone ahead anyway.
- (b) adds no new test. It reuses a choice the agency already makes, whether a building gets the poorest-renter boost, so it costs no extra paperwork. It also points the money at the homes the evidence says the credit serves best (memo 3 part 1).
- (a) adds nothing and leaves the gap to HOU-3C. HOU-3C's money is spent on purpose, home by home.

## Recommendation: (b) (not chosen; Espen chose (a))

Add section 308, but only for buildings using the poorest-renter boost.
- It helps the deep-income buildings that are hardest to finance, at small cost, with a test agencies already apply.
- (c) would spend about four to six times as much, mostly on projects that would be built anyway or that HOU-3C already finances.
- Publish yearly counts of boosted bond projects by area, and review in 2034 alongside the credit trials. If the deep-income buildings still don't pencil, widen toward (c) then.
- If you prefer the plainest law, (c) is defensible at about $2.4-6 billion. (a) is the cheapest, and loses little, because HOU-3C covers most gaps.
