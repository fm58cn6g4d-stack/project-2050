# Memo 2: How broad the rental guarantee should be

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 2 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

**This memo decides the scale and funding of the new rental help: the million new vouchers (HOU-4A) and the $135 billion envelope (HOU-4D).** On October 30 you made both working choices rather than settled ones (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`), so keeping them exactly as filed is option A here, weighed like the others. The recommendation keeps both amounts, HOU-4A's schedule and mandatory funding, but changes what the $135 billion buys: a guarantee for a named group instead of a second batch of vouchers for no one in particular.

## The question

On October 26 you asked for a guarantee that ends waiting-list luck for the lowest-income renters. Should new help be a fixed number of vouchers, or a guarantee for named groups? Which renters should it cover, and how fast? The plan starts in 2029; the first new help arrives in 2030.

**Current law.** The ROAD to Housing Act (2026) does not change this memo's options: it adds no rent help (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-201] [EV-HOU-202]. Its one change to the income rules makes disabled veterans easier to qualify for the veterans' voucher program (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-233].

## Why it matters

Today rental help is rationed. Only 28% of very low-income renters get any (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-022], and housing agencies keep waiting lists (Congressional Research Service, voucher program overview, 2023) [EV-HOU-012]. A guarantee means everyone in the covered group who qualifies gets help, like food stamps, instead of a lottery.

Three groups matter here:
- **Extremely low-income renters** earn at or below 30% of their area's median income. There were 12.0 million such households in 2023. Only 1.9 million of them managed without help and without severe housing problems (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-185].
- **Among them, families with children with severe problems:** 1.717 million households (same report) [EV-HOU-186].
- **Very low-income renters** earn at or below 50%. There were 19.14 million (same report) [EV-HOU-020].

## What the money buys

The costs below are this dossier's rough arithmetic, not a score. They use the project's planning figure of $15,200 a voucher a year in 2026, growing 3% a year (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-4A): about $17,100 in 2030 and $22,300 in 2039, about $19,600 on average over 2030-39. A check from 2023 budget data gives about $13,000: $30.25 billion for about 2.3 million households (Congressional Research Service, voucher program overview, 2023) [EV-HOU-187] [EV-HOU-013]. Rents have risen since. Section 6 prices them properly.
- **The million vouchers:** 250,000 more each year from 2030 to 2033, then a million a year. That is 8.5 million voucher-years through 2039, about $170 billion if every voucher were in use all year; the plan's working figure is $150 billion, allowing for vouchers not yet in use.
- **The $135 billion:** about 6.9 million voucher-years. On the same schedule as the first million, that is about 800,000 more vouchers.

## The options

**A. Keep the plan as filed: a fixed number of vouchers, no guarantee.**
- The million new vouchers plus about 800,000 more from the $135 billion. Waiting lists stay for everyone else.
- *For people:* nearly two million more families helped by the late 2030s. Who gets them depends on local waiting lists: help still goes to roughly one household in four that qualifies (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-011] [EV-HOU-012]. Most of the 5.8 million poorest renters with severe problems would still wait (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-186].
- *Cost:* already in the plan: about $285 billion over 2030-39.
- *What holds up:* the amounts and the mandatory funding. *What doesn't:* the second batch has no named group, so it keeps the waiting-list luck you asked to end.

**B. The same money, as the first two steps of a guarantee (recommended).**
- *Step 1, 2030-33:* the million vouchers as filed, for people without a home first, then the first families with children (memo 8).
- *Step 2, from 2034:* the $135 billion guarantees help to every remaining extremely low-income family with children with severe problems. If the million reach about half a million of these families, about 1.2 million remain. Adding them at 300,000 a year over 2034-37 costs about $114 billion through 2039 at full sign-up, inside the $135 billion (this dossier's arithmetic; the split rests on an assumption, because the register does not yet count homeless households).
- Later groups, such as older and disabled renters living alone, follow only when the budget model shows the money.
- *For people:* for example, a single mother of two paying most of her income in rent would know that help is coming in 2034 or soon after, not hope her name comes up.
- *Evidence for it:* long-term rent help sharply reduced homelessness for homeless families who got it (Family Options Study, Gubits and others, 2018) [EV-HOU-086]; moving young children to lower-poverty areas raised their adult earnings (Chetty, Hendren and Katz, Moving to Opportunity study, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2015) [EV-HOU-090].
- *Cost or risk:* about the same as A through 2039, about $27 billion a year for step 2 by 2039. But step 2 is a right, so its cost rises if more families qualify or rents rise faster; the $135 billion becomes an estimate, not a cap. Section 6 must check it against the fiscal rule.

**C. A guarantee for all extremely low-income renters by 2039.**
- *For people:* every household in the lowest band, including older and disabled people living alone, can count on help within the decade.
- *Cost:* roughly $99 billion to $132 billion a year at full scale in 2030 terms, depending on how many of the 5.8 million to 7.7 million unassisted households take it (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-185] [EV-HOU-186]. That is three to four times as many households as A or B reach. The plan's fiscal rule already falls short in 2040 (legacy governing decisions, D4), so it needs new permanent revenue, mostly from the top 1% and corporations under the fairness test.
- *Risk:* landlords can capture part of the money where homes are scarce. Raising voucher limits across a whole city mostly raised landlords' rents (Collinson and Ganong, 2018) [EV-HOU-095]. Memos 1 and 3 address supply.

**D. A guarantee for all very low-income renters.**
- *For people:* reaches 13.77 million unassisted households, including working families just above the lowest band (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-023].
- *Cost:* roughly $236 billion a year at full take-up, in 2030 terms.
- *Risk:* the bigger the program, the more of it can go to landlords.
  - When France extended help to all low-income renters, 78 cents of each euro went into higher rents (Fack, 2006) [EV-HOU-156].
  - A U.S. voucher increase did not raise rents overall (Eriksen and Ross, 2015) [EV-HOU-096], so the U.S. risk may be smaller. But it has never been tested at this scale.

**Funding and pace, under every option.** Keep mandatory funding. Today's voucher money is voted each year, and 87% of it goes to renewing vouchers people already have (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-187]; new help funded the same way would compete with renewals. Congress has funded new vouchers outside the yearly vote before: $5 billion for 70,000 Emergency Housing Vouchers (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-188]. Keep 250,000 new vouchers a year too: even now, only about three in five new voucher holders find a home within six months (Ellen, O'Regan and Strochak, 2024) [EV-HOU-092], so a faster start would outrun housing agencies and landlords. Both points are this dossier's judgment from that evidence.

## Recommendation

**B.** It keeps the plan's amounts but spends the second part on a named group. It rests on three findings:
1. Needs are deepest among extremely low-income renters: they make up 68.6% of all worst case needs (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-185]. Their problem is mostly income. Rents for the poorest fifth of renters rose no faster than for the richest from 1985 to 2019 (Federal Reserve Board staff, 2021) [EV-HOU-070].
2. Long-term rent help sharply reduced homelessness for homeless families who got it (Family Options Study, 2018) [EV-HOU-086], and children gain most from early moves (Chetty, Hendren and Katz, 2015) [EV-HOU-090].
3. Waiting lists ration help to about one eligible household in four (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-HOU-011]. A second batch of vouchers without a named group would go through the same lists.

On this dossier's arithmetic, the money you already set aside is about enough to make help a right for every extremely low-income family with children with severe problems by 2037. Keep C and D as later steps, decided on what the phase-in shows about rents. The French evidence is the main reason to wait (Fack, 2006) [EV-HOU-156].
