# Memo 2: How broad the rental guarantee should be

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 2 of the housing design (`../3-design.md`). Status: awaiting Espen's decision. Nothing changes until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; the bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`).

## The question

On October 26 you asked for a guarantee that ends waiting-list luck for the lowest-income renters. Which renters should it cover?

**Current law.** The ROAD to Housing Act (2026) does not change this memo's options or recommendation: it adds no rent help (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-HOU-201] [EV-HOU-202]. Its one change to the income rules makes disabled veterans easier to qualify for the veterans' voucher program (ROAD to Housing Act, 2026) [EV-HOU-233].

## Why it matters

Today rental help is rationed. Only 28% of very low-income renters get any (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-022], and housing agencies keep waiting lists (Congressional Research Service, voucher program overview, 2023) [EV-HOU-012]. A guarantee means everyone in the covered group who qualifies gets help, like food stamps, instead of a lottery.

Two income groups matter here:
- **Extremely low-income renters** earn at or below 30% of their area's median income. There were 12.0 million such households in 2023. Only 1.9 million of them managed without help and without severe housing problems (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-185].
- **Very low-income renters** earn at or below 50%. There were 19.14 million (same report) [EV-HOU-020].

## The options

The costs below are this dossier's rough arithmetic, not a score. They use the project's planning figure of $15,200 a voucher a year in 2026 (`legacy/domains/domain-housing.md`, HOU-4A). A check from 2023 budget data gives about $13,000: $30.25 billion for about 2.3 million households (Congressional Research Service, voucher program overview, 2023) [EV-HOU-187] [EV-HOU-013]. Rents have risen since. Real costs will be higher by 2030, plus administration, and depend on how many people sign up and how deep the help must be. Section 6 prices them properly.

**A. Bounded help, no guarantee.**
- The million new vouchers already decided, plus the extra $135 billion envelope. Waiting lists stay for everyone else.
- *For people:* about a million more families helped. Most of the 5.8 million poorest renters with severe problems would still wait (HUD, Worst Case Housing Needs report, 2025) [EV-HOU-186].
- *Cost:* already in the plan; roughly $15 billion a year for the million vouchers once phased in.

**B. A guarantee for extremely low-income renters (recommended).**
- Phased in group by group (memo 8 sets the order). The million vouchers and the $135 billion are its first steps.
- *For people:* for example, a disabled man living alone on a small fixed income, now paying most of it in rent, would be able to count on help once his group is reached. Long-term rent help sharply reduced homelessness for homeless families who got it (Family Options Study, Gubits and others, 2018) [EV-HOU-086].
- *Cost:* roughly $88 billion to $117 billion a year at full scale, before growth to 2030, depending on how many of the 5.8 million to 7.7 million unassisted households take it (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-185] [EV-HOU-186]. This is a large permanent cost, and the plan's fiscal rule already falls short in 2040 (legacy governing decisions, D4). It needs new revenue, mostly from the top 1% and corporations under the fairness test.
- *Risk:* landlords can capture part of the money where homes are scarce. Raising voucher limits across a whole city mostly raised landlords' rents (Collinson and Ganong, 2018) [EV-HOU-095]. Memos 1 and 3 address supply.

**C. A guarantee for all very low-income renters.**
- *For people:* reaches 13.77 million unassisted households, including working families just above the lowest band (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-023].
- *Cost:* roughly $209 billion a year at full take-up, before growth to 2030.
- *Risk:* the bigger the program, the more of it can go to landlords.
  - When France extended help to all low-income renters, 78 cents of each euro went into higher rents (Fack, 2006) [EV-HOU-156].
  - A U.S. voucher increase did not raise rents overall (Eriksen and Ross, 2015) [EV-HOU-096], so the U.S. risk may be smaller. But it has never been tested at this scale.

## Recommendation

**B, phased.** It is the group you named on October 26, and it rests on three findings:
1. Needs are deepest here: these renters make up 68.6% of all worst case needs (HUD, 2025) [EV-HOU-185].
2. Their problem is mostly income. Rents for the poorest fifth of renters rose no faster than for the richest from 1985 to 2019 (Federal Reserve Board staff, 2021) [EV-HOU-070]. Rents tracked incomes until 2007; after that, renters' incomes fell while rents rebounded (Collinson, Ellen and Ludwig, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2016) [EV-HOU-071].
3. Long-term rent help sharply reduced homelessness for homeless families who got it (Family Options Study, 2018) [EV-HOU-086]. Most renters this guarantee covers are housed, so the effect for them is not measured by that trial.

Keep C as a later step, decided on what the phase-in shows about rents. The French evidence is the main reason to wait (Fack, 2006) [EV-HOU-156].
