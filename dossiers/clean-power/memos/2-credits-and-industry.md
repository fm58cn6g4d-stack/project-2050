# Memo 2: Clean-energy credits and clean industry after the 2025 repeal

Role: decision memo for Espen, forks 3 and 4 of the clean power dossier (`../3-design.md`). Status: draft, for decision (E-C1). Each part is decided on its own.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **part 1 B, the legacy credit set started again in 2030 (power, storage, manufacturing, household and vehicle credits; refundable, direct pay and point of sale), stepping down on a published schedule as the clean power standard and vehicle standards take over, with a cost review trigger; part 2 A, contracts for difference for clean steel, cement, chemicals and other heavy industry, with a federal Buy Clean purchasing rule.** Cost: as booked, about **$0.87 trillion over FY2030-39 and about $76 billion in FY2040** (credits $0.757 trillion and $55.9 billion; contracts $0.110 trillion and $20.5 billion), with a real risk of running higher on take-up. Evidence: JCT's and CBO's scores of the IRA credits and their growth; the Clean Investment Monitor's investment record; the 2025 law's dates; UK and German contract records; the legacy portfolio model's step-down (a project estimate).

## The question

In January 2029 the IRA's wind, solar, household and vehicle credits will have been closed to new projects and buyers for one to three years (section 1, part F) [EV-CLP-220] [EV-CLP-224] [EV-CLP-225]. What should replace them, and how should the plan support heavy industry, where neither credits nor a price have yet driven the change?

The legacy design restored every repealed credit (CLI-1A) and then, in the October 26-28 portfolio work, stepped them down as standards take over; that is what the budget model books (CLI-1, $0.757 trillion) [EV-CLP-702] [EV-CLP-716]. Clean-industry contracts (CLI-9, $0.110 trillion) are booked too [EV-CLP-702]. Both are working choices (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`).

## Why it matters

- The credits are the plan's largest climate cost line [EV-CLP-702].
- They are also its least predictable: JCT scored the IRA's energy credits at $271 billion over ten years in 2022 [EV-CLP-200]; by 2024 CBO put them at about $800 billion for 2025-34 [EV-CLP-201]. The legacy carbon model counts its own credit set at about $1.50 trillion, twice the booking [EV-CLP-710].
- They worked: record clean power additions (53 GW in 2025) and investment that went mostly to Republican districts [EV-CLP-209] [EV-CLP-208], and they were repealed within three years [EV-CLP-214].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Uncapped credits build more and cost more than scored** (JCT, CBO, Penn Wharton, Goldman Sachs) [EV-CLP-200] [EV-CLP-201] [EV-CLP-203] [EV-CLP-202].
2. **The investment followed, and fell back after the repeal** (Rhodium and MIT's Clean Investment Monitor; E2) [EV-CLP-206] [EV-CLP-207] [EV-CLP-212].
3. **Clean power is cheaper than new gas before credits** (Lazard, 2026): solar $40-98 and onshore wind $37-99 a megawatt-hour against $51-129 for combined-cycle gas [EV-CLP-456]. Credits now mostly speed deployment and lower bills, rather than make clean power viable.
4. **Contracts for difference protect both sides** (UK record): UK generators paid back a net £346 million in 2022 when prices spiked [EV-CLP-450], and received a record £2.64 billion in 2025 [EV-CLP-451]. Germany's carbon contracts back 15 industrial projects for up to €2.8 billion over 15 years [EV-CLP-452].
5. **Industrial grants were the first cut** (DOE, 2025): the demonstration and hydrogen awards were cancelled [EV-CLP-440] [EV-CLP-439]. Contracts signed with funds obligated at signing are harder to cancel than grants paid over time.

## Part 1. The credits

**A. Restore everything, no step-down (CLI-1A as first written).**
- *Cost:* about $0.9 trillion in the legacy file [EV-CLP-701]; the legacy step-down saved $0.18-0.35 trillion over 2030-39 and about $53 billion in FY2040, so A is roughly $0.94-1.11 trillion and over $100 billion in FY2040 on the booked basis (project arithmetic on [EV-CLP-716] [EV-CLP-702]). The model's high end is $1.5 trillion [EV-CLP-710].
- *What it buys:* the strongest pull on investment for the longest time.

**B. The legacy credit set, stepping down as standards take over (recommended; as booked).**
- *What it does:* starts the credits again from 2030 for power, storage, manufacturing, homes and vehicles; refundable, with direct pay for public power and co-ops and transfer to the dealer or installer at the point of sale; each credit steps down on a published schedule tied to the clean power standard and vehicle standards (memo 3), so the subsidy ends as the rule takes over.
- *For people:* a family buying a heat pump or a used EV gets the credit off the price at the counter, not on next year's tax return.
- *Cost:* $0.757 trillion over FY2030-39 and $55.9 billion in FY2040 (budget model, case `mixed`) [EV-CLP-702].
- *Burden:* for buyers, none beyond the sale; for dealers and installers, a transfer filing; for Treasury, the IRA's existing direct-pay system.
- *Cost risk:* if take-up runs like the IRA's, the cost could be well above the booking [EV-CLP-201] [EV-CLP-710]. **Point 7** adds a review trigger: if the credits' cost runs more than 25% above the booked path for two years, Treasury reports and the step-down schedule moves earlier unless Congress acts.

**C. Power, storage and manufacturing only; households helped through targeted rebates.**
- *What it does:* the technology-neutral power and storage credits and the manufacturing credit return; household and vehicle credits are replaced by point-of-sale rebates for low- and moderate-income households only (income checked from tax records).
- *Cost:* roughly $0.5 trillion over FY2030-39 (project estimate: the household, vehicle and charger credits scored at about $0.19 trillion of savings on the vehicle side and about $0.1 trillion on the household side in the 2025 repeal) [EV-CLP-215] [EV-CLP-216].
- *Burden:* an income test at the point of sale, which is exactly the kind of test the design lens asks to earn its place; it would exclude many middle-income buyers.

**D. No credits; standards and the price carry the transition.**
- *Cost:* saves the $0.757 trillion. Clean building slows in 2030-33, when the plan most needs it, and bills are higher while the standards ramp.

**Why B.** Standards set the destination; credits make the early years cheaper for households and get projects through the queue sooner. A step-down ends the subsidy as the rule takes over, which answers the IRA's cost growth better than a cap would, and point-of-sale delivery reaches people who owe little tax.

## Part 2. Clean industry

**A. Contracts for difference plus Buy Clean (recommended; CLI-9 as booked, with procurement).**
- *What it does:* DOE signs long-term contracts with producers of low-carbon steel, cement, aluminum, chemicals and hydrogen that pay the gap between their cost and a reference price, and claw back when the market pays more, timed to furnace and kiln rebuilds. Funds are obligated when the contract is signed. Federal agencies buy low-carbon materials for federal projects (Buy Clean in statute) [EV-CLP-448] [EV-CLP-447].
- *Cost:* $0.110 trillion over FY2030-39 and $20.5 billion in FY2040, booked at expected net cost (legacy portfolio model) [EV-CLP-702].
- *Burden:* on firms, a competitive bid and verified emissions; on DOE, a contracts office with independent verification.

**B. Credits only** (the manufacturing and industrial credits in part 1). Cheaper to run, but they pay for factories, not for running them clean, and they end at the credit dates.

**C. Neither; the carbon price (memo 1) and the border adjustment carry industry.** No cost; the plan relies on a price path that reaches $150 only in 2037, after most furnace rebuild decisions are made.

**Why A.** Heavy industry is where the price works slowest (the legacy model's largest fee base, $0.82 trillion [EV-CLP-714]), and contracts are the tool that worked in the UK and Germany. With memo 1 B, the same industries pay the price and are protected at the border.

## Consequences

- *A solar developer in Georgia* can plan on the credit for projects starting in 2030-35 and knows the date it steps down.
- *A cement plant in Texas* bids for a contract that pays the gap for a capture or electric kiln, and pays the carbon price on what it still emits.
- *Taxpayers:* the credit line is the plan's largest climate cost and its largest climate cost risk.
