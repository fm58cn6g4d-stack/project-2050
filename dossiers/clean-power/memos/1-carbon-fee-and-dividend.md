# Memo 1: The carbon fee and the dividend

Role: decision memo for Espen, forks 1 and 2 of the clean power dossier (`../3-design.md`). Status: draft, for decision (E-C1). Each part is decided on its own.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **part 1 B, a carbon price on power plants and large industry, with the border adjustment, on the legacy price path ($25 a ton in 2030 rising to $150 by 2037), and no direct charge on home heating fuels or road fuels; part 2 A, the Climate Dividend paid equally to every adult, quarterly, tax-free and named, starting before the price reaches bills, on the same IRS payment rail as the child benefit.** Cost against the booked legacy rows: about **−$0.03 trillion over FY2030-39 and about −$26 billion in FY2040** on the general fund (project arithmetic, range about −$0.10 to +$0.05 trillion), with a dividend about a third smaller. Evidence: measured effects of carbon prices in British Columbia, the EU and Europe's carbon taxes; Canada's PBO and polling on why its rebate was repealed; the legacy carbon model's split of receipts by source (a project estimate).

## The question

How much of the plan's climate policy should ride on a carbon price, who pays it directly, and how the money comes back.

The legacy design (REV-C1A, decided by Espen in the legacy files on October 20-26, 2026, and a working choice now under `DEC-2026-10-30-02`) is an economy-wide upstream fee on coal, natural gas and non-road oil, $25 a ton in 2030 rising to $150 by 2037, with gasoline and diesel exempt; 80% returned equally per adult and 20% to a transition share (legacy climate file) [EV-CLP-700]. The budget model books it today: the dividend and fee net to zero in their own channel, and the general fund bears the income and payroll tax offset on the fee (CX, CXP), about $0.50 trillion over FY2030-39 and $71 billion in FY2040, less the process-emissions bridge (CLI-2O, −$0.09 trillion) (budget model audit lines, case `mixed`).

## Why it matters

- A carbon price is the plan's largest new money flow after taxes: about $1.9 trillion over ten years on the legacy design [EV-CLP-700], or $2.0 trillion on the legacy carbon model [EV-CLP-705].
- It is also the most exposed piece politically. Canada's consumer charge and rebate were repealed in 2025 [EV-CLP-310] [EV-CLP-312]; Australia's price was repealed in 2014 [EV-CLP-343]; France cancelled a scheduled rise after the 2018 protests [EV-CLP-341].
- It decides much of the D3 fairness test: the dividend is what leaves most low- and middle-income households ahead [EV-CLP-707].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Prices cut emissions modestly and do not hurt jobs or growth** (peer-reviewed, several countries): 5-15% in British Columbia (Murray and Rivers review, 2015) [EV-CLP-302], contested as smaller outside transport (Pretis) [EV-CLP-303]; about 10% at EU-regulated plants (Dechezleprêtre and others) [EV-CLP-334]; 4-6% for a $40 tax across European countries (Metcalf and Stock) [EV-CLP-337] [EV-CLP-336].
2. **The price on large emitters did more of the work than the consumer charge** (Canada): the industrial system was expected to deliver 23-48% of Canada's cuts against 8-14% for the consumer charge (Canadian Climate Institute, 2024, as reported by CBC) [EV-CLP-318]; the EU's system on power and industry has cut those sectors 50% since 2005 (European Commission) [EV-CLP-322].
3. **Equal dividends leave most households ahead** (U.S. Treasury 2017; Resources for the Future; the Climate Leadership Council) [EV-CLP-353] [EV-CLP-356] [EV-CLP-358], and Canada's did too on the fiscal count (PBO, 2024) [EV-CLP-313]; counting wider economic effects, the PBO found a small net cost [EV-CLP-314].
4. **People did not believe it** (polling and peer-reviewed surveys): in 2023, 54% of Canadian recipients thought they paid more than they got back while the government said 80% came out ahead (Angus Reid) [EV-CLP-321]; awareness of rebates was low in Canada and Switzerland alike (Mildenberger and others, 2022) [EV-CLP-319].
5. **A border charge abroad now prices U.S. exports** (EU law): the EU's carbon border charge took effect in January 2026, with certificates sold from February 2027 [EV-CLP-333]. A U.S. price on the same industries lets American firms pay at home instead.

## Part 1. How wide the price is

**A. The legacy economy-wide fee (REV-C1A).**
- *What it does:* an upstream fee on coal, gas and non-road oil, $25 a ton in 2030 to $150 by 2037, then 5% a year; road fuels exempt [EV-CLP-700].
- *For people:* home heating gas, electricity and goods all cost more; the dividend comes back. On the legacy model in 2035, the bottom two-fifths come out ahead by about $60-100 a household and the fourth fifth behind by about $240, on the carbon channel alone [EV-CLP-707].
- *Cost:* booked; about $1.90 trillion raised and $1.52 trillion returned over 2030-39 [EV-CLP-700]. The general fund bears the tax offset (about $0.50 trillion, above).
- *Burden:* none for households to apply for; a small number of upstream payers (fuel producers and importers) for the agencies.
- *Political risk:* the highest. A visible charge on every gas bill is the piece that ended Canada's program.

**B. A price on power plants and large industry, with the border adjustment (recommended).**
- *What it does:* the same price path, charged on fuel sold to power plants and on large industrial facilities (and their process emissions), with the border adjustment (REV-C2) on imports of the same goods. Homes, small businesses and road fuels are not charged directly; vehicle, appliance and clean-heat standards and the credits carry those sectors (memos 2 and 3).
- *For people:* no new line on the gas bill or at the pump. Electricity and some goods cost more, and the dividend comes back; it is smaller, about $430 an adult in 2035 instead of about $680 (project arithmetic: the legacy model's $681 [EV-CLP-706] scaled by power and industry's share of receipts).
- *Cost:* on the legacy model, power and industry are $1.26 trillion of the $2.00 trillion the fee raises over 2030-39, about 63% [EV-CLP-714]. On the booked basis that is about $1.2 trillion raised, $0.96 trillion returned and $0.24 trillion for the transition share. The general fund's tax offset falls by about $0.17 trillion; the add-ons lose about $0.14 trillion of their financing. **Net, about −$0.03 trillion over FY2030-39 and about −$26 billion in FY2040** (project arithmetic; range about −$0.10 to +$0.05 trillion, because less covered emissions may abate faster or slower than the model assumes).
- *Burden:* payers are power plants and large facilities, which already measure their emissions; the border adjustment needs product carbon accounting at customs, which the EU now runs.
- *Political risk:* lower. It looks like the parts that survived elsewhere: the EU system and Canada's industrial price.
- *What it gives up:* buildings' emissions are priced only through electricity; the legacy model's buildings receipts ($0.47 trillion) [EV-CLP-714] and the price signal on gas furnaces go. Memo 3's clean-heat standard (point 9) is how buildings are covered instead.

**C. No carbon price.** Standards, credits and public investment carry the cuts.
- *Cost:* the tax offset and process bridge go (about −$0.41 trillion net), but the add-ons lose their $0.38-0.43 trillion of financing, so the general fund is about the same over FY2030-39 and about $70 billion a year better in FY2040 (project arithmetic). No dividend.
- *What it gives up:* the only instrument that raises revenue for the transition; protection for U.S. exporters against the EU charge; and the legacy model's fee-only cut of about 10 points in 2035 [EV-CLP-704].

**D. A, delayed to 2032.** The fee starts after the standards and credits are running and two years of dividends have been paid from a Treasury advance.
- *Cost:* about 8% less revenue over the window (project arithmetic: the legacy model's 2030-31 receipts are about $157 billion of about $2.0 trillion [EV-CLP-705]).
- *What it buys:* time to show the dividend; it keeps every household-facing risk of A.

**Why B.** The evidence says the price on large emitters does most of the price's work, the consumer-facing charge carries most of its political risk, and the plan's household-facing sectors are better covered by standards that do not show up on a bill. B keeps the revenue for the transition and a dividend, and answers the EU's border charge. Its cost: a smaller dividend, and buildings left to a standard.

## Part 2. The dividend

**A. Equal per adult, quarterly, tax-free, named, paid first (CLI-2A; recommended).**
- *What it does:* every adult 18 and over gets one equal share; children are covered by the child benefit instead (`DEC-2026-11-12-06`). It is called the Climate Dividend, paid through the IRS to filers and automatically to Social Security, SSI, VA and Railroad Retirement beneficiaries from their agencies' records, with one simple sign-up for others. The first payment goes out before the price reaches bills. It shares the child benefit's rail and notice, which Espen has already decided (`DEC-2026-11-12-06`, memo 2 B).
- *For people:* a couple gets two shares a quarter, labeled, on the same deposit schedule as the child benefit.
- *Burden:* none for most people; a one-page sign-up for non-filers who are not beneficiaries.

**B. Equal per person, children at half a share** (the 2019 Energy Innovation and Carbon Dividend Act design) [EV-CLP-355] (CLI-2F).
- *What it changes:* more to families with children, less to single adults and retirees; the child benefit already reaches children.

**C. A, but counted as taxable income** (CLI-2B).
- *What it changes:* returns roughly $0.15-0.2 trillion less to households on the legacy design, which comes back to the general fund (legacy estimate) [EV-CLP-722]; higher-income adults keep less. Adds a tax-form line for every filer.

**Why A.** It is already the design the secure floor's payment rail assumes, it is the simplest to deliver, and the visibility lesson from Canada and Switzerland points to a named, separate payment, not a tax credit or a premium offset [EV-CLP-319] [EV-CLP-339].

## Points decided with this memo (section 3, points 2-6)

Road fuels stay outside the price; the methane fee; the border adjustment; the 80/20 split; a dividend account with a permanent appropriation.

## Consequences for real households (2035, on the legacy model, project estimates)

- *A retired couple in Ohio heating with gas:* under A, the fee adds about $90-240 a year to home heating, depending on income (2025 dollars) [EV-CLP-707], and they get two dividend shares, about $1,360 in 2035 (nominal) [EV-CLP-706]; under B, no heating charge and about $860 in dividends, with somewhat higher electricity and goods prices.
- *A single renter in Phoenix:* under A or B, one share; under B, no gas charge, and summer cooling bills carry the power price.
- *A steelworker in Indiana:* under B, the mill pays the price, and imports of steel pay it too at the border.
