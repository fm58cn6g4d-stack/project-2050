## What the runs say

1. **The grid is the binding lever, as the dossier's evidence says.** Every run that adds grid access cuts more than any price or standard change: the full-strength builder about 1.4 Gt over 2030-50 and the permitting package about 2.2 Gt, both with lower bills. The external evidence agrees: transmission must roughly double its growth rate [EV-CLP-400]; the typical project built in 2025 waited about 61 months to connect [EV-CLP-733] and 13% of requests are built [EV-CLP-408]; about 435 miles of 345 kV and above were built in 2025 [EV-CLP-734]. The engine's own note: grid access, not price, limits wind and solar (SRC-0077).
2. **When the grid binds, the standard is mostly a fee.** Its payments ($163 billion on the decided set) are large and its added cut small, because suppliers cannot connect the clean power the standard asks for. The cap's level barely moves carbon: $20, $40 or $60 all give 41% in 2035, within 0.1 points in 2040, and within about 0.1 Gt over 2030-50, while payments run $85, $163 or $229 billion and the 2040 price 15.8, 16.1 or 16.3 cents. The payments fall as the grid grows: to $93 billion with the full builder and $74 billion with permitting. That pointed at using the payments to build the grid that ends them: **memo 9**, decided B.
3. **More builder helps, with steadily smaller returns per dollar on the model.** From the decided half-strength builder, 75% adds about $68 billion of credits for about 0.75 Gt; full strength adds about $135 billion for about 1.43 Gt: about **$90-95 a ton** either way (project arithmetic on nominal dollars and undiscounted tons), against EPA's 2023 social cost of carbon of $230 a ton in 2030 (2020 dollars; SRC-5006). Bills are up to 0.5 cents a kWh lower in 2040. These are upper bounds: see "What the engine assumes about the builder" below.
4. **The economy-wide price (memo 1 A, not chosen)** cuts about 1.36 Gt more over 2030-50, mostly after 2035, with about $840 billion more receipts and a heating charge on households. Espen chose B (`DEC-2026-11-13-06`); nothing here reopens it.
5. **The methane fee is the cheapest tonne in the set:** it lifts net greenhouse gases from 41.0% to 42.8% below 2005 in 2035 and raises money (about $22 billion). Kept as decided.
6. **The standard's cap could be lower without losing carbon, on the model.** A $20 cap gives the same cut, a lower 2040 price (15.8 cents) and $78 billion less in payments. Under memo 9 B the payments now fund the grid account, and the research below finds money is rarely what binds, so a lower cap would mostly reduce receipts. Not put to Espen: the cap was decided in memo 3, the model's standard is crude (one national credit, no banking), and the cap also guards against shortfalls the model can't see. Recorded as a finding to revisit at the next model version.

## What the engine assumes about the builder

The builder is the engine's federal grid program at a fraction of full strength (`assumptions.csv`, `grid_standby_scale`, 0.5, range 0.25-1.0; `engine/cfd-model.js` line 43: +0.8 points a year of transmission growth, +15 GW a year of reconductoring access and $12 billion a year at full strength). Five features make every builder run an upper bound:
- **It ramps to full effect by 2032** (line 460: a three-year ramp from 2030), faster than any real builder could site a line.
- **It adds linearly on top of permitting** (lines 461 and 464: its transmission growth and access are summed with the permitting package's, with no overlap), so the builder-plus-permitting gain is overstated.
- **It credits all 15 GW a year of reconductoring to the builder** (line 464), though most reconductoring would be done by incumbent utilities on their own systems once paid for.
- **It has no trigger and no supply limit:** it builds everywhere every year, not only where the one-year trigger is met, and never waits on transformers, crews or siting.
- **Its $12 billion a year is booked as a federal cost** (line 515, counted in the credits), though under CLI-10.02 and CLI-10.03 the builder's capital is borrowed and repaid through rates.

So `bld_hi`'s −1.43 Gt is the most a builder could add on this engine, not a forecast. M4 has been asked for a run with the builder at full strength together with the permitting package, to bound the overlap.

## How transmission actually gets built

**Already in the repository.** The legacy work reached the main conclusion before this step, and this section builds on it rather than re-deriving it:
- *Siting authority and planning bind, not money alone.* SRC-0155's lever table gives transmission funding or a tax credit 30-40% of its 2035 potential without the permitting bill and 85-95% with it: "money plus siting authority; money alone failed in the IRA era", so "pair money with planning". Interconnection queues are "the first wall for solar and storage", and equipment is "a separate bottleneck that permitting reform doesn't solve" (SRC-0156).
- *The permitting bill is the biggest lever.* SRC-0156's scenarios give 345 kV+ build of 500-1,000 miles a year in 2030-35 without the bill and 1,500-2,500 with it (3,000-5,000 with a climate law as well); the bill has FERC "shall issue" permits for lines of 230 kV and up, drops DOE's corridor step and lets planned lines satisfy the backstop findings; without it, "backstop siting stays dead without DOE corridors" and "interregional lines remain the exception" (SRC-0155). Passage odds about 22% (SRC-0155) and 27% (SRC-0156) in the lame duck.
- *Public power builds with tools it already has.* Legacy CLI-10 gives TVA, Bonneville and WAPA large new borrowing authority and a TVA-model corporation "that builds where no regional builder exists, using existing staff, balance sheets and eminent domain" (`legacy/domains/domain-climate.md`, CLI-10); that eminent domain is the public builders' own (TVA, 16 U.S.C. 831c(h) and 831x; Bonneville's acquisition authority, 16 U.S.C. 832a, with 838c limiting condemnation of operating lines), not FPA 216's. Legacy CLI-6D, "Congress permits the backbone" (a statutory list of lines deemed approved), is the strongest speed lever, kept as a floor option.
- *Reconductoring and grid-enhancing technologies* are the fast lever on existing corridors (SRC-0155, SRC-0156; legacy CLI-6), and legacy CLI-5 puts the 45X credit on transformers and switchgear for the equipment bottleneck.

**What is new since the legacy work**, and where it changes a legacy conclusion:
- **The legacy work assumed the permitting bill is enacted; the dossier does not.** `legacy/domains/domain-climate.md` stipulates "AAJA is enacted in 2026-28" (Dec. D1), and legacy CLI-6 counts FERC backstop siting as "already in the stipulated law". The dossier plans for both cases (CLI-3A.03, CLI-3A.04), so any piece of the design that leans on backstop siting needs a path if the bill fails. This is the change behind the CLI-10.03 fix in this PR.
- **The manager's amendment changes one legacy reading.** SRC-0155 read the bill as banning a federal right of first refusal; the November 7 substitute preserves utilities' existing right of first refusal, exempts certain vertically integrated utilities from the new planning duties, and has developers go to the state for a year before federal siting [EV-CLP-263] (a law firm's summary). The CRS section summary confirms §2101 rewrites FPA 216 and §2103 lets planned lines meet the permit criteria (SRC-4532), but does not describe the corridor, voltage, state-clock or eminent-domain details, so those still rest on SRC-0155's reading of the earlier text [EV-CLP-740] [EV-CLP-741] [EV-CLP-742].
- **Independent estimates now bracket SRC-0156's range.** On the September 30 text, Rhodium finds interregional build of 1.9-2.6 TW-miles a year against 0.7 and up to 1.5 Gt less CO2 [EV-CLP-743] [EV-CLP-744]; RFF finds about 30% more transmission by 2041 and about 1.8 Gt less [EV-CLP-745]. Both are non-government analyses with no stake in the bill's passage; they support the legacy ordering (the bill outweighs money).
- **The build record got worse.** About 435 miles of 345 kV and above were built in 2025 [EV-CLP-734] (the same firm's congestion report says 411 [EV-CLP-735]), down from 888 in 2024 [EV-CLP-051] that the legacy work used. Projects built in 2025 waited about 61 months in interconnection queues [EV-CLP-733], consistent with the legacy "over 5 years". No project has come from FERC Order 1000's interregional process since 2011 [EV-CLP-746].
- **The corridors are gone.** DOE cancelled its three remaining national-interest corridors on August 12, 2026 (already noted in SRC-0155 and SRC-0156), citing in part a "lack of expressed developer interest in seeking FERC backstop siting authority" [EV-CLP-736] [EV-CLP-737] (second Trump administration; the cancellation is a fact, the reason its view). FPA 216's permit and its eminent domain (216(e)) need a corridor (SRC-4771), so without the bill there is no federal siting backstop at all.
- **Federal money has been offered and mostly not used, which strengthens the legacy "money alone failed".** WAPA's Transmission Infrastructure Program has $3.25 billion of borrowing authority (42 U.S.C. 16421a) and has financed two lines in seventeen years (the Montana-Alberta Tie Line in 2013 and ED5-Palo Verde in 2015) [EV-CLP-738] [EV-CLP-739]. DOE's Transmission Facilitation Program is nearly fully obligated (legacy `climate-portfolio-allocation.md`); the 2025 reconciliation law (OBBBA) rescinded the unobligated balances of IRA §§50151 to 50153 (transmission lending, siting grants and interregional planning) [EV-CLP-754].
- **Regional plans are funded; execution binds.** MISO's Tranche 2.1 ($21.8 billion, 2032-34), SPP's 2025 plan ($8.6 billion, including about 949 miles of 765 kV), PJM's 2025 plan ($11.8 billion) and ERCOT's 765 kV backbone ($20-33 billion, depending on scope) are approved and paid through rates; over 10,000 miles of 500 kV and above are planned by 2034, most in 2030-34 [EV-CLP-751] [EV-CLP-752] [EV-CLP-753] [EV-CLP-759]. In these regions a federal builder would duplicate.
- **Equipment is shorter than the legacy work knew.** Power transformers ran about 30% short in 2025, with about 80% imported [EV-CLP-747]; McKinsey puts Europe and North America's combined 2025 deficit at 38% [EV-CLP-748].
- **Merchant lines show the time it takes.** SunZia took almost two decades of permitting, planning and financing (SRC-0156 said about 17 years) and reached commercial operation in 2026 [EV-CLP-756]; the Champlain Hudson Power Express (339 miles) entered service in May 2026 under a New York contract [EV-CLP-757]; Grain Belt Express lost its DOE guarantee in 2025 [EV-CLP-435], won at the Illinois Supreme Court in January 2026 on a narrow question (the financing condition), and has a federal permitting target of January 2027 [EV-CLP-758].
- **Public power owns a large share already.** The power marketing administrations alone own about 14% of US transmission [EV-CLP-749], before TVA's; Bonneville's grid expansion and reinforcement portfolio is nearly $8 billion across 20-plus projects [EV-CLP-750].
- **The model adds a bound.** On MOD-energy the full builder is worth up to about 1.4 Gt and the permitting package about 2.2 Gt over 2030-50 (above), the same ordering as the legacy work and the outside estimates.

**Where a builder adds, and where it duplicates.** A federal builder of last resort adds most on cross-seam and interregional lines (DOE's July 2026 draft needs study values ERCOT ties at $31-48/MWh and SPP-WestConnect at $18-33/MWh [EV-CLP-755]), in the non-RTO Southeast and West, on the public power systems' own lines (reconductoring), and as anchor offtaker for stranded merchant lines. It duplicates in the RTO regions and ERCOT, where $70 billion and more is approved and execution binds; there it competes for transformers and crews, and Treasury-rate financing could make "last resort" the default. That is why memo 9's account pays first for reconductoring, risk cover, equipment and siting grants, and only for ready-to-build work, and why the trigger and competitive-cost test stay.

**The builder's siting path, corrected in CLI-10.03.** The specification said the builder "uses the existing federal siting backstops outside ERCOT, not new eminent domain". Under current law there is no working federal backstop: FPA 216's permits and their eminent domain (216(e)) reach only lines inside a DOE corridor (SRC-4771), and none is left [EV-CLP-736] [EV-CLP-737]. The bill as introduced (S.5653) strikes the corridor language from 216(b), has FERC "issue" permits for lines of 230 kV and up once a state has held an application for a year, and keeps 216(e)(1)'s eminent domain for permit holders, changing only its court procedure (§2101(b) and (d)) [EV-CLP-740] [EV-CLP-741] [EV-CLP-742]; the November substitute keeps it too, and by striking "or a State" in 216(e)(1) would also reach state-owned land; both versions strike 216(f)'s fair-market-value compensation rule, leaving the Constitution's just-compensation requirement (the substitute read from a trade association's copy, to confirm against congress.gov) [EV-CLP-741] [EV-CLP-742]. So CLI-10.03 now names the three paths: TVA's and Bonneville's own condemnation powers in their regions; FERC's permits if the bill passes; and if it fails, CLI-3A.04's backstop (which must reach listed lines without a corridor) or legacy CLI-6D, Congress listing the lines itself. Two older inconsistencies this settles: legacy CLI-10 relies on "eminent domain" (the public builders' own) while CLI-10.03 said "not new eminent domain"; and the legacy domain assumes the bill is enacted, which the dossier dropped. **Open, for Espen with the next batch:** the new eastern corporation has no condemnation power unless its charter grants one. Granting it (as TVA has) makes the standby builder able to act where states and FERC cannot; withholding it keeps the builder to willing-seller and state-sited routes. Not decided here.

## Limits

- The builder is a labeled assumption with the five generous features above. How fast a real builder can site lines is the open question [EV-CLP-435] [EV-CLP-437].
- Two of the model's benchmarks fail (HI-1, PR-2; `6-cost.md`, part 6, 1a): its near-term power cuts and early receipts lean high. The grid lever acts on wind and solar access rather than coal-to-gas switching, so the comparison between runs is less exposed than the levels.
- The household split of memo 9's options is not modeled: the household module reads the price, not where the payments go.
- M4's open #366 changes the engine; these figures are on main at `37ee069` and are refreshed if it moves them.
