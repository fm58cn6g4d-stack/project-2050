# 1. The problem

Role: section 1 of the clean power dossier: what is wrong, for whom and how large. Status: draft (step E-C1). Every figure cites `evidence.json`; grade-D entries (EV-CLP-700 on) restate legacy project estimates and are labeled as ours.

**In one paragraph.** Electricity is getting more expensive, faster than prices generally since 2022, and the households that can least afford it already spend about three times the share of income others do. Demand is rising for the first time in a generation, driven mostly by data centers, while new power and transmission wait years to connect, and grid operators now warn of shortfalls in several regions by the end of the decade. U.S. emissions stopped falling in 2024 and rose in 2025; after the 2025 reconciliation law repealed most of the Inflation Reduction Act's clean-energy credits, independent projections put 2035 well short of the country's own target. Meanwhile disasters cost well over $100 billion in most recent years, home insurance is getting costlier and harder to buy in the places most at risk, and the federal programs that help people prepare were cut back in 2025. The workers and towns that depend on coal, and soon on gas and oil, face the transition with little to catch them. Each of these hits low- and middle-income households hardest.

**Outcome served.** OUT-05, "Clean, affordable power and a prepared country" (`charter/outcomes.md`), whose measures are net greenhouse emissions, household energy burden, disaster and uninsured losses, and employment in transition regions. Parts A to E below follow those measures. The dossier also serves OUT-02 (a shock no longer sets off a chain of losses: disasters, shutoffs and insurance shocks are such shocks), OUT-04 (a home people can keep: insurance and disaster recovery) and OUT-11 (the fairness and fiscal tests: the carbon fee and dividend are the plan's largest household-incidence question outside taxes).

## A. Power bills and energy hardship

**Prices are rising.**
- The average residential electricity price rose from 13.15 cents a kilowatt-hour in 2020 to 17.23 cents in 2025, about 31% [EV-CLP-001]. EIA forecasts 18.2 cents in 2026 and 18.7 cents in 2027 [EV-CLP-002]. EIA itself said in 2025 that retail prices had risen faster than inflation since 2022 [EV-CLP-003].
- An independent review by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) and the Brattle Group found that national prices had largely tracked inflation through 2024 and rose 4.4% (1.9% after inflation) in the first seven months of 2025, with very different trends by state [EV-CLP-006] [EV-CLP-007]. Prices range from 8.20 cents in North Dakota to 35.72 cents in Hawaii across all sectors [EV-CLP-004]. The national energy assistance directors' association (NEADA), an advocate for assistance programs, puts the rise in the average monthly electric bill since 2021 at 29.2% [EV-CLP-005].
- Natural gas, which sets the power price in most hours, rose from $2.19 per million Btu at Henry Hub in 2024 to $3.53 in 2025; EIA expects it to ease to $2.88 in 2027 [EV-CLP-008]. NEADA links part of the rise to expanding liquefied gas exports [EV-CLP-009].

**Who is hurt.**
- Low-income households spend about 6% of income on home energy, three times the 2% others spend, by the Department of Energy's LEAD tool; about 51 million households are low-income by its definition [EV-CLP-034]. ACEEE's estimate, on older (2017) data, is 8.1% against 2.3% [EV-CLP-033].
- In Census survey data for December 2022 to September 2024, 22.4% of adults reported being unable to pay an energy bill in full, 22.0% kept their home at an unsafe temperature, and about a third went without necessities to pay energy bills (a peer-reviewed analysis) [EV-CLP-031]. Among households earning under $50,000 the share unable to pay was 37.4% [EV-CLP-032].
- NEADA estimates that one household in six was behind on energy bills in December 2025, owing $23 billion in all [EV-CLP-028], and that shutoffs rose from 3.0 million in 2023 to 3.5 million in 2024 [EV-CLP-029]. No official national count of shutoffs exists [EV-CLP-030], which is itself a gap.

**The help that exists reaches few and is under threat.** The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) reached an estimated 5.9 million households in FY2024, about 17% of those eligible [EV-CLP-038]; in the past its reach has ranged from 14% to 22%, rising only when funding rose [EV-CLP-039]. Congress funded it at about $4 billion for FY2026 [EV-CLP-035] [EV-CLP-036]. The second Trump administration laid off the staff who ran it in April 2025 and has proposed eliminating it in each of its budgets [EV-CLP-035] [EV-CLP-036].

**Data centers and bills: two views.** LBNL and Brattle found that over 2019-24 states with faster load growth generally saw real prices fall, because fixed grid costs were spread over more sales [EV-CLP-025], but warn that this can reverse now that growth is accelerating and new supply and wires are scarce and costly [EV-CLP-026]. A Harvard Law School review of 40 state proceedings found special contracts for data centers are often approved with little scrutiny and argues they can shift costs to other customers [EV-CLP-027]. In the PJM market (13 states and Washington, D.C.), the cost of reserving capacity jumped from $28.92 to $269.92 a megawatt-day between the 2024/25 and 2025/26 delivery years and then hit the price cap [EV-CLP-019] [EV-CLP-020]; PJM's own market monitor attributes $9.3 billion of the 2025/26 cost, about 64% of it, to data center load [EV-CLP-024], and PJM expected bills for some customers to rise 1.5-5% from one auction alone [EV-CLP-023]. The register has no independent national estimate of how much data centers add to household bills (see "Evidence still needed").

## B. Demand and reliability

**Demand is rising fast, and forecasts are uncertain.**
- Data centers used about 4.4% of U.S. electricity in 2023; LBNL projects 6.7% to 12% by 2028, a range that shows how uncertain the outlook is [EV-CLP-010] [EV-CLP-011] [EV-CLP-012].
- Utilities' five-year forecasts of peak growth rose from 24 GW to 166 GW in three years, with data centers about 55% of it [EV-CLP-013]; Grid Strategies judges the data center part overstated by about 25 GW [EV-CLP-014]. NERC, the reliability organization, forecasts summer peak demand rising by over 224 GW in ten years [EV-CLP-015].

**Supply is not keeping up.**
- NERC rates the Midwest grid (MISO) at high risk of shortfall from 2028, and PJM, Texas (ERCOT) and the Northwest from 2029 [EV-CLP-016]. PJM's last capacity auction came in 6,623 MW short of its reliability requirement [EV-CLP-021], and PJM attributes nearly all of the increase in its forecast peak to data centers [EV-CLP-022].
- About 2,290 GW of projects, mostly solar, wind and batteries, waited in interconnection queues at the end of 2024; the typical project built in 2024 took 55 months from request to operation, against 22 months in 2008, and only 13% of capacity that asked to connect in 2000-19 was ever built [EV-CLP-047] [EV-CLP-048] [EV-CLP-049].
- High-voltage transmission construction has slowed: about 536 miles a year in 2020-24 against 1,781 in 2010-14 [EV-CLP-051] [EV-CLP-050]. Large transformers take three years or more to arrive [EV-CLP-052] [EV-CLP-053].

**Outages are getting longer.** Customers averaged 11 hours without power in 2024, nearly twice the previous decade's average, mostly from major storms [EV-CLP-040] [EV-CLP-041]. Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 forced the largest controlled blackout in U.S. history, left more than 4.5 million Texans without power and killed 246 people by the state's count, or about 814 by an excess-mortality estimate [EV-CLP-044] [EV-CLP-042] [EV-CLP-043]; frozen equipment and fuel failures caused three-quarters of the plant outages [EV-CLP-045]. Winter Storm Elliott in December 2022 forced 5,400 MW of load shedding [EV-CLP-046].

## C. Emissions

- **Where emissions are.** Net U.S. greenhouse emissions were 5,257 million metric tons in 2023, about 20% lower than twenty years earlier [EV-CLP-101] [EV-CLP-100]. After 2024 was flat (about 20% below 2005), they rose 2.4% in 2025 to 18% below 2005, led by buildings and a 13% rise in coal generation (Rhodium Group's preliminary estimates) [EV-CLP-108] [EV-CLP-109] [EV-CLP-110].
- **The official record has stopped.** EPA did not publish the national inventory in 2025 or 2026, citing the Paris withdrawal; the 2025 inventory became public only through a records request, and researchers at the University of Maryland now produce one on EPA's methods [EV-CLP-103] [EV-CLP-104] [EV-CLP-105] [EV-CLP-107]. OUT-05's first measure therefore needs an independent source until the inventory resumes.
- **Where they are heading.** The U.S. target submitted in 2024 is 61-66% below 2005 by 2035 [EV-CLP-116]; the United States left the Paris Agreement on January 27, 2026 [EV-CLP-117]. Rhodium's projections fell from 38-56% below 2005 in 2035 (2024) to 26-35% (2025) after the 2025 law [EV-CLP-112], or 27-44% with its wider range [EV-CLP-113]. A peer-reviewed review puts the 2035 outcome at 39-48% with the IRA intact and lower after the 2025 law [EV-CLP-115]; the Princeton REPEAT project estimated the law adds about 470 million tons a year by 2035 [EV-CLP-114]. Rhodium's 2026 outlook, on policy as of June 2026, gives 27-41% below 2005 in 2040 [EV-CLP-111].
- **On our own model.** The legacy carbon model's current-law path, which assumed a permitting deal, gives energy CO2 29% below 2005 in 2035 [EV-CLP-704], inside the independent ranges. Every published path falls short of the 2035 target: by about 17 points at the most hopeful end of the ranges and by 25 to 35 points in most.

## D. Climate damage and insurance

**Damage.**
- NOAA counted 27 disasters of at least $1 billion in 2024, costing $182.7 billion and killing at least 568 people, and 403 since 1980 costing more than $2.9 trillion [EV-CLP-120] [EV-CLP-121]. NOAA retired the database in May 2025 [EV-CLP-122]; Climate Central, an independent research group, has continued it, counting 24 costing $118.5 billion in 2025 [EV-CLP-124] [EV-CLP-125], an average of $153 billion a year in 2020-24 against $23 billion in the 1980s [EV-CLP-128]. The 2023 National Climate Assessment put direct damages at $150 billion a year or more [EV-CLP-127]; the administration dismissed the authors of the next assessment in 2025 [EV-CLP-129].
- Heat killed 2,391 people in 2024 by CDC's count [EV-CLP-126].

**Insurance is getting costlier and scarcer where risk is highest.**
- Home insurance rose from $1,902 to $2,530 a year on average in 2020-23, 13% after inflation, and the premium gap between riskier and safer ZIP codes widened as reinsurance costs doubled (Keys and Mulder, NBER) [EV-CLP-140] [EV-CLP-141]. The Treasury's Federal Insurance Office found premiums rose 8.7% faster than inflation in 2018-22, the riskiest fifth of ZIP codes paid 82% more, and their non-renewal rate ran about 80% higher [EV-CLP-142] [EV-CLP-143] [EV-CLP-144]. Estimates of the increase since 2019 differ by source, from 31% (First Street) to about 70% (Dallas Fed) [EV-CLP-152] [EV-CLP-149].
- When private insurers leave, state insurers of last resort fill in: California's FAIR Plan grew 157% to 696,562 policies between September 2022 and June 2026 [EV-CLP-147]; Florida's Citizens held 769,316 in September 2025 [EV-CLP-148]. Dallas Fed economists estimate rising premiums already push tens of thousands of mortgages into delinquency a year [EV-CLP-150], which is how insurance becomes a housing and banking problem.
- Flood is mostly uninsured: FEMA estimates about 4% of homeowners carry flood insurance [EV-CLP-157]. The National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) covers nearly 4.5 million policies [EV-CLP-156], owes Treasury $22.525 billion after a $16 billion cancellation in 2017 [EV-CLP-154], has needed 36 short-term extensions since 2017 and is authorized only to December 11, 2026 [EV-CLP-608]. Its move to risk-based rates raised about two-thirds of premiums in the first year and will take until about 2037 to finish [EV-CLP-159].

**The federal help to prepare was cut.** FEMA ended its main pre-disaster mitigation program (BRIC) in April 2025; a court found that unlawful in December 2025, and only $1 billion has been offered since [EV-CLP-601] [EV-CLP-602] [EV-CLP-603]. Of $4.6 billion BRIC made available for FY2020-23, about $1 billion had been obligated by March 2026 [EV-CLP-600]. The administration's FEMA Review Council (May 2026) proposes shifting more cost to states and moving some flood policies to private insurers [EV-CLP-160] [EV-CLP-614] [EV-CLP-615]; most of that needs Congress. Only about a third of hazard-prone jurisdictions have adopted current hazard-resistant building codes [EV-CLP-613].

## E. Workers and places in transition

- **Who works where.** Coal mines employed 44,060 people in 2024 [EV-CLP-500]. The energy sector employs 8.5 million, including about 1.05 million in fuels and 934,000 in power generation [EV-CLP-502]; clean energy is now about 44% of energy jobs [EV-CLP-503], with a higher union share than the private sector as a whole (11.7% against 6.7%) [EV-CLP-504].
- **Closures hurt for years.** Coal workers most exposed to the industry's decline lost earnings equal to 1.6 years of pay (Census and university economists) [EV-CLP-516]; British coal miners displaced in the 1980s earned 80-90% less a year later and were still behind 15 years on [EV-CLP-517]. Canadian evidence is more hopeful, with median earnings recovering within three years [EV-CLP-518].
- **Places lose their tax base.** In 79 energy-producing counties studied by Resources for the Future, fossil fuels supplied 90% of energy-related local revenue [EV-CLP-513]; in Rosebud County, Montana, the Colstrip plant and mine were about $76 million of $95 million of taxable property [EV-CLP-514]. Fossil fuels raise about $138 billion a year for governments in all [EV-CLP-515].
- **The federal response was dismantled.** The interagency working group for coal and power plant communities was abolished in January 2025 [EV-CLP-520]. The Appalachian Regional Commission's POWER grants ($518 million in 605 projects since 2015) have never been independently evaluated [EV-CLP-523] [EV-CLP-525].
- **Retirements are being held back, at a cost.** Operators retired 2.6 GW of coal in 2025 against 8.5 GW planned [EV-CLP-506]. The Department of Energy's emergency orders to keep plants open cost ratepayers about $313 million for one Michigan plant so far by an advocacy group's tracker [EV-CLP-511], and could cost $3.1-5.9 billion a year by 2028 if extended to every large retiring plant (a Grid Strategies scenario) [EV-CLP-512] [EV-CLP-509].
- **Where the new jobs went.** The first year of IRA clean investment went disproportionately to energy communities (36.8% of investment against 18.6% of population) [EV-CLP-531], and 74% of clean manufacturing investment since 2000 is in Republican districts [EV-CLP-533]; $20.8 billion of planned factories were cancelled in 2025 [EV-CLP-532].

## G. The legacy record, restated

The legacy climate file (`legacy/domains/domain-climate.md`) and its models carry a full design, most of it already booked in the budget model. Its figures are restated in the register as project estimates (grade D), not as evidence that the design works:
- the fee and dividend: $25 a ton in 2030 rising to $150 by 2037, road fuels exempt, about $1.90 trillion over 2030-39, $1.52 trillion returned per adult [EV-CLP-700]; the carbon model's own estimate is $2.00 trillion [EV-CLP-705];
- the credits: about $0.9 trillion before the October redesign [EV-CLP-701], booked at $0.757 trillion after it [EV-CLP-702];
- the model's outcomes: 42% below 2005 in 2035 with the full package [EV-CLP-704], short of the 61-66% target, and a household table in which the bottom two fifths come out ahead on the carbon channel alone and the top three fall behind [EV-CLP-707].

Section 3 traces each inherited assumption and says whether Espen decided it. Two legacy foundations no longer hold as written: the "stipulated" permitting law is still a bill (part F), and the credits the legacy file "restores" were repealed on dates that pass before 2029, so restoring them is now a fresh start, not a continuation.

**Left out of section 1 on purpose.** Legacy figures without a traceable source (the durability session's sector judgments, the Talarico scenario's insurance-collapse figures) are not restated here; section 3 cites them only as labeled session or scenario estimates where a memo needs them.

## Evidence still needed

- An independent national estimate of how much data center load adds to household bills.
- An official national count of utility shutoffs; a Census successor to the Household Pulse energy questions.
- HHS's LIHEAP report for FY2024-25.
- A 2035 range from Rhodium's Taking Stock 2026; Energy Innovation's emissions projection for the 2025 law.
- A U.S.-only share of uninsured disaster losses (the register has North America only, [EV-CLP-161]).
- Colorado's coal securitization record; an independent study of local fiscal effects of plant closures beyond RFF's.
