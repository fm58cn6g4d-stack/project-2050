## In brief

- **Wage floors: the evidence covers floors up to about 60% of the local median wage well, and higher ones thinly.**
  - Reviews of the U.S. and U.K. research find small employment effects at the levels studied: up to about 59% of the median in U.S. state studies and 81% in county studies (Dube's review for HM Treasury) [EV-WRK-050] [EV-WRK-051]. Godøy and Reich find no detectable loss in low-wage counties at ratios as high as 0.82 (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-063].
  - The other view: Neumark and Shirley's review finds most estimates negative [EV-WRK-034]. Clemens and Wither find the 2007-09 federal increases cut low-skilled employment during the recession [EV-WRK-064]. Seattle's $13 step cut low-wage hours 6-7% on one team's estimate [EV-WRK-060], against no loss in food service on another's [EV-WRK-061].
  - The United Kingdom reached two-thirds of the median wage in 2024-26, through an expert commission, with no significant job loss on that commission's judgment [EV-WRK-053] [EV-WRK-054]. The U.S. federal floor is 24.09% of the median, against 61.85% in the United Kingdom (OECD) [EV-WRK-057].
  - Germany's 2015 floor raised wages and moved workers to better firms without lowering employment (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-058]. A second study finds a 1.7% loss in affected establishments [EV-WRK-059].
- **Prices and poverty.** Grocery prices fully absorb the cost, 0.36% for a 10% increase (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-065]. Dube finds poverty falls [EV-WRK-066]; Neumark and Wascher's older work finds gains and losses among low-income families that roughly offset [EV-WRK-076].
- **Subminimums.** Evidence on ending the tipped wage is contested and thin on both sides [EV-WRK-067] [EV-WRK-069]. Ending 14(c) raised employment of people with cognitive disabilities in New Hampshire but showed no significant change in Maryland [EV-WRK-074]. GAO found fewer than half of former 14(c) workers tracked in two states had moved to other jobs [EV-WRK-073].
- **Unions.** Unions raise pay and narrow inequality across the twentieth century (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-164]. At the margin of a close election, effects on firms are disputed: near zero on one design [EV-WRK-161], payroll and survival down on another [EV-WRK-162], pay up mainly through benefits on a third [EV-WRK-163].
- **Organizing rules: Canada is the closest test.**
  - Certification success fell 19 points when British Columbia moved from card check to mandatory votes, and rose back when card check returned (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-168].
  - First-contract arbitration cut first-agreement strikes by at least half and was rarely used [EV-WRK-169].
  - On replacement bans the studies disagree [EV-WRK-170] [EV-WRK-171].
- **Right-to-work laws lower unionization;** whether they lower wages is contested [EV-WRK-165] [EV-WRK-166] [EV-WRK-187].
- **Sectoral standards.** Coordinated and sectoral bargaining abroad goes with higher employment and narrower pay gaps (OECD, cross-country association) [EV-WRK-173]. U.S. wage boards bind only where a law gives them the power, and New Zealand's sectoral law was repealed a year after it began, with no agreement finalised [EV-WRK-175] [EV-WRK-178].
- **Enforcement.** Randomized safety inspections in California cut injuries 9.4% with no detectable job loss (randomized) [EV-WRK-249]. One OSHA press release did as much for nearby firms' compliance as 210 inspections (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-251]. Complaint-driven enforcement misses the worst violators [EV-WRK-253].
- **Heat.** California's 2005 standard cut hot-day injuries 15-27% by industry (quasi-experimental, working paper) [EV-WRK-258].
- **Unemployment insurance.** More generous benefits lengthen joblessness somewhat [EV-WRK-368], but the pandemic's large supplements barely slowed job finding (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-370] [EV-WRK-371]. Longer benefits protect spending more than higher ones [EV-WRK-372]. Pandemic fraud ran to $100 billion to $135 billion on GAO's estimate [EV-WRK-378].
- **Getting people back to work.** Reemployment assessments shorten claims about a week (randomized) [EV-WRK-380]. Intensive job-search help raised earnings in the national workforce experiment, while training funded through the same system showed no gain within 30 months (randomized) [EV-WRK-385] [EV-WRK-386]. Sector-focused training raised earnings 11 to 40 percent (randomized; secure floor's register) [EV-SEC-143].
- **Failures to avoid.** England's apprenticeship levy was followed by a 26% fall in starts [EV-WRK-387]. Italy's short-time work saved jobs in firms that were going to shrink anyway [EV-WRK-383].

## Part A. Wage floors

### A.1 How high a floor can go: the bite

The legacy design (LAB-4A) is $17, then two-thirds of the national median wage, with a county circuit breaker at about 60% of the local median [EV-WRK-700]. The question the evidence can answer is how employment responds as the floor rises relative to local wages (its "bite").

| Finding | Who and when | Strength; where | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Research points to a "very muted" employment effect; room for a floor of 60% to two-thirds of the median | Dube, for HM Treasury, 2019 | Government-commissioned review of U.S., U.K. and other evidence | [EV-WRK-050] |
| Small effects up to about 59% of the median in U.S. state studies, up to 81% in county studies; modest in the seven highest-wage states, where the floor binds for about 17% of workers | Same | Same | [EV-WRK-051] |
| No detectable loss of jobs, hours or weeks in low-wage counties at ratios as high as 0.82; household and child poverty down | Godøy and Reich, 2021 | Peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental; U.S. counties | [EV-WRK-063] |
| 138 state increases: low-wage job counts unchanged after five years | Cengiz and others, 2019 | Peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental (section 1) | [EV-WRK-033] |
| 79.2% of estimated employment elasticities negative; stronger for teens and the less educated | Neumark and Shirley, 2022 | Review (counts of estimates, not weighted by quality) | [EV-WRK-034] |
| The 2007-09 federal increases cut employment and income growth of low-skilled workers in bound states, lowering employment rates there by at least half a point | Clemens and Wither, 2019 | Peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental; a recession | [EV-WRK-064] |
| Seattle's $13 step: low-wage hours down 6-7%, pay down $74 a month per job; restaurants zero | Jardim and others (University of Washington), 2018 | Quasi-experimental working paper; one city | [EV-WRK-060] |
| Seattle up to $13: food-service wages up, no detectable job loss | Reich and others (Berkeley), 2017 | Synthetic control; restaurants only | [EV-WRK-061] |
| $15 nationally: 1.3 million to 1.4 million fewer jobs (median), 0.9 million to 1.3 million fewer in poverty | CBO, 2019 and 2021 | Official projection (section 1) | [EV-WRK-029] [EV-WRK-032] |
| California's $20 fast-food wage: no loss (two studies) against 2.7% to 3.2% fewer jobs (one) | Section 1 | Working papers | [EV-WRK-153] [EV-WRK-154] [EV-WRK-155] |

**Abroad.**
- **The United Kingdom** is the one large economy to reach two-thirds of the median.
  - Its commission recommended £12.71 from April 2026 to meet the two-thirds target [EV-WRK-053]. It judged that recent increases had no significant negative effect on employment [EV-WRK-054]. Real pay of the lowest-paid rose 20% from 2016 to 2025, against 4% for the median job [EV-WRK-056].
  - The qualifications: the commission expects rising risk with more ambitious targets or less time, and employers oppose further targets [EV-WRK-055] [EV-WRK-056]. The U.K. now ranks 7th of 31 OECD countries, so there are fewer places left to learn from [EV-WRK-055].
  - *Strength:* official data and the commission's own judgment, which the Dube review supports [EV-WRK-050]. No independent causal study of the 2024-26 steps is in the register.
- **Germany's 2015 floor** covered 15% of employees.
  - Dustmann and others find wages rose without lower employment, as workers moved to larger, better-paying, more productive firms (peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-058].
  - Bossler and Gerner find about 1.7% less employment in affected establishments, mainly through fewer hires (peer-reviewed) [EV-WRK-059].
  - Germany's floor was about half the median (49.56% in 2025) [EV-WRK-057], so it does not test two-thirds.
- **Where the United States stands.** The federal floor is 24.09% of the median full-time wage, against 49.24% in Canada, 49.56% in Germany, 61.85% in the United Kingdom and 62.48% in France (OECD, 2025) [EV-WRK-057].

**How to read it.**
- The research is strongest up to roughly 60% of the local median, which is about where the legacy circuit breaker starts [EV-WRK-051] [EV-WRK-700]. One county study reaches 0.82 [EV-WRK-063]. Beyond that, the evidence is the United Kingdom's recent experience, on its own commission's reading.
- A national two-thirds-of-median floor would sit far above two-thirds of the median in low-wage counties. The register has no county-level measure of how far a given floor reaches (section 1, Evidence still needed).
- **Puerto Rico, where the federal floor once bit hardest.** Castillo-Freeman and Freeman found that the federal minimum, high there relative to average earnings, substantially lowered employment (NBER, 1991) [EV-WRK-470]; Krueger's reanalysis of the same evidence found it "surprisingly fragile" (NBER, 1994) [EV-WRK-471]. Neither tests a floor near the whole local median, which is where $17 would sit in Puerto Rico in 2035 (93% of the median on 3.5% growth; `tools/territory_bite.py`) and in the Northern Mariana Islands (about 105%). So the evidence's reach (about 60% in state studies, 0.82 in one county study) is the guide for the territories too (`5-delivery.md`, part 5, LAB-4.10).
- **American Samoa.** From 2007 to 2014, as its industry minimums rose, overall employment fell 4% and cannery employment 50%, and workers' average real earnings fell about 11% (GAO) [EV-WRK-472]. GAO reports that minimum wage increases were a factor in cannery closures but not a main one [EV-WRK-473]. These are measured changes, not causal estimates.
- **The disagreement is real and partly about method.** The studies finding small effects mostly use local comparisons; those finding losses include a recession and one city's hours data [EV-WRK-064] [EV-WRK-060]. Authors on both sides have long-standing positions (section 1, A.4).
- **Recessions matter.** The one finding of loss specific to a downturn [EV-WRK-064] bears on the legacy recession off-ramp. Dube's review recommends a mandate for the commission to pause if job losses appear [EV-WRK-052]. That is the nearest thing in the evidence to an off-ramp; no study evaluates a trigger.

### A.2 Indexing

- **How common.** Fifteen states and DC index their floors to inflation, and five more were scheduled to (CRS, October 2024) [EV-WRK-070].
- **The case for it** is the erosion of a fixed floor: the federal floor is worth 41% less than in 1968 [EV-WRK-023]. This is arithmetic, not a study of indexing.
- **The one study of its employment effect** finds the immediate job loss from an increase about three times larger in indexing states, concentrated in the first 14 quarters. The finding does not hold in the authors' most restrictive specification (peer-reviewed; AEI-affiliated author) [EV-WRK-077].
- **The U.K. institution.** The United Kingdom indexes by a target share of the median, set each year on an expert commission's advice [EV-WRK-053]. This is the model for the legacy design's two-thirds rule. No evaluation of the institution itself is in the register.

### A.3 Phase-ins and the small-business credit

- **Size-based phase-ins exist.** Seattle phased in by employer size, with four levels from $10.50 to $13 in 2016 [EV-WRK-062]. New York's fast-food board phased in over three to six years and covered only chains with 30 or more establishments [EV-WRK-186].
- **No evaluation separates the phase-in's effect.** No study in the register tests whether firm-size or county phase-ins reduce job loss.
- **No evaluation of a small-business wage credit** (the legacy LAB-5) was found. Its case rests on judgment.

### A.4 Prices and poverty

- **Prices.** A 10% increase raises grocery prices 0.36%, which is full pass-through, equally for goods bought by poor and rich households (peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-065]. California's fast-food wage raised prices 1.5% [EV-WRK-153].
- **Poverty: two views.**
  - Dube finds a long-run poverty elasticity of −0.220 to −0.459. Tax credits and transfers offset part of the gain: net income gains are about 66% as large [EV-WRK-066].
  - Neumark and Wascher find increases both lift some poor families out of poverty and push some non-poor families in, a redistribution among low-income families. Their data are from the 1980s and 1990s [EV-WRK-076].
  - CBO's $15 estimate has both effects: fewer jobs and fewer people in poverty [EV-WRK-029].
- **The benefit phase-out.** How much of a raise a family keeps depends on the plan's own phase-outs (section 1, A.6) [EV-SEC-537]. The household model has not measured it (section 1, Evidence still needed).

### A.5 Subminimums

**The tipped wage.**
- Neumark and Yen find that higher tipped wages cut tipped restaurant jobs (elasticity about −0.08) without raising total earnings much, and are poorly targeted at poor families (working paper; authors long critical of large increases) [EV-WRK-067] [EV-WRK-068].
- EPI finds tipped workers' poverty about 4 points higher in $2.13 states than in states that pay the full wage, and about 8 points higher for waitstaff and bartenders. This is a descriptive comparison, not a causal one (EPI, a labor-aligned think tank) [EV-WRK-069].
- Seven states pay tipped workers the full minimum: Alaska, California, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, Oregon and Washington [EV-WRK-069].
- **Recent local changes.** Chicago is phasing out its tipped wage over five years [EV-WRK-072]. The District of Columbia's council in July 2025 froze the tipped wage at $10 and slowed the voters' phase-out to 75% of the full wage by 2034, instead of 100% by 2027 [EV-WRK-071]. Neither has been evaluated yet.
- *Strength:* both sides are weak. Neither is a causal study of full elimination.

**The disability subminimum (section 14(c)).**
- New Hampshire's 2015 repeal was followed by a 5.2-point rise in labor force participation and 7 points in employment for people with cognitive disabilities. Maryland's 2016 changes showed no significant effect (peer-reviewed, synthetic difference-in-differences) [EV-WRK-074].
- In Colorado and Oregon, fewer than half of about 1,000 former 14(c) workers the states could track had moved to other jobs; 54% to 61% were not working but received Medicaid-funded day services (GAO, 2025) [EV-WRK-073].
- The U.S. Commission on Civil Rights majority recommended repeal with a planned phase-out and more funding for supported employment [EV-WRK-075]. That is a recommendation, not an effect.
- **How to read it.** Ending the certificates did not by itself move most workers into jobs. The one state with gains is one study's result. What came with repeal (supported employment, a phase-out) is what the evidence points to, not repeal alone.

**The youth wage.** No study of the $4.25 youth wage's effect was found (Evidence still needed).

## Part B. Power at work

### B.1 What unions change

| Finding | Who and when | Strength | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A household union premium of 10 to 20 log points, steady from 1936 on; unions explain a significant share of the fall in inequality from the mid-1930s to the late 1940s | Farber, Herbst, Kuziemko and Naidu, 2021 | Peer-reviewed; new survey data and an instrument | [EV-WRK-164] |
| Where unions barely won elections (1984-1999), effects on survival, employment, output and wages small, wages close to zero | DiNardo and Lee, 2004 | Peer-reviewed, regression discontinuity | [EV-WRK-161] |
| After correcting for selection in close elections, unionization lowers payroll, employment and survival; effects on stayers' earnings small | Frandsen, 2021 | Peer-reviewed, same design with matched data | [EV-WRK-162] |
| After unionization, compensation and pension contributions rise, raising labor's share; bargaining targets benefits more than wages | Knepper, 2020 | Peer-reviewed, regression discontinuity, firm data | [EV-WRK-163] |
| Productivity effects small and variable, profits lower, employment growth slower; little difference in business failure | Hirsch, 2010 | Review; the author notes the evidence is dated and not causal | [EV-WRK-167] |

**How to read it.**
- Over the century, unions raise pay and narrow inequality [EV-WRK-164].
- For a single new unit won in a close election, the evidence splits on what happens to the firm [EV-WRK-161] [EV-WRK-162]. Part of the gain shows up in benefits, which wage-only studies miss [EV-WRK-163].
- The close-election studies describe the marginal case. They say little about what a broader change in density would do.

### B.2 Organizing rules: what Canada shows

Canada's provinces have changed their labor laws many times, so they are the nearest natural experiment for the PRO Act's core (LAB-1).

- **Card check against mandatory votes.** British Columbia's move to mandatory elections in 1984 cut certification success by an average of 19 points across more than 6,500 certifications; the return to card check in 1993 restored it. Management opposition was about twice as effective under elections (peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental; one province, two law changes) [EV-WRK-168]. The legacy design does not include card check; the bargaining order (*Cemex*) is the PRO Act's nearer instrument (section 1, B.5).
- **First-contract arbitration.** In Canadian jurisdictions that adopted it, first-agreement strikes fell by at least half. Arbitration was used rarely and contracts were seldom imposed. It did not change how long such strikes lasted (peer-reviewed, panel of provinces) [EV-WRK-169]. The study does not measure how many new units reach a contract, the U.S. problem in section 1 [EV-WRK-125].
- **Replacement bans: two views.**
  - Cramton, Gunderson and Tracy find a ban on replacement workers goes with higher wages and more and longer strikes, and that union workers come out ahead [EV-WRK-170].
  - Budd finds a general ban goes with longer strikes, but little effect from banning *permanent* replacements on strikes or wages [EV-WRK-171].
  - The PRO Act, as the legacy design follows it, bans permanent replacement (`legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`), which is Budd's narrower question.
  - Canada banned replacement workers in federally regulated workplaces from June 20, 2025, with fines up to $100,000 a day [EV-WRK-172]. It has not yet been evaluated.
- **Penalties.** CBO's score of the 2019 PRO Act assumed penalties of up to $50,000 per violation and $100,000 for discriminatory discharge, imposed in about 120 cases a year, raising $39 million over 2020-2029 [EV-WRK-176]. It is a budget estimate, not an estimate of deterrence. No study in the register measures how penalties change employer behavior in organizing.
- **First-contract mediation.** CBO reported that the mediation service gets about 500 notices a year of new units seeking a first contract, and mediates in roughly 10 percent of cases, only when both sides ask [EV-WRK-177].
- **Captive-audience bans in the states.** Twelve states had banned or restricted mandatory meetings by September 2025. Minnesota's law gives workers a private right of action. The Eighth Circuit dismissed the challenge to it on standing, so whether federal labor law preempts such laws is unresolved [EV-WRK-185]. No evaluation of their effect exists yet.

### B.3 Right-to-work and fair-share fees

| View | Finding | Who | Strength | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Right-to-work lowers wages and unionization | Event study of five states' adoptions since 2011 and a differential-exposure design | Fortin, Lemieux and Lloyd, 2022 | Working paper | [EV-WRK-165] |
| Lowers unionization; no effect on wages or employment | Oklahoma, synthetic control | Eren and Ozbeklik, 2016 | Peer-reviewed; one state | [EV-WRK-166] |
| Raises income growth | 0.8 point more personal income growth and 0.5 point more population growth a year, 1947-2011 | Hicks and LaFaive for the Mackinac Center, 2013 | Free-market think tank; state growth regressions, not a causal design | [EV-WRK-187] |
| Weakens unions' political role | Democratic presidential vote down 3.5 points, turnout down 2 points; fewer working-class legislators | Feigenbaum, Hertel-Fernandez and Williamson, 2018 | Working paper (later published); border counties | [EV-WRK-188] |

**How to read it.** Every study agrees that right-to-work lowers unionization. The wage effect is disputed: the stronger designs find a loss or none, and the growth gain rests on a weaker design from an advocate of the laws. The legacy design's fair-share override (LAB-1A) is the most contested provision on political grounds (`legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`); the evidence does not settle that choice, which is Espen's.

### B.4 Sectoral standards and bargaining

**Abroad.**
- **The OECD (2019)**, across 35 countries over 1980-2016, links coordinated bargaining with higher employment and lower unemployment than fully decentralised systems, and finds pay dispersion smallest under sectoral agreements. Centralised systems with high coverage showed lower productivity growth (cross-country association) [EV-WRK-173]. Where sectoral bargaining lacks broad representation, it says extension of agreements can help, limited by representativeness tests, a public-interest test and hardship opt-outs [EV-WRK-174].
- **Australia** sets minimum rates in 121 industry awards; about 20.7% of employees are paid award minimums, about 10.5% of the wage bill [EV-WRK-183]. It is the largest working example of floors set by industry.
- **New Zealand's** Fair Pay Agreements Act took effect on December 1, 2022 and was repealed a year later. Six sectors were approved to begin bargaining; none finalised an agreement [EV-WRK-175]. A sectoral system that takes years to produce its first agreement can be undone before it does.
- **Codetermination** (worker seats on boards): zero or small positive effects at the firm level and none in the aggregate [EV-WRK-184]. It is not in the legacy baseline.

**At home.**
- **New York's 2015 fast-food board** (three members: employer, worker and public) set $15, phased in to 2018 in New York City and 2021 elsewhere, for chains of 30 or more [EV-WRK-186]. No evaluation of its effects was found.
- **Boards that bind and boards that advise.**
  - Binding: Minnesota's nursing home board set minimums from September 10, 2026 [EV-CAR-618]; it has nine members, worker and employer seats split evenly [EV-WRK-179]. New York's farm board's 2022 recommendations were adopted in 2023, phasing the farm overtime threshold down from 60 to 40 hours by January 1, 2032 [EV-WRK-182].
  - Advisory: Nevada's home care board recommended, and the legislature then set $16. Colorado's board recommended $25 by 2026, and the legislature set $17 [EV-WRK-178]. Seattle's and Detroit's boards recommend to their councils [EV-WRK-180] [EV-WRK-181].
- **California's Fast Food Council** has not met as a full body in nearly two years, and its powers end on January 1, 2029 (section 1) [EV-WRK-151] [EV-WRK-152].
- **Boards that work through public payment.** Minnesota's floor took effect only once the federal Medicaid rate was approved (context of EV-WRK-179). The care dossier's $20 floor runs through the payer, not a board (`DEC-2026-11-11-04`).

**How to read it.** The evidence on outcomes is cross-country and associational [EV-WRK-173]. The U.S. record is mostly about process: boards set floors where the law gives them binding power and a funding route, and stall or advise where it does not [EV-WRK-178] [EV-WRK-152]. Durability is part of what works: New Zealand's system and California's council each ended or stalled within a few years [EV-WRK-175] [EV-WRK-151].

## Part C. The rules in practice

### C.1 Does enforcement work?

| Finding | Who and when | Strength; where | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Randomly inspected establishments had 9.4% fewer injuries and 26% lower injury costs, with no loss of jobs, sales, credit or survival | Levine, Toffel and Johnson, 2012 | Randomized natural experiment; 409 California establishments | [EV-WRK-249] |
| About $355,000 in avoided medical costs and lost earnings per inspection over five years | Same | Same | [EV-WRK-250] |
| An OSHA press release cut violations 73% at nearby peers; 210 inspections would be needed to match one release | Johnson, 2020 | Peer-reviewed, regression discontinuity; national | [EV-WRK-251] |
| Complaints track violations poorly; enforcement has to go where workers are unlikely to complain | Weil and Pyles, 2005 | Observational, industry data | [EV-WRK-253] |
| 43% of those who complained or organized faced retaliation; 20% with a serious problem stayed silent, half for fear of losing their job | Broken Laws survey, 2008 (three cities) | Survey, advocacy partners | [EV-WRK-254] |

**How to read it.**
- Inspection pays for itself on the best evidence, and publicity multiplies it. The randomized study and the press-release study share an author, so no independent replication is in the register.
- The retaliation figure counts all reprisals the report calls illegal; a later reanalysis reads about a third of those reprisals as unlawful (context of EV-WRK-254).
- **For the staffing question (section 1, C.1):** the evidence favors targeted inspection and publicity over waiting for complaints [EV-WRK-251] [EV-WRK-253].

### C.2 Penalties and state wage-theft laws

- **State laws.** Galvin finds treble damages had the largest deterrent effect of the state wage-theft laws, with significant falls in violations in five states. The effect depended on political support, worker coalitions and real enforcement (observational, state panels) [EV-WRK-252].
- **Federal remedy.** Federal law pays unpaid wages plus an equal amount in liquidated damages (section 1) [EV-WRK-205]. No study of doubling them was found.
- **Co-enforcement** with community groups (Seattle, Los Angeles, San Francisco) has no quantitative evaluation in the register (Evidence still needed).

### C.3 Coverage: misclassification and the ABC test

- **California's AB 5: two readings of one study.**
  - Mercatus researchers estimate that, in occupations not exempted, self-employment fell 10.5% and overall employment 4.4%, with no robust rise in payroll employment [EV-WRK-259].
  - The same study finds that in the quarter of occupations where self-employment was most common, payroll employment rose 10.2% and self-employment fell 17.7%, with no overall change [EV-WRK-260].
  - *Strength:* a working paper from a free-market center. Its authors say the estimates are not causal, and other economists dispute its pre-trends (context of EV-WRK-259).
- **Massachusetts (2004).** EPI reports a Harvard study's estimate that 14% to 24% of construction employers misclassified workers, costing the state up to $152 million in income taxes, and that state experts think the ABC test reduced misclassification [EV-WRK-261]. That is expert opinion, not a measured effect.
- **App-based drivers.** California's Proposition 22 made app drivers contractors under set conditions, and the state Supreme Court upheld it on July 25, 2024 [EV-WRK-262]. A state ABC test can be undone for one sector by a ballot measure.
- **What is known for certain** is the cost of misclassification to unemployment insurance: 10% to 30% of audited employers misclassified workers in nine states (section 1) [EV-WRK-216].

### C.4 Heat

- **The harm.** Hot days cause about 20,000 injuries a year in California, indoors as well as outdoors (quasi-experimental working paper) [EV-WRK-257].
- **The standard works where tried.** California's 2005 heat standard cut injuries on hot days by 15-17% in construction, 19-25% in transportation and 24-27% in agriculture (quasi-experimental, NBER working paper, 2026) [EV-WRK-258].
- **OSHA's 2024 proposal** estimated $7.8 billion a year in costs against $9.179 billion in benefits, preventing 531 of 559 heat deaths a year [EV-WRK-255]. That is the Biden administration's analysis, resting on its own undercount factor and full compliance (section 1, C.5). Industry comments contest it; they were read but not entered (Evidence still needed).
- **States.** California (2005), Washington (2023) and Oregon (2022) have standards with a plan requirement [EV-WRK-256]. No evaluation of Washington's or Oregon's was found.

### C.5 Pay transparency: two kinds of law

- **Salary ranges in job postings** (Colorado's model): postings with pay rose 30 points and wages 1.3-3.6%, with no change in employment (quasi-experimental working paper) [EV-WRK-407].
- **The right to discuss pay**: state laws protecting it lowered wages about 2%, as employers bargained harder with everyone (peer-reviewed, *Econometrica*) [EV-WRK-408].
- They study different laws. The legacy "pay-transparency floor" (LAB-7) does not yet say which kind it means.

## Part D. Support when jobs change

### D.1 Unemployment insurance: generosity, job finding and spending

| Finding | Who and when | Strength | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Higher benefits lengthen unemployment: median duration elasticity 0.53 (0.38 in U.S. studies) | Schmieder and von Wachter, 2016 | Review of 18 quasi-experimental studies | [EV-WRK-368] |
| One more month of benefits adds about 4 days of nonemployment (European studies) | Same | Same | [EV-WRK-369] |
| The $600 and $300 pandemic supplements had large effects on spending and small effects on job finding (elasticities 0.06 to 0.22) | Ganong, Greig, Noel, Sullivan and Vavra | Quasi-experimental, bank-account data | [EV-WRK-370] |
| Ending expanded benefits early in 2021 cut receipt 36.3 points but raised employment only 6.8 points; benefits lost $2,529, earnings gained $292 | Coombs and others | Quasi-experimental, states that ended early | [EV-WRK-371] |
| Spending falls 6% when a job is lost and 12% when benefits run out; extending benefits protects spending four times as much as raising them | Ganong and Noel | Quasi-experimental, bank-account data | [EV-WRK-372] [EV-WRK-373] |

**The macro debate: two views.** Hagedorn and others find the 2008-13 extensions to as long as 99 weeks raised wages, cut vacancies and raised 2011 unemployment by 2 points [EV-WRK-374]. Chodorow-Reich, Coglianese and Karabarbounis, using only variation from data errors, find at most 0.3 point [EV-WRK-375]. The second design isolates the policy more cleanly, but the question stays open.

**How to read it.** Benefits do lengthen claims somewhat [EV-WRK-368]. In the one recent test at scale, the effect on job finding was small against a large effect on spending [EV-WRK-370] [EV-WRK-371]. On the spending evidence, the length of benefits matters more for families than their level [EV-WRK-372].

### D.2 Reach, modernization and fraud

- **Modernization.** The 2009 recovery act offered states $7 billion to modernize; 41 of 53 jurisdictions had or adopted an alternative base period [EV-WRK-376]. The Labor Department's evaluation estimated first payments up 6 to 10 percent but could not rule out no effect [EV-WRK-377].
- **Pandemic fraud.** GAO estimated fraud of $100 billion to $135 billion, 11% to 15% of benefits; the Labor Department called that overstated, and GAO disagreed [EV-WRK-378]. The Labor Department's Inspector General puts improper payments at $191 billion or more of $888 billion paid, including over $76 billion stolen [EV-WRK-379].
- **How to read it.** Reaching new groups of workers quickly through 53 separate state systems came with fraud on this scale. Any wider reach (gig workers, part-time workers) has to be built with identity and wage checks that use records government already holds, not new paperwork for claimants (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`).

### D.3 Reemployment services and activation

- **Assessments.** In randomized trials, selection for reemployment and eligibility assessments cut claims by about a week, raised employment by about 2 points and first-year earnings by $400 to $500 [EV-WRK-380]. Less than half of the effect came from the help itself; the rest came from enforcing work search [EV-WRK-381].
- **The long run.** Nevada's trial, followed eight years in tax records, raised employment and earnings on average. About 40% of participants may have had negative long-run effects, matched by more entry into disability insurance [EV-WRK-382].
- **Denmark.** Random assignment to mandatory activation raised job finding by 30%, mainly through more contact with the employment service (randomized) [EV-WRK-392].
- **How to read it.** Contact and help speed reemployment on the best evidence. Part of the effect is the work-search rule, which pushes some people off benefits rather than into jobs [EV-WRK-381] [EV-WRK-382]. That is the kind of condition the design lens asks to weigh against its burden (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`), and LAB-10's principle that participation is an offer bears on it (`DEC-2026-11-12-06`).

### D.4 Work sharing

- **Abroad.** Italy's short-time work in the Great Recession cut hours and saved jobs. Its insurance value vanished in low-productivity firms, and it slowed employment growth in firms that did not use it (quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-383].
- **At home.** Thirty states run work sharing, and participants are about 1% of claimants (section 1) [EV-WRK-312] [EV-WRK-313].
- **How to read it.** Work sharing saves jobs in downturns, but it can also keep workers in firms that would shrink anyway [EV-WRK-383]. The register has no evaluation of U.S. use in 2020 (Evidence still needed).

### D.5 Notice and severance

- **New Jersey's 2023 law** requires 90 days' notice from employers with 100 or more workers for a layoff of 50 or more, and one week's severance per year of service, plus four more weeks if notice is short [EV-WRK-384].
- **Federal law** requires 60 days, covers fewer layoffs and has no agency enforcing it (section 1) [EV-WRK-325] [EV-WRK-328]. Only about 45% of displaced workers got written notice [EV-WRK-318].
- **Evidence on effects.** Studies that advance notice shortens joblessness (Ruhm; Addison and Blackburn) were found but not entered (Evidence still needed). New Jersey's law has not been evaluated.

### D.6 Training and apprenticeship

| Finding | Who and when | Strength | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intensive job-search services raised earnings $3,300 to $7,100 (7 to 20 percent) over 30 months, cost-effectively | National WIA evaluation (Mathematica for the Labor Department), 28 areas | Randomized | [EV-WRK-385] |
| Training funded through the same system showed no positive effect within 30 months (not conclusive) | Same | Randomized | [EV-WRK-386] |
| Sector-focused training (Year Up, Project QUEST, WorkAdvance, Per Scholas): earnings up 11 to 40 percent | Pooled trials (secure floor's register) | Randomized | [EV-SEC-143] |
| Registered apprentices earned $5,839 more in the ninth year | Mathematica for the Labor Department, ten states | Matched comparison | [EV-SEC-144] |
| Job Corps raised education, cut crime and raised earnings for several years; on tax data the gains faded except for the oldest | National Job Corps Study, 15,400 youths | Randomized | [EV-WRK-393] |
| Trade adjustment training: no gain or a loss at four years on one study, $50,000 more over ten years on another | Section 1; secure floor's register | Matched comparison; quasi-random | [EV-SEC-147] [EV-SEC-148] |

**A failure to avoid abroad.** England's apprenticeship levy (0.5% of large employers' pay bills, from 2017) was followed by a 26% fall in starts, from 509,400 in 2015/16 to 375,800 in 2017/18, and levy payers used only 9% of their funds in the first year [EV-WRK-387]. The National Audit Office notes part of the fall may reflect a move away from low-quality programs [EV-WRK-388]. A financing mechanism that funds employers' choices does not by itself create apprenticeships.

**How to read it.** The randomized evidence favors training built with employers for specific occupations [EV-SEC-143], and apprenticeship's matched evidence points the same way [EV-SEC-144]. General training vouchers through the workforce system show no near-term gain [EV-WRK-386]. Section 1's apprenticeship counts (D.4) and the legacy LAB-8 sizes are weighed in part F.

### D.7 Wage insurance

- Section 1 holds the evidence: in TAA's wage insurance, eligibility raised employment 8 to 17 points and earnings over $18,000 over four years, and the program paid for itself (regression discontinuity) [EV-WRK-365]. A related paper finds wage insurance and training had similar long-run effects [EV-WRK-366]. Take-up was low [EV-WRK-367] [EV-WRK-332].
- The sample is older, long-tenured workers displaced by trade, so the result may not carry to younger workers or larger programs (context of EV-WRK-365).
- Clean power's decided transition guarantee pays wage insurance of up to 90% of prior earnings for two years (`DEC-2026-11-13-06`). Whether a general wage insurance builds on it is section 3's question.
- **AI displacement.** No program built for AI-driven job loss has been evaluated. The evidence for AI-4's design is the general displacement evidence above and in section 1 (D.5).

### D.8 Job guarantees and national service

- **Austria's job guarantee pilot** in Gramatneusiedl (2020-2024) offered jobs to the long-term unemployed. It raised well-being, cut long-term unemployment and showed no negative spillovers on other employment (matched randomization and synthetic control) [EV-WRK-389]. It enrolled 107 people; it cost about €237 per participant per month against a €102 rise in participants' income [EV-WRK-390]. It is one small town.
- **AmeriCorps.** The early longitudinal study of more than 2,000 members found gains in civic engagement and in choosing public-service careers, and no short-term effect on education (comparison group; agency-commissioned) [EV-WRK-391]. No earnings result or cost per member was found (Evidence still needed). LAB-9's case rests mainly on its civic purpose, not on labor-market evidence.

## Part E. Schedules and non-competes

- **Fair Workweek laws** raised two weeks' notice of schedules by 13 points and cut "clopening" shifts by 8 points, with no rise in hours and no offsetting cut elsewhere in job quality (stacked difference-in-differences, original survey data) [EV-WRK-409]. An interview study found Oregon's statewide law broadly ineffective; results vary with enforcement [EV-WRK-410]. Seattle's results and the Gap trial are in section 1 [EV-WRK-402] [EV-WRK-403].
- **Non-competes** (COM-4's home). Oregon's 2008 ban for hourly workers raised hourly wages 2%-3% and job mobility (quasi-experimental, one state) [EV-WRK-411]. A law-review critique finds the wider literature mixed and not supportive of a ban across all sectors, but allows that low-wage workers are a case for action [EV-WRK-412].

## Failures to avoid

- **A sectoral system that cannot produce its first agreement before an election.** New Zealand's was repealed with none finalised [EV-WRK-175]; California's council stalled [EV-WRK-152].
- **Boards with advice but no power or funding.** Colorado's board recommended $25 and got $17 [EV-WRK-178].
- **Repealing a subminimum without the support that moves people into jobs** [EV-WRK-073].
- **Enforcement that waits for complaints** [EV-WRK-253] [EV-WRK-254].
- **A training levy or voucher that funds employers' or customers' choices without employer-built programs** [EV-WRK-387] [EV-WRK-386].
- **Expanding unemployment insurance fast through weak identity checks** [EV-WRK-378] [EV-WRK-379].
- **Work sharing that props up firms that would shrink anyway** [EV-WRK-383].
- **A coverage test a single sector can vote itself out of** [EV-WRK-262].

## Questions this section leaves for section 3

These are questions the evidence raises. None is a recommendation.
1. **The floor's level and how it adjusts:** a national target against a local one, and who sets the annual step (a rule in statute, an indexed formula or a commission), given the evidence's range (A.1, A.2).
2. **Protection where the floor bites hardest:** circuit breakers, phase-ins and a recession pause, none evaluated (A.1, A.3).
3. **Subminimums:** the pace of each phase-out and what support travels with the 14(c) repeal (A.5).
4. **Organizing rules:** which of the Canadian instruments (election rules, first-contract arbitration, replacement bans) to carry, and how to enforce penalties after *Jarkesy* (B.2; section 1, B.5).
5. **Fair-share fees:** Espen's choice, with the evidence in B.3.
6. **Sectoral standards:** binding boards with a funding route, advisory boards or sectoral bargaining, and how to make them last (B.4).
7. **Enforcement design:** targeting, publicity and penalties for the Wage and Hour Division and OSHA (C.1, C.2), and which coverage test (C.3).
8. **Pay transparency:** which kind of law (C.5).
9. **Unemployment insurance:** reach, length against level, financing and fraud controls (D.1, D.2), and the role of work-search rules (D.3).
10. **Training:** employer-built sector programs and apprenticeship against general vouchers (D.6), and how wage insurance meets clean power's guarantee (D.7).
11. **Service and job guarantees:** what LAB-9 is for, given the evidence (D.8).
