# Fair pay and real power at work: delivery

Role: section 5 of the work dossier (flagship 5). It says who runs each provision of `4-specification.md`, what capacity each agency needs, the timeline from January 2029 to full operation, how people get remedies when delivery fails, what states do, and how the provisions reach rural places. Status: **draft** (stage F), on `DEC-2026-11-17-01` to `-36`, `-95` and `-96`, and on `DEC-2026-11-13-08` (use existing machinery first) and `DEC-2026-11-09-26` (rural delivery through USDA's local channels first). Items still with Espen (memo 10, LAB-19; points 5 and 11, LAB-14.01 and LAB-17.01; good-cause quits in LAB-18.03) are delivered here on their recommended option and stay provisional. Agency capacity belongs to the cross-cutting capacity work (CAP-7, democratic renewal, `DEC-2026-11-17-27`); this section states work's needs for it. Costs are reported for stage G and priced in `6-cost.md`; nothing is booked. Dates are the working timeline of section 4; where a step's date is a working choice, it says so.

**Agencies already doing the work.** Every job runs through a channel that exists. The new pieces are small: the wage commission and a standards boards office inside Labor; Wage and Hour's new duty to enforce layoff notice; and a general wage insurance run by the state UI agencies that paid trade adjustment's narrower one until its phased termination began on July 1, 2022 [EV-WRK-330]. The delivery risks are concentrated in two places (part 2): the state UI agencies, which take on the most change at once, and the hiring of Labor's and the Board's enforcement staff, which is slow to build.

**What the act does not build.** No new federal agency, payment system or registry. Five simplifications follow `DEC-2026-11-13-08`; each is a working choice the milestone review can test:
1. **Wage insurance starts from the unemployment claim,** not a separate petition and certification as trade adjustment required: the separation is the state's own finding (LAB-18.03), and the claim form offers wage insurance to everyone who files (LAB-18.05).
2. **The credit is a line on the payroll return** (LAB-5.01), not an application, so the IRS checks it against wage data it already holds.
3. **Layoff notice is enforced by Wage and Hour's existing investigators** (LAB-13.01), not a new office.
4. **Apprenticeship grants pay on the completion counts programs already file** (LAB-8.01), and national service runs through AmeriCorps' grantees and the state commissions (LAB-9.01).
5. **One date for the states' unemployment changes** (working choice): the standards (LAB-11.01 to 11.03), the new Extended Benefits triggers (LAB-11.04) and wage insurance (LAB-18.08) all start on January 1, 2031, so each state changes its law and its systems once. The hours field (LAB-18.06) and, if Espen chooses memo 10 C, the occupation field (LAB-19.01) come together on January 1, 2033, so employers change their wage report once.

## 2. Capacity

Point 14 sends the agencies' needs to democratic renewal's CAP-7, built from workload (`DEC-2026-11-17-27`); section 4, part F, sizes them. This part says what each agency faces and what must be true for the dates to hold. The money is in CAP-7 or in the provisions' own lines, as part 7 lists.

**The NLRB.**
- *The starting point.* Regional field staff fell a third, from 1,067 FTE in FY2014 to 719 in FY2022 [EV-WRK-118]; the agency had 1,151 FTE in FY2026 [EV-WRK-119], with 3,286 election petitions and 21,292 unfair labor practice charges in FY2024 [EV-WRK-109]. It lacked a quorum for most of 2025 [EV-WRK-135].
- *The new work.* Card checks against payroll within 14 days (LAB-1.01); the General Counsel's penalty suits in district court, about 120 cases a year on CBO's reading of the 2019 bill [EV-WRK-176]; mandatory injunctions for firings during campaigns (LAB-2.03); and, if a quorum lapses, regional certifications without Board review (LAB-2.04).
- *What it needs* (section 4, part F): about 400-600 FTE above FY2026, field staff back to at least FY2014's level by FY2032, about $0.10-0.15 billion a year and $1.0-1.4 billion over FY2030-39 (project arithmetic). The legacy capacity file allocated $4 billion by judgment [EV-WRK-706]; the legacy second-round review behind democratic renewal's table carries the NLRB at $0.05 billion of surge, $1.9 billion sustained over the decade (range $1.3-3.3 billion) and $0.2 billion a year at the decade's end, among "other entities" [EV-DEM-330]. Work's workload figure sits below that review's central estimate, near its low end, and well below the legacy $4 billion.
- *Sequence.* Hiring starts with CAP-7's surge, funded on enactment in FY2029, not after the labor act passes, because the new caseload lands on the regions and the courts at once (the legacy capacity note, `legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`, "Capacity note", made the same point). Card checks are likely less work per case than elections (judgment), so the regions can absorb them first; the penalty and injunction litigation needs trial attorneys, the slowest hires.

**FMCS.** About 500 newly certified units a year reach it today, and it mediates about 10% of first-contract cases, only where both sides ask [EV-WRK-177]. Under LAB-1.05 any side can ask after 90 days, and arbitration panels follow 30 days later. On card check's higher certification rate the caseload could rise severalfold (judgment). About $10-20 million a year (section 4, part F) pays for mediators and the panels' roster.

**The Wage and Hour Division.**
- *Investigators* rise from 611 (May 2025) to at least 1,200 by FY2034 [EV-WRK-207], about 590 more (project arithmetic), or about 120 net hires a year from FY2030 plus replacements. Each investigator covered about 278,000 workers in 2025 [EV-WRK-208], and concluded compliance actions halved from FY2013 to FY2025 [EV-WRK-201].
- *New duties* on the same staff: the standards boards' standards (LAB-3), layoff notice (LAB-13.01), schedules (LAB-15.01), pay transparency (LAB-7.08, 7.09), home care coverage (LAB-12.01, whose enforcement Wage and Hour suspended in 2025 [EV-WRK-225]), the overtime threshold and the wage floor's steps. Directed inspections (LAB-7.02) and publication (LAB-7.03) stretch the staff further than complaint-driven work: one published release cut violations at nearby peers as much as 210 inspections did [EV-WRK-251].
- *Training time.* A new investigator takes about two years to carry a full caseload (judgment), so the floor's full effect arrives around FY2036. The statutory floor (LAB-7.01) protects the staff once hired, with a report to Congress if appropriations fall below it.
- *Capacity line:* democratic renewal's table carries Wage and Hour at about +1,300 staff and OSHA at about +700 [EV-DEM-331], inside Labor's $0.2 billion of surge, $4.6 billion sustained over FY2030-39 and $0.6 billion a year at the decade's end [EV-DEM-329]. On section 4's $0.3-0.5 billion a year at full strength from FY2034, ramped from FY2030, Wage and Hour and OSHA together need about $2.4-4.0 billion over FY2030-39 (project arithmetic: about eight full years' worth), inside that $4.6 billion.

**OSHA.** Federal inspectors rise from 618 [EV-WRK-232] to about 1,200 (working assumption, LAB-7.01), about 580 more (project arithmetic). The heat standard (LAB-6.01) is the main new rule: OSHA's 2026 heat program relies on the General Duty Clause because no standard is final [EV-WRK-239]. With support staff about 580 inspectors need about +1,100 staff, above democratic renewal's +700 for OSHA (section 4, LAB-7.01, judgment). That difference, about 400 staff or roughly $0.08 billion a year at full strength (judgment, at about $200,000 a staff year), is one of the items this PR sends to democratic renewal (part 7).

**The wage commission and the standards boards office.** Small staffs inside Labor: the commission's research staff and the boards' hearing and rulemaking staff, about $0.1-0.3 billion a year with the boards' own costs (memo 5; section 4, part F). The first board must be appointed within 90 days of enactment and the commission's county measure is due within 18 months (LAB-3.02, 4.04), so both offices are staffed in the first months of the labor act.

**The state UI agencies: the largest delivery risk.**
- *The change at once.* Every one of the 53 UI jurisdictions [EV-WRK-376] must, by January 1, 2031: pay the new benefit formula and duration, cover part-time workers and the new monetary test, count the latest quarter, disregard partial earnings, apply the ABC test, run work sharing, take weekly attestation without contact counts, check identity and wages against federal records, apply the new Extended Benefits triggers, and run wage insurance with its determination for workers who never claimed. By 2033 they also collect hours (and, if Espen chooses memo 10 C, occupation) in quarterly wage reports.
- *The systems they start from.* In the pandemic, 17 of 50 states said their systems could not implement the CARES Act, and states with modernized systems started the new programs one to two weeks faster [EV-DEM-295]; 9 of 12 sampled states could not pay 87% of claims within 21 days [EV-DEM-294]. Labor's standard is 87% of first payments within 14 or 21 days; six sampled states paid 70% of initial claims within 21 days from mid-2020 to September 2021 [EV-WRK-427]. GAO lists the unemployment insurance system among the programs behind most reported improper payments [EV-DEM-112], and pandemic fraud ran to $100-135 billion on GAO's estimate [EV-WRK-378]. The lesson is the one memo 7 drew: expand through checks on records the government holds (LAB-11.06), not through weak ones.
- *Money.* State administration grants were $3.14 billion in FY2024 [EV-WRK-309], against $2.76 billion enacted for FY2026 and requested for FY2027 [EV-WRK-308]; LAB-11.08's floor restores about $0.38 billion a year (project arithmetic). That pays for running the larger program, not for rebuilding systems. Section 5 adds **a one-time conformity and systems grant (LAB-11.09)**: about $1-2 billion over FY2030-32 (judgment: 53 jurisdictions at about $20-40 million each), paid from R1 on a plan each state files, with the hours and occupation fields priced separately in LAB-19.01. Without it, the 2031 date rests on systems many states cannot change in 18 months.
- *Wage insurance's volume.* On memo 8's $2-4 billion a year and an average payment of about $6,500 a year, roughly 0.3-0.6 million workers draw it each year (judgment); section 6's step-by-step chain puts about 0.32 million on the rolls at a time (`6-cost.md`, part 1). That is far larger than trade adjustment's wage insurance, which only 11% of all TAA participants collected in FY2021 [EV-WRK-332] (a share of participants, most of them not eligible for it, not a take-up rate), and larger than clean power's guarantee, about 8,000 covered layoffs a year (`dossiers/clean-power/5-delivery.md`, part 2), which the same agencies pay. Trade adjustment's evaluation found its wage insurance "challenging to implement at first" [EV-WRK-367]. The agencies' administrative cost for wage insurance is paid from federal funds (part 7).
- *The hourly-pay route.* Most states' wage records hold earnings, not hours (section 4, "Evidence still needed"); how many hold hours today is not yet in the register (part 8). Until they do, payment rests on employers' reports and, under LAB-18.08, on the worker's monthly statement (part 3, "A gap section 4 leaves").

**The IRS.** The credit (LAB-5.01) adds a claim to Form 941 for about three years: a per-worker calculation (new rate less old wage, times hours) for four quarters after each step, and a controlled-group test the IRS already applies to other payroll rules. The Treasury transfers the offset employer taxes to the Social Security and Medicare trust funds, so they are made whole (LAB-5.01). The size and form, including a flat amount per hour as a simpler alternative, are for Espen (W7). The cap of $20,000 per employer per year limits the exposure to improper claims. Running cost is small and inside the IRS's baseline (judgment); the IRS's general rebuild is CAP-7's.

**Labor's Office of Apprenticeship and the state agencies.** New registered apprentices must nearly double, from 311,035 in FY2025 [EV-WRK-349] to 600,000 by FY2034 (LAB-8.04), about 32,000 more starts each year (project arithmetic). The current administration's own order sets a larger target of 1 million active apprentices [EV-WRK-351], so the direction is shared; the constraint is registering and overseeing programs, and paying grants on their records. The grants are LAB-8's money; the federal registration staff in states without their own apprenticeship agency is a CAP-7 need not yet sized (part 7). About 42% of registered apprentices complete within a year of their expected date [EV-WRK-350]: the mentors and supports (LAB-8.03) are the capacity that protects completion as starts grow.

**AmeriCorps.** Its FY2026 appropriation was $1.254 billion [EV-WRK-397]. LAB-9.01's 150,000 more full-time positions at about $5 billion a year at full scale is about four times that (project arithmetic). The agency lost staff in 2025 (legacy capacity note), so the ramp starts at about 30,000 a year and the gate on the second tranche (LAB-9.03) is also a capacity test: the tranche opens only once the first 150,000 are filled and completing. The binding constraint is host sites and supervisors in classrooms, disaster recovery, public health and election offices, which the grantees and state commissions recruit (judgment). The grantees' match is part of it: AmeriCorps' rule requires an overall share of 24% of program costs, rising to 30% for long-running grantees [EV-WRK-454], about $1.5 billion a year at full scale that host organizations, states and their funders must raise (`6-cost.md`, part 1; LAB-9.01's working choice). The rule's waiver for rural and severely economically distressed communities eases it where raising money is hardest. The agency's administration is inside LAB-9's cost.

**The 14(c) transition.** 642 employers paid 35,534 workers under certificates on October 1, 2025 [EV-WRK-008]. The grant ($1-3 billion over five years, LAB-4.09) goes through vocational rehabilitation and developmental disability agencies that already serve these workers; 16 states have ended certificates and hold the experience [EV-WRK-011]. No new certificates are issued after enactment; existing ones continue, renewed on their existing terms, until repeal on January 1, 2034 (LAB-4.08). GAO found that fewer than half of tracked former 14(c) workers in two states moved to other jobs [EV-WRK-073], which is why the services guarantee holds a person's services until a plan offers integrated work.

**BLS.** The median for indexing (LAB-4.02), the unemployment rate for the pause (LAB-4.03), the overtime threshold's percentile (LAB-12.02) and the displaced-worker survey [EV-WRK-315] are published today. The yearly displacement report and the annual survey are memo 10's (LAB-19.03, about $20-50 million a year, provisional).

**OPM and the FLRA.** Restored bargaining (LAB-16.01) returns unfair labor practice and negotiability cases to the FLRA and contract work to agencies' labor relations staff. The added load is small (judgment) and is listed for democratic renewal (part 7).

**If R1 is late.** The UI standards' and wage insurance's start moves to the first January 1 at least 18 months after R1's enactment (working choice), so states keep the same lead time; the conformity grant moves with it.

**If the Byrd rule strikes the UI standards from R1** (section 4, "Why this split"), they ride the labor act; on the working timeline that keeps January 1, 2031.

**If the labor act is late.** The first wage step moves to the first January 1 at least six months after signature, and every later step and date moves with it (LAB-4.01); the credit pays nothing until a step is enacted. The executive steps of January 2029 continue meanwhile.

**If the Senate rules reform fails** (D2). The labor act's rules need 60 votes, which the legacy vote simulation gave a 13% chance in the plan's first Congress [EV-WRK-712]. R1's parts still run: unemployment insurance's money, Extended Benefits, wage insurance, apprenticeship, the 14(c) grant and national service's fallback. The executive steps hold only as long as an administration keeps them (LAB-4.05, 2.06, 16.01). Section 8 names this as the flagship's main legislative risk.

**A gap section 4 leaves: wage insurance's monthly payment and quarterly reports** (decided as recommended, `DEC-2026-11-18-06`, with Espen's request for a gut check, below). LAB-18.02 pays "each month", but under way (i) the new employer reports hours and rate "each quarter" (LAB-18.06), so as written the agency would know the hours only after a quarter ends. Section 5 adds the smallest fix (LAB-18.08): the worker confirms each month, on the state's claim portal, the hours worked at the new job, with the hourly rate the employer reported already filled in, the way claimants already certify each week for unemployment insurance; the employer's quarterly report (and from 2033 the wage record) checks it after the fact, and an honest overpayment is waived (LAB-18.03's rule). This is clean power's model for its guarantee, which Espen chose over caseworker review (`DEC-2026-11-13-06`). *Burden:* one short monthly confirmation for the worker, for up to two years. *The alternatives:* pay quarterly in arrears on the employer's report, which needs nothing from the worker but delays the first payment by three to four months after the new job starts; or require monthly employer reports, which adds paperwork for every new employer.

**The gut check Espen asked for (W6, `DEC-2026-11-18-06`): does the monthly statement add problematic paperwork?** Short answer: no, with two small fixes now written into LAB-18.08.
- *What the worker does.* Once a month, for at most two years, the worker opens the state's claim portal (or uses its phone line or a Rural Development front door, part 6), sees the new employer's reported hourly rate already filled in, enters the month's hours and submits. No documents, no pay stubs, no interview. That is less than unemployment insurance asks today: claimants certify every week or two (LAB-11.07).
- *What the employer does.* Nothing new beyond the quarterly short form LAB-18.06 already asks for, which the statement does not duplicate in time: the worker reports monthly so the payment comes on time, and the employer's form checks it after the quarter.
- *What the agency does.* It pays on the statement and compares each quarter with the employer's report. The one real source of friction is there: a small mismatch would send the worker an overpayment notice, and notices and repayment demands are the burden that falls hardest on people in tight months (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`).
- *Fix 1, a tolerance.* A quarter's difference of less than 10% of the payment or $100, whichever is larger (working choice), is not pursued either way: no notice and no repayment. Larger gaps are corrected on the next payment, and an honest overpayment is waived (LAB-18.03's rule).
- *Fix 2, the statement ends itself.* In a state whose wage records hold hours (by January 1, 2033, LAB-18.06), payment is figured from the records and the statement stops; until then, if an employer reports hours monthly through its payroll provider, the hours are filled in and the worker only confirms. A month the worker misses is not lost: it can be filed until the end of the benefit year.
- *Cost.* Inside the administration line (`6-cost.md`, part 1); the tolerance trims recovery work and gives up a small amount of recoveries, too small to price.
- *Verdict.* One short monthly entry for up to two years, ending when the records can do the work, is light new paperwork of the useful kind Espen allows ("Some new p&p is good!"). The alternatives remain: quarterly payment in arrears asks nothing of the worker but delays the first payment three to four months; monthly employer reports put paperwork on every new employer.


## 4. Remedies

**When an agency fails.** The design leans on remedies that do not depend on the agency:
- *The Board.* If it is captured or loses its quorum, workers sue (LAB-2.02) and certifications stand (LAB-2.04).
- *Wage and Hour and OSHA.* If they are cut, the statutory staffing floor requires a report to Congress (LAB-7.01), and workers keep the private right (LAB-7.05).
- *The Secretary.* If the fast-food board misses its deadline, the Secretary issues a standard on its record within 90 days (LAB-3.02). If OSHA misses the heat standard's one-year deadline, the statutory minimums keep applying (LAB-6.01), and a court can compel the overdue rule under the Administrative Procedure Act (judgment).
- *Published counts with a review trigger, not gates* (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`): violators published (LAB-7.03), apprenticeship starts and completions by state and group (LAB-8.04), service's mixing report (LAB-9.04), the commission's yearly report (LAB-4.04) and, if Espen chooses memo 10 C, the displacement report (LAB-19.03).

**When a state fails to conform** (LAB-11.10, a working choice). Today the federal standards are enforced by an all-or-nothing rule: a state Labor does not certify loses its employers' tax credit and its administrative grant. The sanction is so heavy that it is rarely credible, and it falls on the state's employers, not its legislature. Section 5 adds a step before it, on the model of the credit reduction that already applies in states with outstanding federal loans, where employers lose 5% of the federal tax as credit after a second January 1 with a balance and 5% more each later year [EV-WRK-428]: Labor gives notice and a cure period of one legislative session, with the conformity grant (LAB-11.09) available. After that, two authorities apply (provisional fix, a working choice for the milestone review; `6-cost.md`, part 2): a *conformity reduction*, separate from the loan reduction, cuts the state's employers' credit by the same steps, 0.3 percentage point of the 6.0% tax a year (project arithmetic), until the state conforms, with the added tax paid into a federal account in the trust fund; and Labor may pay claimants the difference between the state's benefit and the federal standard through the state agency under an agreement, from that account, paying directly only if the state refuses. Where a state also carries a loan, the loan reduction keeps priority. The all-or-nothing rule stays as the last step. Two new authorities are needed because in today's law the reduced credit's added tax repays the state's federal loan and any excess goes to the state's own account, which pays only under the state's law (42 U.S.C. 1101(d)(1)) [EV-WRK-429] [EV-WRK-430]. Two states carried outstanding federal advances of $21.4 billion on January 1, 2026 [EV-WRK-305], so the credit-reduction machinery is in use today.

## 5. States' roles

- **State UI agencies** carry the largest share (part 2): the 2031 standards, work sharing, attestation, the identity checks, wage insurance as Labor's agents, the determination for workers who never claimed, the separation-accuracy sample (LAB-11.08) and, from 2033, hours (and possibly occupation) in wage reports. Benefits stay state-paid from state trust funds, financed by employers' state taxes on the larger base; 18 states met the solvency standard on January 1, 2026 [EV-WRK-304]. Wage insurance and the administrative grants are federal money. Extended Benefits are fully federal (LAB-11.04).
- **State wage records** are the data backbone. They stay under the UI confidentiality rules; BLS uses them for statistics only (LAB-19.01); workers who moved between states have their tenure read across states through the interstate wage arrangements states already use for combined-wage claims (working choice).
- **State labor departments** keep their own wage and hour laws and enforcement: the federal floor, the boards' standards, schedules and transparency rules are floors, and higher state and local rules are not preempted (LAB-3.01, 4.01, 7.08, 15.01). Wage and Hour may enter co-enforcement agreements with them and with the outreach grantees (LAB-7.05).
- **State rapid-response units** receive the 90-day notices, as the state dislocated worker unit receives WARN notices today [EV-WRK-424], and must deliver rapid response when a closure or mass layoff is announced [EV-WRK-423]; they start services at the site. (Memo 10's reason box, under which they would forward reason counts to BLS, is deferred to the AI topic, `DEC-2026-11-18-01`; LAB-19.02.)
- **State OSHA plans.** Each State Plan adopts the heat standard, or one at least as effective, within six months of OSHA's (LAB-6.01) [EV-WRK-420]. A gap: the act's interim minimums are not an OSHA standard, so the at-least-as-effective rule would not reach them in State Plan states. Section 5 adds LAB-6.03 (working choice): the interim minimums are treated as an occupational safety and health standard for State Plan purposes. Seven states already have heat protections [EV-WRK-237], and Virginia's boards must adopt one by May 2028 [EV-WRK-240]; the federal floor overrides state laws that bar localities from going further (LAB-6.01).
- **State standards boards and councils** (California's fast-food council, Minnesota's nursing-home board and others [EV-WRK-179] [EV-WRK-181]) keep operating; a federal board's standard is a floor under theirs, and the federal board takes their records as evidence (working choice).
- **State apprenticeship agencies, workforce boards and community colleges.** Where the Labor Department recognizes a State Apprenticeship Agency [EV-WRK-421], it registers programs; elsewhere the federal Office of Apprenticeship does. Colleges and boards and sponsor them; local boards move at least half their WIOA training money to sector programs (LAB-8.05).
- **State vocational rehabilitation, developmental disability and Medicaid agencies** run the 14(c) transition and keep services in place (LAB-4.09).
- **State service commissions** run most of national service's positions, as they run AmeriCorps State today (LAB-9.01), and the fallback's formula grants (LAB-9.06).
- **States as employers.** The wage floor and overtime rules already reach state and local government workers under the Fair Labor Standards Act, so the steps raise some states' own payroll; the labor act leaves state public-sector bargaining to state law (section 4, "Where particular groups fall"). No provision adds a new state matching cost; the costs states bear are their employers' unemployment taxes and their own payroll, and, where a state or its commission funds AmeriCorps grantees, a share of the existing national service match on the new positions (LAB-9.01; about $1.5 billion a year at full scale across all grantees, `6-cost.md`, part 1).
- **The territories.** Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands run federal-state unemployment insurance, so LAB-11 and LAB-18 reach them through their agencies. Wage law applies in every territory as today; the floor's schedule in the territories is LAB-4.10 (below).

**The territories' wage floor (LAB-4.10, revised for Espen's W9, `DEC-2026-11-18-09`, provisional until Espen confirms).** Section 4 left the territories to this section, and the milestone review found the first draft incomplete: it gave Puerto Rico, Guam and the Virgin Islands the national schedule with no measure of its bite (`review-milestone.md`, M-1). This part measures it the way memo 1 measures the national floor (the floor over the local all-jobs median, the median grown at 3.5% a year, range 3-4%; `tools/territory_bite.py`).

- *What it shows.* The Virgin Islands and Guam sit near the low-wage state areas (58-70% in 2035, against 62-68% in Mississippi). Puerto Rico and the Northern Mariana Islands do not: $17 would sit near or above the whole local median, beyond any studied range (about 60% in state studies, 0.82 in one county study, `2-what-works.md`, A.1) [EV-WRK-051] [EV-WRK-063]. The one body of evidence from a floor with real bite, Puerto Rico's in earlier decades, is contested: Castillo-Freeman and Freeman found real job losses [EV-WRK-470], Krueger found that evidence fragile [EV-WRK-471].
- *Limits.* The medians are all-jobs medians from BLS's employer survey, except the Northern Mariana Islands', which is the Commonwealth's own wage census taken in a pandemic month with low participation (GAO says the low-wage count fell sharply that year, so the true median is more likely lower) [EV-WRK-460] [EV-WRK-469]. These are first area measures, not the county measure the commission builds (LAB-4.04). The share of jobs below each step needs OEWS percentiles, which bls.gov's spreadsheets hold and which could not be downloaded; at a floor near the median, close to half of jobs would be below it (judgment).
- *Today's floors.* Each territory's own law sets more than $7.25 except the Northern Mariana Islands': $10.50 in Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands, $9.25 in Guam [EV-WRK-466]. Puerto Rico's $10.50 (July 2024) came from Act 47-2021 with its own Minimum Wage Review Commission, which approved it on an economic study [EV-WRK-468]; it is about 84% of that year's median on this growth, so Puerto Rico has chosen a high bite for itself (project arithmetic). Territorial laws can stay above the federal floor under any option, as states' can (29 U.S.C. 218).

*Revised for W9 (`DEC-2026-11-18-09`): the national schedule everywhere, slower where an abrupt rise is dangerous.* Espen doubted the cap was fair: "if we're raising the wage we ought to do so everywhere", with "slower schedule in territories where doing it abruptly would be economically dangerous", and noted that Puerto Rico "likely becomes a state by 2031/32" in the plan, so a separate wage there "seems tricky". The revision drafts that direction, provisional until Espen confirms. Every option has **no federal cost to speak of**: the floor is a mandate on employers, and Puerto Rico's largest federal programs, nutrition aid and Medicaid, run on capped grants that do not follow wages as they do in the states (judgment; not priced). What differs is the risk to jobs and the size and timing of the raise.

The territorial wage committee (the former D) is dropped: it is the opposite of one rule everywhere, and its proposers paired it with a moratorium [EV-WRK-473].

- *Drafted (B), the rule* (`tools/territory_bite.py`, `slow_floor`). Each January, in each State as federal wage law defines it, which already includes the District of Columbia and every territory and possession (29 U.S.C. 203(c)) [EV-WRK-498], the floor is the national rate (LAB-4.01, 4.02), unless that rate is more than 75% of the jurisdiction's all-jobs median hourly wage as last published before the Secretary's October 1 notice. Then the floor rises by at least $1.00 that year (from 2036, by at least the national rate's own rise plus $0.25, so the gap always closes), or to 75% of the median if that is higher, never above the national rate, never down. Once a jurisdiction is back on the national rate it stays there. The median is BLS's OEWS median and, for the Northern Mariana Islands until BLS publishes one, the Commonwealth's Prevailing Wage Study [EV-WRK-460].
- *Where it slows anything.* On today's medians, only Puerto Rico and the Northern Mariana Islands. Guam, the Virgin Islands and every state, Mississippi included (62% in 2035), stay on the national schedule. Puerto Rico follows the national steps through 2031 ($11.00), then rises $1.00 a year: $12.00 in 2032, $15.00 in 2035, $20.00 in 2040, the national rate about 2042.

- *Puerto Rico's statehood.* The plan's status act holds a binding vote with Congress committed to the result (DEM-6.01); Espen expects statehood by about 2031-32 if Puerto Ricans choose it (Espen's words, not a settled date). The rule names no territory and reads the same whether Puerto Rico is a territory or a state, because federal wage law already counts both as a "State" [EV-WRK-498]. Nothing has to be rewritten at admission, and no state has a separate federal wage: the slower path is one rule that could in principle apply to any jurisdiction whose wages are low enough.
- *What B does not do.* It slows the rise; it does not change where it ends. Unless Puerto Rico's wages grow faster than the nation's, the floor there reaches about 94% of the local median when it joins the national rate, and in the Northern Mariana Islands about 106%: the same bite as A, about seven to nine years later. That is still beyond every studied range (about 60% in state studies, 0.82 in one county study) [EV-WRK-051] [EV-WRK-063], and the one record of such a bite, Puerto Rico's own, is contested [EV-WRK-470] [EV-WRK-471]. The time buys evidence: the commission reports on each slowed jurisdiction every year (LAB-4.04), the recession pause applies as everywhere (LAB-4.03), and any change takes an act of Congress. Puerto Rico's own commission stays free to go faster, as it did in 2024 [EV-WRK-468].
- *Why B.* It is the direction Espen gave: the same floor everywhere, reached later only where the bite would pass 75% of local pay in a single step. Compared with C, a full-time worker in Puerto Rico earns about $3,700 more in 2035, and the floor joins the national one instead of staying separate. Compared with A, it avoids a jump to 93% of the median within six years.
- *Against B.* The endpoint risk above, and a second published rate in two jurisdictions until about 2042-44.

*American Samoa (unchanged, `DEC-2026-11-18-05`, Espen's W5).* American Samoa's industry minimums rise by $0.40 an hour every third year from September 30, 2015 until they equal the federal minimum [EV-WRK-425]; garment work pays at least $5.78 and fish canning $6.36 since September 30, 2024 [EV-WRK-467] [EV-WRK-474]. GAO found the old schedule would reach $7.25 by 2036 and that any federal increase lengthens the time [EV-WRK-472]; against LAB-4.01's floor rising with the median after 2035, the gap never closes (project arithmetic). From 2007 to 2014 employment fell 4% and cannery employment 50% [EV-WRK-472]. No median wage is published for the territory, so the cap in B cannot be computed.
- *Recommended:* American Samoa keeps the schedule in today's law; the commission (LAB-4.04) reports on American Samoa each year and may recommend a faster path to Congress, or the slower path once a median is published. *Alternatives:* LAB-4.01's schedule from 2030, a jump of several dollars an hour in the first year alone, or a separate, faster statutory path set now.
- *Why:* the floor's own design measures its reach before going further (memo 1). GAO's reports (GAO-17-83 and GAO-20-467, read in summary) [EV-WRK-472] [EV-WRK-473] describe falling employment and real earnings, with the wage increases a factor but not the main one in cannery closures. The full reports are not read (Evidence still needed).

## 6. Rural delivery

The rural direction asks each dossier to look first at USDA's local channels (`DEC-2026-11-09-26`; `dossiers/foundations/rural-delivery.md`). USDA runs no labor program, so its channels serve as front doors and partners, not as payers:
- **Where the provisions reach rural workers.** Unemployment and wage insurance, apprenticeship and the WIOA share reach rural workers through the state UI agencies' online and phone claims and about 2,300 American Job Centers [EV-WRK-422]. Wage and Hour's and OSHA's directed inspections (LAB-7.02) put agriculture, food processing and construction on the first list, which is where rural violations concentrate (IMM-4.16).
- **Rural Development's offices as a front door** (working choice). Its state and area offices, more than 400 in all [SRC-2158], already serve as front doors for the child benefit (FAM-1.10) and the registry (IMM-6.04). For work they display and help with online unemployment and wage insurance claims, the monthly wage insurance statement (LAB-18.08) and apprenticeship referrals, and pass questions to the state agency the same day. They decide nothing. Rural Development lost about 36% of its staff in the first half of 2025 [SRC-2143], to an estimated 3,058 FTE in FY2026 [SRC-2130]; its field rebuild is booked in housing's HOU-14.01, which four other flagships already draw on, so work adds a referral role only and no staff ask.
- **Cooperative Extension** (working choice): USDA's land-grant extension offices work in or near almost every county (no official count is in the register), the most local federal channel in farm counties. Labor's outreach grants (LAB-7.05) may fund extension programs that teach heat safety (LAB-6.01) and wage rights to farmworkers and small rural employers, alongside community organizations and unions.
- **Rural apprenticeship.** Sponsors in rural places are mostly community colleges, joint programs and sector partnerships (LAB-8.01). Whether Rural Development's Community Facilities loans, which finance rural clinics and child care centers (`rural-delivery.md`, section 5), could also finance training space for rural colleges is left open (part 8). LAB-8.03's transport supports are weighted to rural trainees' distances, as the secure floor's on-ramps are (`dossiers/secure-floor/5-delivery.md`, part 6).
- **National service.** Rural host sites (schools, clinics, disaster recovery, county election offices) are recruited by the state commissions with the rest (working choice); LAB-9.04's target for serving outside one's home region sends members from cities to rural host sites and the reverse.
- **The floor's rural reach.** The floor bites harder where wages are lower: nonmetro median household income was 12% below the national median after a cost-of-living adjustment in 2023 [EV-WRK-405]. The commission measures rural counties first (LAB-4.04), the check memo 1 put in place of the legacy county circuit breaker.

## 8. What delivery leaves open

- **How many states hold hours in wage records, and the cost of adding them everywhere.** Studied in implementation: the 2027-28 survey sizes it; LAB-18.08's monthly statement carries payment meanwhile.
- **The tribal employers question.** Whether the labor act reaches tribal governments' commercial enterprises is unsettled in the circuits (section 4, "Where particular groups fall"); the act is silent and the Board's practice applies. To check in drafting.
- **Wage insurance's caseload and take-up.** Memo 8's range rests on the displaced-worker survey [EV-WRK-315]; the first two years' state counts confirm it.
- **The pace of hiring.** Whether Wage and Hour and OSHA can hire about 120 each a year net and the NLRB 400-600 in all, against federal hiring limits as they stand in 2029. If not, the floors' dates move, not the floors.
