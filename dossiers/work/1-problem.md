# Fair pay and real power at work: the problem

Role: section 1 of the work dossier (flagship 5, `charter/PLAN.md` 3.1: "labor law, wage floors, sectoral standards, support when jobs change"). It covers what is wrong, for whom and how large, and how the law will stand in January 2029 (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`), in five parts:
- A. pay at the low end (the wage floor);
- B. power at work (unions, bargaining and the labor board);
- C. the rules in practice (wage theft, misclassification, overtime, safety and heat);
- D. support when jobs change (unemployment insurance, notice, retraining, apprenticeship, AI);
- E. time, place and the share of income that goes to work (cross-cutting).

Part F is the January 2029 baseline table, part G the seams with decided dossiers, and part H the legacy record weighed.

## In brief

- **The federal wage floor has not moved in more than 17 years.** It has been $7.25 since July 24, 2009, longer than any earlier gap in the Labor Department's table [EV-WRK-002] [EV-WRK-003]. The tipped cash wage of $2.13 and the $4.25 youth wage are older still [EV-WRK-004] [EV-WRK-005] [EV-WRK-006]. EPI puts the floor's real value 41% below 1968 [EV-WRK-023].
- **Few people earn exactly the federal floor; many earn little above it.** In 2024, 82,000 hourly workers earned exactly $7.25 and 760,000 less, together 1.0% of hourly workers [EV-WRK-019]. Thirty states and DC set higher floors [EV-WRK-021]; DC is at $18.40 [EV-WRK-015]. But 20 states still use $7.25 (project count from the Labor Department's table) [EV-WRK-012] [EV-WRK-013] [EV-WRK-014], and Oxfam estimates 39 million workers earn under $17 an hour [EV-WRK-028]. Pay at the bottom grew fastest after the pandemic [EV-WRK-025] [EV-WRK-026].
- **Whether a higher floor costs jobs is contested.** CBO's central estimate for $15 was 1.3 million to 1.4 million fewer jobs and 0.9 million to 1.3 million fewer people in poverty [EV-WRK-029] [EV-WRK-032]. Peer-reviewed work splits: no net loss of low-wage jobs in 138 state increases [EV-WRK-033], against mostly negative estimates in a broad review [EV-WRK-034]. Evidence of employer wage-setting power is one reason the effects can be small [EV-WRK-035] [EV-WRK-037].
- **Most people approve of unions; few have one.** Gallup finds 71% approval [EV-WRK-105]; BLS counts 10.0% of workers as members, half the 1983 rate, and 5.9% in the private sector [EV-WRK-100] [EV-WRK-101].
- **The labor board has more work, less money in real terms and an unsettled legal footing.** Union election petitions doubled from FY2021 to FY2024 [EV-WRK-109]. The board's budget was flat for nine years while field staff fell a third [EV-WRK-118]. It had no quorum for most of 2025 [EV-WRK-115] [EV-WRK-135]. Its remedies carry no penalties [EV-WRK-129]. After *Trump v. Slaughter* its members' protection from removal is unlikely to hold [EV-WRK-136] [EV-WRK-134].
- **The rules are enforced by a shrinking staff.**
  - The Wage and Hour Division had 611 investigators in May 2025, against 1,232 in 1978, about one for every 278,000 workers [EV-WRK-207] [EV-WRK-208].
  - It recovered $259 million in FY2025 [EV-WRK-200], against estimates of $13 billion to $15 billion a year lost to minimum-wage violations alone [EV-WRK-210] [EV-WRK-211].
  - Federal OSHA could inspect each workplace once in 191 years (AFL-CIO's count) [EV-WRK-232].
- **Several 2024 worker rules are gone or going.** The overtime rule was vacated [EV-WRK-222]. The independent-contractor rule is not enforced and is proposed for rescission [EV-WRK-218] [EV-WRK-219]. Home care workers' wage coverage is suspended [EV-WRK-225]. The heat standard is still a proposal, now set for a supplemental proposal in December 2026 [EV-WRK-237].
- **Losing a job costs more and is cushioned less.**
  - 3.3 million long-tenured workers were displaced in 2023-25, up 746,000 on the previous survey [EV-WRK-315]. Only 49% of those back in full-time work earned as much as before [EV-WRK-317]. Losses last for years [EV-WRK-321] [EV-WRK-322].
  - Unemployment insurance reached 27% to 29% of unemployed people [EV-SEC-045] [EV-SEC-046] and replaced 43% of wages [EV-WRK-302]. Only 18 state trust funds meet the solvency standard [EV-WRK-304].
  - Trade Adjustment Assistance, the one federal program built for workers displaced by trade, has been closed to new workers since July 2022 [EV-WRK-330].
- **AI has not yet shown up in the overall labor market, but young workers in exposed jobs may already feel it.** Studies agree there is no broad displacement so far [EV-WRK-357] [EV-WRK-358] [EV-WRK-363]. They disagree on whether entry-level employment in exposed occupations has fallen: one finds it 19% below trend [EV-WRK-355], others find little sign [EV-WRK-361].

## Part A. Pay at the low end

### A.1 The legal floor

| Item | Current law | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal minimum wage | $7.25 an hour, the last of three steps under the 2007 amendments ($5.85, $6.55, $7.25) | [EV-WRK-001] |
| In effect since | July 24, 2009; no increase since. The longest earlier gaps in the Labor Department's table ran from September 1997 to July 2007 and from January 1981 to April 1990 | [EV-WRK-002] [EV-WRK-003] |
| Tipped cash wage | $2.13, frozen at its August 20, 1996 level, with a tip credit of up to $5.12 | [EV-WRK-004] [EV-WRK-005] |
| Youth wage | $4.25 for workers under 20 in their first 90 days | [EV-WRK-006] |
| Disability subminimum (section 14(c)) | By special certificate; 642 employers reported paying 35,534 workers below the minimum on October 1, 2025, down from about 424,000 workers in 2001. Sixteen states had ended the certificates by January 2025. The Labor Department withdrew its proposal to phase them out on July 7, 2025 | [EV-WRK-007] [EV-WRK-008] [EV-WRK-009] [EV-WRK-010] [EV-WRK-011] |
| Federal contractors | The contractor minimum wage (Executive Order 14026) was revoked on March 14, 2025 and is no longer enforced | [EV-WRK-038] [EV-WRK-039] |

**What $7.25 buys.** Full-time, year-round work at $7.25 earns $15,080, which CRS put at 72% of the poverty threshold for a family of three, on 2020 thresholds [EV-WRK-022]. In real terms EPI puts the floor 30% below its value 15 years ago and 41% below 1968, in January 2025 dollars (EPI, a labor-aligned think tank) [EV-WRK-023]. A St. Louis Fed blog agrees on the direction [EV-WRK-024]. Both are analysis from price indexes; the direction is not in dispute.

### A.2 States have moved; a third of them have not

The Labor Department's table, updated November 5, 2026, shows:
- **no state law** in Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina and Tennessee [EV-WRK-012];
- **a state rate of $7.25** in Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, New Hampshire, North Carolina, North Dakota, Pennsylvania, Texas, Utah and Wisconsin [EV-WRK-013];
- **a rate below $7.25 that federal law overrides** in Georgia and Wyoming, with Oklahoma at the federal rate by reference [EV-WRK-014];
- **the highest floors** in the District of Columbia ($18.40), Washington ($17.13), New York City and its suburbs ($17.00), Connecticut ($16.94) and California ($16.90) [EV-WRK-015].

That makes 20 states where the federal $7.25 is the working floor. The Labor Department does not print the count; it is the project's count from its table. NCSL counts the five states without a law and three states below $7.25 together [EV-WRK-018]. BLS reports 30 states and DC above the federal floor in 2024 [EV-WRK-021].

Scheduled steps before 2029 include Michigan to $15.00 on January 1, 2027 [EV-WRK-016], Alaska to $15 in July 2027, Hawaii to $18 in January 2028 and Nebraska's indexing from January 2027 [EV-WRK-017]. Many states index each January (the Labor Department's table; no current source gives the count). The gap between the federal floor and the states that have moved will therefore be wider in 2029 than now.

### A.3 Who is paid at or near the floor

- **At or below $7.25:** 82,000 hourly workers earned exactly $7.25 in 2024 and 760,000 less. Together that is 1.0% of 80.3 million hourly workers, against 13.4% in 1979 [EV-WRK-019].
- **Who they are:** workers under 25 were one-fifth of hourly workers but 43% of those at or below the minimum. About two-thirds worked in leisure and hospitality [EV-WRK-020].
- **The larger group just above it:** the count at $7.25 is small because most employers in most places pay more; the policy question is the much larger group a little above it.
  - Oxfam America, an advocacy group, estimates that over 39 million workers, 23% of the workforce, earned under $17 an hour in 2024 [EV-WRK-028]. It is a modeled estimate from survey data and has not been checked against a government count (Evidence still needed).
  - A preprint finds the share of hourly workers below $15 in real terms fell from 38% to 13% between 2014 and 2024 [EV-WRK-027].
  - The two use different thresholds and methods. Both show the low-wage share shrinking, and both leave tens of millions of workers below the levels in today's bills.

**Recent trends.**
- EPI reports that the real hourly wage of the lowest-paid tenth rose 15.3% from 2019 to 2024, faster than any other part of the distribution [EV-WRK-025].
- Autor, Dube and McGrew find that the post-pandemic rise at the bottom undid about a third of the four-decade growth in the gap between the 90th and 10th percentiles. They attribute it partly to employers' weaker wage-setting power in a tight labor market [EV-WRK-026].
- Rural workers at the bottom gained less: ERS finds total compensation in the bottom tenth of rural earners rose 20% between 2000-04 and 2020-24, against nearly 43% at the top, and credits state minimum wage increases for part of the bottom's gain [EV-WRK-406].
- The 2025 figures will be less certain than usual, because the October 2025 Current Population Survey was not collected during the shutdown (context of EV-WRK-100).

### A.4 The evidence on employment effects: two views

| View | Finding | Who | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Some job loss at $15 | $15 by 2025: pay up for 17 million, 1.3 million jobless (median; two-thirds range about zero to 3.7 million), 1.3 million out of poverty. $12 by 2025: 0.3 million jobless, 0.4 million out of poverty | CBO, July 2019 | [EV-WRK-029] [EV-WRK-030] |
| | Raise the Wage Act of 2021 ($15 by June 2025): 1.4 million fewer jobs (0.9%), 0.9 million fewer in poverty, $54 billion more deficit over 2021-31 | CBO, February 2021 | [EV-WRK-032] |
| | 79.2% of estimated employment effects negative, 46.2% negative and significant, stronger for teens and the less educated | Neumark and Shirley (2022), a review | [EV-WRK-034] |
| Little or no job loss in the range studied | 138 state increases, 1979-2016: the number of low-wage jobs essentially unchanged after five years, with some loss in tradeable sectors | Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer (2019) | [EV-WRK-033] |
| Why the effect can be small | Employers' wage-setting power lowers wages by roughly 20% against a competitive market (Treasury, 2022, Biden administration, a literature review); most local labor markets are highly concentrated, and concentration is linked to 5% to 17% lower posted wages | Treasury; Azar, Marinescu and Steinbaum | [EV-WRK-035] [EV-WRK-036] [EV-WRK-037] |

How to read it:
- The studies cover different ranges. The state increases Cengiz and others study reach roughly $10 to $13 in nominal terms. CBO's $15 national floor goes beyond what any state study fully covers, especially in low-wage states and counties (context of EV-WRK-033).
- CBO names the two things it is least sure of: how fast wages would grow anyway, and how strongly employment responds [EV-WRK-031].
- Both camps include authors with long-standing positions. One of the Cengiz authors works at EPI; Neumark has long been a critic of large increases. The register records both as peer-reviewed, and section 2 weighs them.
- No CBO estimate after 2021 is in the register (Evidence still needed).

**The California fast-food wage** is the newest large test.
- **The law:** AB 1228 set $20 an hour from April 1, 2024 and created a council that may raise it by up to 3.5% a year. The council's powers end on January 1, 2029 [EV-WRK-151].
- **The findings:** a UC Berkeley study finds wages up about 11%, no employment loss and prices up 1.5% [EV-WRK-153]. Dube finds wages up about 7%, with employment effects ranging from slightly negative to slightly positive across 32 specifications [EV-WRK-154]. Clemens, Edwards and Meer find fast-food employment down 2.7% to 3.2% against the rest of the country, about 18,000 jobs [EV-WRK-155].
- **Why they differ:** the main difference is the comparison group, and all three are working papers. EPI's review of four studies sides with the first two [EV-WRK-156]. Section 1 records the dispute and does not settle it.

### A.5 Bills and the 2025 law

- **The Higher Wages for American Workers Act** (S. 2013; Hawley, with Welch) would set $15 from the January after enactment, then index it [EV-WRK-040]. It was referred to committee on June 10, 2025 [EV-WRK-041].
- **The Raise the Wage Act of 2025** (H.R. 2743 and S. 1332) would rise from $9.50 to $17.00 over five years and then index. It would also phase out the tipped and youth wages and 14(c) [EV-WRK-042] [EV-WRK-043]. Both versions were referred to committee on April 8, 2025 [EV-WRK-044] [EV-WRK-045]. Neither bill's status was rechecked on Congress.gov today (Evidence still needed).
- **Tips and overtime deductions.** The 2025 reconciliation law (Pub. L. 119-21) added two income-tax deductions:
  - **Tips:** up to $25,000 of qualified tips [EV-WRK-046], in occupations that customarily received tips by the end of 2024 [EV-WRK-047];
  - **Overtime:** up to $12,500 ($25,000 joint) of the overtime premium [EV-WRK-048] [EV-WRK-049];
  - **Phase-out:** both shrink by $100 for each $1,000 of income above $150,000 ($300,000 joint) [EV-WRK-046] [EV-WRK-048];
  - **Expiry:** both end for tax years beginning after December 31, 2028 [EV-WRK-047] [EV-WRK-048].

  They lower income tax for people who owe it; they do not change any wage. For the January 2029 baseline, tax year 2028 is their last year.

### A.6 Where the wage floor meets income support

A higher wage is partly offset for low-income families as benefits phase out.
- CBO found that 78% of low- and moderate-income taxpayers faced marginal rates above 19% in 2016 once phase-outs were counted [EV-SEC-537]. Its example single parent faced rates averaging 75% over a narrow income range [EV-SEC-538].
- The secure floor dossier holds this evidence and the plan's own phase-out rules (`dossiers/shared-rules.md`, rule 2; `dossiers/secure-floor/1-problem.md`). Shared rule 1's working answer counts wage gains as income for rent (`dossiers/shared-rules.md`, rule 1).
- Section 1 does not measure how much of a wage increase a family keeps under the plan's decided benefits. That needs the household model (Evidence still needed).

## Part B. Power at work

### B.1 Few workers have a union

| Measure (2025 unless noted) | Figure | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Union members | 14.7 million, 10.0% of wage and salary workers; 20.1% and 17.7 million in 1983 | [EV-WRK-100] |
| By sector | Public 32.9%, private 5.9%; about equal numbers of members (7.3 million and 7.4 million) | [EV-WRK-101] |
| Represented by a union (covered by a contract) | 16.5 million, 11.2%, including 1.8 million non-members | [EV-WRK-102] |
| International comparison | Adjusted bargaining coverage 11.1% in 2024; bargaining at company level, with no mechanism to extend agreements to other employers (OECD/AIAS) | [EV-WRK-104] |
| Pay gap, raw | Nonunion full-time workers' median weekly pay is 84% of members' ($1,174 against $1,404), with no controls | [EV-WRK-103] |

The 2025 BLS figures are averages of 11 months, because October 2025 was not collected during the shutdown, so the small change from 2024 is within survey noise (context of EV-WRK-100). The raw pay gap is not an estimate of what a union adds; section 2 takes up that research.

### B.2 Demand for unions

- **Gallup, August 2026:** 71% approve of unions, tying 2022's reading; the low was 48% in 2009 [EV-WRK-105]. A record 47% want unions to have more influence and 23% less. Only 27% expect unions to get stronger. 52% of Republicans approve [EV-WRK-106].
- **Would workers vote for one?** In a 2017 national survey, 48% of nonunion workers said they would vote for a union, up from about a third in 1977 and 1995 [EV-WRK-107].
- **The qualification:** EPI's 2024 review of six surveys finds willingness well below general approval. In three of the four most recent surveys, "don't know" was the most common answer; forced to choose, the 2017 survey split 48 to 52 [EV-WRK-108].
- So the gap between wanting a union and having one is real but smaller than headline approval suggests. These are stated preferences, not behaviour.

- The House committee's FY2026 bill would have cut the board by a third [EV-WRK-120]; the enacted cut was 1.7%.
- An employer-side consultancy's tally finds unions won 78% of representation elections in the first nine months of 2026 [EV-WRK-159].

### B.4 What happens in organizing campaigns

- **Charges against employers.** EPI found employers were charged with breaking the law in 41.5% of union elections in 2016-17, with illegal-firing charges in 19.9% under its narrowest measure [EV-WRK-121]. It estimated employers spend about $340 million a year on anti-union consultants; no independent source checks that figure [EV-WRK-122].
- **The contesting view.** The Heritage Foundation argues that violations the board actually finds are rare: firings in 6% of elections, threats in 7% [EV-WRK-123]. The two measure different things, charges against findings, and Heritage's analysis is from 2009.
  - The board's own merit rate across all charges, 32.8% then 22.0% [EV-WRK-110] [EV-WRK-112], supports neither extreme.
  - No current employer-side analysis of recent elections was found (Evidence still needed).
- **First contracts.**
  - Bloomberg Law finds the average time from certification to a first contract was 409 days in its 2021 sample (median 356) [EV-WRK-124] and 463 days in 2026 [EV-WRK-126].
  - A study of 226 elections in 2018 found 63% of new unions had no contract after a year and 43% after two [EV-WRK-125].
  - No source measures the share that never reach one.
- **Captive-audience meetings.** The board held mandatory anti-union meetings unlawful in November 2024, for the future only [EV-WRK-127]. The current General Counsel will urge the board to reverse that and the *Cemex* bargaining-order standard [EV-WRK-128].

### B.5 The legal footing

- **Remedies.** The labor law lets the board order a violator to stop and to reinstate workers with back pay. It provides no civil penalty [EV-WRK-129]. Board orders are not self-enforcing; a court of appeals must enforce them (context of EV-WRK-129).
- **Injunctions.** *Starbucks v. McKinney* (2024) requires the stricter four-factor test when the board seeks a 10(j) injunction [EV-WRK-130]. Only four were authorized in FY2026 [EV-WRK-117].
- **Jury trials.** *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) requires a jury trial when the SEC seeks civil penalties for fraud [EV-WRK-131]. Employers have raised it against the board, so far without success, though three appeals courts have set aside the board's broader damages awards [EV-WRK-132].
- **Removal.**
  - Member Wilcox was removed on January 27, 2025, the first such removal in the board's history; the Supreme Court stayed her reinstatement [EV-WRK-133].
  - The D.C. Circuit held the members' removal protection unconstitutional on December 5, 2025 [EV-WRK-134]. The Fifth Circuit held the same for members and the board's judges [EV-WRK-137].
  - *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 29, 2026, 6-3) overruled *Humphrey's Executor* for the FTC. CRS notes the NLRB has the same kind of protection [EV-WRK-136]. The Court did not rule on the NLRB, but nothing in the register suggests its members' protection survives.
  - The practical result: board doctrine can now change with each president, by adjudication and by removal.
- **Bargaining orders.** The 2023 *Cemex* standard is still board law but under attack:
  - the Sixth Circuit rejected it [EV-WRK-138];
  - the Ninth Circuit enforced the order on the older standard without ruling on *Cemex*, and the Supreme Court declined review in October 2026 [EV-WRK-139] [EV-WRK-160];
  - the board declined to overturn it in March 2026 [EV-WRK-140];
  - the General Counsel will ask it to [EV-WRK-128].
- **Right-to-work.** Twenty-six states and Guam have right-to-work laws; Michigan repealed its law in 2023 [EV-WRK-141].
- **Bills in the 119th Congress.**
  - The PRO Act (H.R. 20) was introduced in March 2025 and has had no later action [EV-WRK-143]. As introduced it carries civil penalties of up to $50,000 per violation, and $100,000 for repeat or serious ones [EV-WRK-142].
  - The House passed the Faster Labor Contracts Act 230 to 193 on June 9, 2026, after a discharge petition, with 20 Republicans in favour [EV-WRK-144]. It provides for bargaining within 10 days, mediation after 90 and binding arbitration after that [EV-WRK-145].
  - The House passed the Protect America's Workforce Act 231 to 195 on December 11, 2025 [EV-WRK-146]. It has had no Senate vote [EV-WRK-147].
  - All three die when the Congress ends on January 3, 2027 unless reintroduced (context of EV-WRK-143).
- **Federal workers.** Executive Order 14251 (March 27, 2025) removed collective bargaining from a long list of agencies [EV-WRK-148]. A trade outlet puts the share of the federal workforce affected at about two-thirds [EV-WRK-150]. The Ninth Circuit vacated an injunction against it without ruling on its lawfulness [EV-WRK-149], and the NTEU's challenge was pending in the D.C. Circuit in September 2026 [EV-WRK-150].

### B.6 Sectoral standards and contract work

- **Wage boards.** California's Fast Food Council has not met as a full body in nearly two years, has had no chair for over a year and has made no decisions [EV-WRK-152]. Its powers end on January 1, 2029 [EV-WRK-151]. The care dossier's register holds the boards that work through public payment: Minnesota's nursing home board [EV-CAR-618] and Washington's home care bargaining [EV-CAR-621].
- **Contingent and contract work.** In July 2023 there were 11.9 million independent contractors (7.4% of employment), 2.8 million on-call workers, 945,000 temporary agency workers and 862,000 contract-firm workers [EV-WRK-158] [EV-SEC-120]. Contingent jobs rose from 3.8% of employment in 2017 to 4.3% [EV-WRK-157]. No federal count of app-based work exists yet (Evidence still needed).

## Part C. The rules in practice

### C.1 The wage enforcer is half the size it was

| Measure | Figure | Evidence |

### C.2 How much pay is taken: wide estimates

| Estimate | Figure | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EPI, 2017 (ten largest states) | 2.4 million workers lose $8 billion a year to minimum-wage violations, 17% of eligible low-wage workers; over $15 billion nationally if the rest are alike | [EV-WRK-209] [EV-WRK-210] |
| Rutgers and Northwestern, 2025 | At least 4 million workers paid below the minimum each year, losing about $3,000 each, over $13 billion in all | [EV-WRK-211] |
| Broken Laws survey, 2009 (Chicago, Los Angeles, New York) | 26% paid below the minimum in the prior week; 76% of those working overtime not paid the overtime rate | [EV-WRK-212] |
| Clemens and Strain (AEI and NBER) | Underpayment rises 12 to 17 cents for each dollar of minimum-wage increase | [EV-WRK-213] |
| BLS, 2024 (federal minimum only) | 760,000 hourly workers below $7.25 | [EV-WRK-019] |

These measure different things: state minimums against the federal one, total pay including overtime and tips, a single week against a year. The survey-based estimates are analysis graded C, and self-reported pay makes measurement error cut both ways (context of EV-WRK-209). Clemens and Strain, from a right-of-center institution, confirm underpayment is real and grows with the minimum. No published critique arguing the EPI estimate is overstated was found (Evidence still needed). Every estimate is far above what is recovered.

**Child labor.** Cases with child labor violations rose from 704 in FY2013 to 976 in FY2025, and minors employed illegally from 1,393 to 5,272 [EV-WRK-203]. Minors in prohibited hazardous jobs rose to 773 in FY2025 from 365 a year earlier [EV-WRK-204]. Rising counts may reflect more violations, more enforcement attention, or both.

EPI reports that six states weakened child labor protections in 2024, though more states strengthened them than weakened them [EV-WRK-246]. Twenty-eight states have seen bills to weaken them since 2021 [EV-WRK-247].

### C.3 Misclassification

- **Scale.** No current national estimate exists. GAO reported that the national extent is unknown. It cited IRS's estimate that 3.4 million employees were misclassified in 1984, and a study for the Labor Department that found misclassified workers at 10% to 30% of audited employers in nine states [EV-WRK-215] [EV-WRK-216].
- **The 2024 rule.** The rule took effect on March 11, 2024 [EV-WRK-217]. Since May 1, 2025 the Wage and Hour Division has not applied it in investigations [EV-WRK-218]. A proposal to rescind it and return to the 2021 test was published on February 27, 2026 [EV-WRK-219]; the regulatory agenda lists a final rule for October 2026 [EV-WRK-220]. No final rule had been published when checked.
- **What it means.** The test decides who is covered by wage, overtime and unemployment law. The 2023 contractor counts are in B.6 [EV-WRK-158]; the misclassified share of them is unknown.
- **State tests** (California's AB 5 and others) were not researched for this section (Evidence still needed).

### C.4 Overtime and home care

- **Overtime.** The 2024 rule would have raised the salary below which overtime is owed to $1,128 a week [EV-WRK-221]. A federal court vacated it on November 15, 2024 [EV-WRK-222].
  - The Labor Department enforces the 2019 level of $684 a week, and $107,432 a year for highly paid employees [EV-WRK-223].
  - A law firm reports the department announced a formal rescission on May 14, 2026, after the appeals were dismissed [EV-WRK-224]; the Federal Register notice was not read.
- **Home care workers.** Coverage under the 2013 rule is suspended: since July 25, 2025 investigators do not act against agencies that claim the companionship exemption [EV-WRK-225]. The July 2025 proposal [EV-CAR-616] is listed for a final rule in November 2026 [EV-WRK-226]. The care dossier has asked the work flagship to keep home care workers covered (`dossiers/care/3d-design-workforce.md`, point 2; part G).

### C.5 Safety and heat

- **Deaths.** BLS recorded 5,070 fatal work injuries in 2024, a rate of 3.3 per 100,000 full-time workers [EV-WRK-227].
- **Heat deaths: measures differ tenfold.**
  - BLS counts 53 deaths from temperature extremes in 2024 [EV-WRK-228], and the National Safety Council 48 from heat [EV-WRK-229].
  - OSHA's 2024 proposal estimates about 559 heat deaths and 24,656 heat injuries a year, with deaths undercounted by a factor of 14 [EV-WRK-230]. The AFL-CIO estimates about 530 [EV-WRK-231].
  - The gap is mostly deaths where heat is not recorded as the cause. OSHA's estimate is a Biden-era agency model and the AFL-CIO is a party with a stake, so the higher figures are analysis.
  - The CDC counts 2,391 heat-related deaths of all kinds in 2024 [EV-CLP-126].
- **Inspectors and penalties.**
  - The AFL-CIO counts 618 federal and 1,033 state inspectors for 12 million workplaces. At that rate federal OSHA would take 191 years to visit each once, against 84 in 1991 [EV-WRK-232]. Average federal penalties for a serious violation were $4,678 in FY2025 [EV-WRK-233].
  - OSHA's own figures were not read to check these (Evidence still needed).
  - The legal maximums are $16,550 per serious violation and $165,514 per willful or repeat one. They were not adjusted for 2026, because the shutdown left BLS without the October 2025 price data [EV-WRK-234].
- **The heat rule.**
  - **Federal:** OSHA proposed a heat standard on August 30, 2024 [EV-WRK-235] and held hearings in June and July 2025 [EV-WRK-236]. The regulatory agenda now lists a supplemental proposal for December 2026 and final action for October 2027 [EV-WRK-237] [EV-WRK-238]. In April 2026 OSHA narrowed its heat inspection program and dropped its numeric goal [EV-WRK-239].
  - **States with protections:** California, Colorado, Maryland, Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon and Washington [EV-WRK-237]; Virginia must adopt one by May 1, 2028 [EV-WRK-240].
  - **Preemption:** Florida (2024) bars local heat rules [EV-WRK-241] [EV-WRK-242]. A news report says Texas (2023) restricts local ordinances generally [EV-WRK-242].
  - **Congress:** the Asunción Valdivia heat bill (H.R. 4443) was introduced in July 2025 [EV-WRK-243].
- **Non-competes** (their home is the competition topic, COM-4). The FTC estimated about 30 million workers are bound by one [EV-WRK-244]. Its rule was vacated in court; the FTC dropped its appeal on September 5, 2025 [EV-WRK-245].

## Part D. Support when jobs change

### D.1 Job loss is common and its costs last

- **The latest survey.** BLS's January 2026 survey counts 3.3 million workers displaced in 2023-25 from jobs held at least three years, up 746,000 on the previous survey; 66.1% had work again by January 2026 [EV-WRK-315]. Counting all tenures, 7.4 million were displaced [EV-WRK-316].
- **Lower pay after.** Of long-tenured workers back in full-time jobs, about 49% earned as much as before, down from 62% two years earlier [EV-WRK-317].
- **Why jobs ended.** Abolished positions accounted for 44.4%, closings or moves 32.6% and insufficient work 22.9%. Manufacturing accounted for 642,000 [EV-WRK-319].
- **Little warning.** Only about 45% got written advance notice, 58% even when a plant closed [EV-WRK-318].
- **The research on long-run losses:**
  - High-tenure workers leaving distressed firms lost about 25% of earnings a year for years afterwards [EV-WRK-321].
  - Workers in mass layoffs lose 1.4 years of earnings if displaced in good times and 2.8 years in a downturn [EV-WRK-322]. German data show the same doubling in downturns [EV-WRK-324].
  - In Washington State, the losses came mostly from lower hourly pay, not from losing a good employer's premium [EV-WRK-323].
  - Coal workers most exposed to the industry's decline lost 1.6 years of pay [EV-CLP-516].
  - No study in the register contests that the losses exist. Some qualify their size and cause; Canada's coal workers recovered faster [EV-CLP-518].

### D.3 Notice, trade adjustment and the workforce system

- **Notice of layoffs.** The WARN Act requires 60 days' written notice [EV-WRK-325]. It covers only employers with 100 or more workers, and only closings of 50 or more at one site or mass layoffs of similar size [EV-WRK-326]. It allows shorter notice in three cases [EV-WRK-327]. No agency enforces it; workers must sue [EV-WRK-328] [EV-WRK-329]. That fits the low notice rates in D.1 [EV-WRK-318].
- **Trade Adjustment Assistance.**
  - TAA has certified no new workers since July 1, 2022 [EV-WRK-330].
  - Its wage insurance paid workers 50 and older half the gap between old and new pay, up to $10,000 over two years [EV-WRK-331]. Only 11% of participants used it in FY2021 [EV-WRK-332].
  - Evaluations of TAA's training are mixed [EV-SEC-147] [EV-SEC-148].
- **WIOA funding.** The main workforce grants are roughly flat in nominal dollars, which means a real decline:
  - Adult: $845.6 million in FY2018 and $875.6 million in FY2026 [EV-WRK-335];
  - Youth: $903.4 million and $948.1 million [EV-WRK-336];
  - Dislocated Worker: $1,208.7 million and $1,396.4 million [EV-WRK-337].
- **The administration's proposals** (proposals, not law):
  - fold 12 programs into one $3.43 billion grant, $1.23 billion below their combined FY2026 level [EV-WRK-339], with a 10% apprenticeship set-aside [EV-WRK-353];
  - eliminate Job Corps [EV-WRK-340]. Congress kept the separate programs and Job Corps in FY2026.
- **Job Corps.** The 2025 pause was rescinded in an August 2026 settlement covering 99 centers [EV-WRK-341]. The operators' association says the program will stay open through at least January 31, 2029 [EV-WRK-342]. Both rest on advocacy and trade-press accounts, not the court record.
- **Workforce Pell.** Grants for short programs of 150 to under 600 clock hours started on July 1, 2026 under the 2025 law [EV-WRK-344]. Programs must show 70% completion and 70% job placement [EV-WRK-345].

### D.4 Registered apprenticeship

- **Size.** Active apprentices rose from 638,234 in FY2023 to 679,960 in FY2024, with about 311,000 new apprentices a year [EV-WRK-347]. FY2025 counts run from over 702,000 [EV-WRK-348] to 703,475 [EV-WRK-349].
- **Completion.** About 42% finish within a year of the expected date, against 34% of first-time two-year college students finishing on time (GAO) [EV-WRK-350].
- **The federal goal.** Executive Order 14278 (April 2025) asks for a plan to reach 1 million new active apprentices, without a deadline [EV-WRK-351]. EPI reads it as 1 million a year [EV-WRK-352]. The budget restates it as 1 million active apprentices [EV-WRK-353]. Reaching 1 million active would take a rise of about 42% from FY2025 (project arithmetic on EV-WRK-349).
- **Results.** Apprenticeship's earnings gains, its cost per apprentice and its care-sector growth are in other registers [EV-SEC-144] [EV-SEC-640] [EV-CAR-633].

**Wage insurance's record.** The best-evaluated U.S. support for displaced workers is TAA's wage insurance.
- Comparing workers just above and below its age cutoff, eligibility raised employment by 8 to 17 points in the first two years and earnings by more than $18,000. The authors find the program paid for itself [EV-WRK-365].
- A related paper finds wage insurance and training had similar long-run effects [EV-WRK-366].
- Take-up was low [EV-WRK-367] [EV-WRK-332].

## Part E. Time, place and labor's share

- **Schedules.**
  - In a large survey of hourly workers at big retail and food-service firms in 2018-19, 26% worked on-call shifts, 67% had shift times changed and 45% worked "clopening" shifts. About 60% got under two weeks' notice of their schedules [EV-WRK-400].
  - Unstable schedules were linked to worse sleep, more strongly than having a young child or working nights [EV-WRK-401].
  - Seattle's 2017 scheduling law raised two-weeks' notice by 11 points and cut unpaid last-minute changes by 13, with better sleep. Over 40% of covered workers still lacked two weeks' notice two years on [EV-WRK-402].
  - A randomized trial at Gap stores found stable scheduling raised sales 7% and productivity 5%. Only 30% of the swings in weekly hours tracked customer traffic [EV-WRK-403].
  - The survey is not a probability sample, and no federal survey measures schedule notice regularly (Evidence still needed). Paid sick leave reaches 41% of the lowest-paid tenth of private workers, against 96% of the top tenth [EV-SEC-302].
- **Labor's share.** BLS's index of labor's share of income in the nonfarm business sector (2017=100) fell from 112.8 in early 2000 to 93.7 in the third quarter of 2026 [EV-WRK-404]. It is an index, so it shows the direction and size of the change, not a level.
- **Rural places.**
  - Nonmetro median household income was $60,459 in 2023. Even after ERS's adjustment for lower living costs, it stays 12% below the national median [EV-WRK-405].
  - Rural workers in the bottom tenth gained less than those at the top over two decades [EV-WRK-406].
  - A national wage floor bites harder where local wages are lower, which is why the legacy design added a county circuit breaker [EV-WRK-700]. The register has no county-level measure of how many workers a given floor would reach (Evidence still needed).
- **Immigrant workers** are named in OUT-06.
  - About 9.6 million unauthorized immigrants were in the labor force in 2023 [EV-IMM-008], 5.6% of the workforce on Pew's count [EV-IMM-010].
  - Immigrants are 30% of direct care workers [EV-CAR-614].
  - Section 1 holds no evidence on wage violations by immigration status (Evidence still needed). E-Verify's worker protections are this flagship's (part G).

## What the evidence leaves for section 3

These are the questions the evidence raises. Section 1 answers none of them, and none is a recommendation.
1. **The wage floor's level and indexing** against the employment evidence, and whether one national floor fits the lowest-wage states and counties (A.2, A.4, E).
2. **Subminimums:** the tipped, youth and 14(c) wages (A.1).
