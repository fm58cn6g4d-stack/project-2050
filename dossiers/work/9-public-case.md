## The brief

**Pay that keeps up, power that holds, and help when a job ends.**

The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 24, 2009, the longest stretch without a raise in the Labor Department's table (Department of Labor, 2026) [EV-WRK-002] [EV-WRK-003]. A full-time year at $7.25 earns $15,080 (Congressional Research Service, 2021) [EV-WRK-022]. The Economic Policy Institute, a labor-aligned think tank, estimates its real value is 41% below 1968's (Economic Policy Institute, 2025) [EV-WRK-023]. In 20 states $7.25 is still the working floor (the project's count from the Department of Labor's table, 2026) [EV-WRK-012] [EV-WRK-013] [EV-WRK-014]. The tipped cash wage has been $2.13 since 1996 (the United States Code; Congressional Research Service, 2021) [EV-WRK-004] [EV-WRK-005].

Meanwhile:
- **Few have a union.** 10.0% of workers were union members in 2025, and 5.9% in the private sector (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026) [EV-WRK-100] [EV-WRK-101], while 71% of Americans approve of unions (Gallup, 2026) [EV-WRK-105].
- **The labor board can't hold its ground.** It had no quorum from the end of January 2025 until January 7, 2026 (NLRB, 2025 and 2026; Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-WRK-115] [EV-WRK-116] [EV-WRK-135]. Its remedies carry no civil penalty (the National Labor Relations Act) [EV-WRK-129]. New unions waited an average of 463 days for a first contract in 2026 (Bloomberg Law, 2026) [EV-WRK-126].
- **The rules go unenforced.** The Wage and Hour Division had 611 investigators in May 2025, against 1,232 in 1978 (Economic Policy Institute, 2025) [EV-WRK-207]. It recovered $259 million in FY2025 (Department of Labor, 2026) [EV-WRK-200]. Researchers estimate workers lose more than $13 billion to $15 billion a year to minimum-wage violations alone (Rutgers researchers, 2025; Economic Policy Institute, 2017) [EV-WRK-211] [EV-WRK-210].
- **Losing a job costs more and is cushioned less.** 3.3 million workers who had held their jobs at least three years were displaced in 2023-25. Of those back in full-time work, only about 49% earned as much as before, and only about 45% of the displaced got written notice (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026) [EV-WRK-315] [EV-WRK-317] [EV-WRK-318]. Unemployment insurance reached 27% to 29% of unemployed people (Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, 2025; National Employment Law Project, 2025) [EV-SEC-045] [EV-SEC-046]. Only 18 state trust funds met the solvency standard at the start of 2026 (Department of Labor, 2026) [EV-WRK-304].

**What the plan does.** Two laws in 2029, executive steps from January 20, 2029, and a bipartisan service bill.

1. **A floor that keeps up** (the labor act).
   - The federal minimum rises in six yearly steps: **$9.50 on January 1, 2030**, then $11.00 (2031), $12.50 (2032), $14.00 (2033), $15.50 (2034) and **$17.00 on January 1, 2035**, the Raise the Wage Act's schedule (H.R. 2743, 2025) [EV-WRK-042]. From January 1, 2036 it rises each year with the median wage, about $17.60 in 2036 on our estimates. It never falls (LAB-4.01, 4.02).
   - A step waits a year if unemployment is rising at the October 1 check before it (LAB-4.03).
   - A wage commission of workers, employers and economists reports each spring on where the floor bites, rural counties first, and may recommend a higher target for counties where it bites hardest. It may also advise a slower step there, a working choice. It advises only: Congress decides (LAB-4.04).
   - The youth wage ends on January 1, 2030. The tipped cash wage rises in seven steps to the full floor in cash on January 1, 2036. Section 14(c) certificates, which let 642 employers pay 35,534 workers with disabilities below the minimum in 2025 (Department of Labor, 2026) [EV-WRK-008], end over five years: no new certificate is issued after enactment, existing ones continue on their existing terms, with pay rising to the full floor, until Section 14(c) is repealed on January 1, 2034, with a funded move into supported jobs and no loss of day services (LAB-4.06 to 4.09).
   - Employers under 50 workers get a small, temporary payroll credit for the first three steps, 2030-32: about $3 billion on our estimates (range about $1.5-7 billion), paid from the general fund so Social Security and Medicare lose nothing. **Its size is provisional,** with Espen, and the shares and cap are working choices (LAB-5.01; below).
2. **Power that holds** (the labor act, on signing, about mid-2029 on the working timeline).
   - A union is certified when a majority of workers sign cards. A union can still ask for an election, held within 20 days (LAB-1.01, 1.02).
   - No mandatory anti-union meetings. Bargaining starts within 10 days, mediation at 90, binding arbitration 30 days after that, the Faster Labor Contracts Act's timetable, which passed the House 230 to 193 (House Clerk, 2026) [EV-WRK-144] (LAB-1.03, 1.05).
   - Penalties of up to $50,000 per violation and $100,000 for a repeat or serious one (the PRO Act's text, 2025) [EV-WRK-142], sought in federal court, where either side may ask for a jury. Workers can sue on their own if the labor board does not act in 60 days. If the board loses its quorum for 90 days, cases go to court and certifications stand (LAB-2.01 to 2.04).
   - Unions may negotiate fees from everyone they represent, overriding the right-to-work laws of 26 states (National Conference of State Legislatures, 2026) [EV-WRK-141], in a separate title that can fall without the rest (LAB-1A.01).
   - Industry standards boards set binding floors. The first, for fast-food chains, is appointed within 90 days of signing and must set its standard within 18 months, about January 2031 (LAB-3.01, 3.02).
   - Federal workers' bargaining is restored by executive order on January 20, 2029, and then in law once the labor act passes (LAB-16.01). The executive order lasts only while an administration keeps it.
3. **Rules that are enforced.**
   - Wage and Hour investigators rise from 611 to at least 1,200 by FY2034, hired from FY2030, if Congress appropriates the money: the law sets a floor that appropriations may not fall below without a report to Congress. OSHA's inspectors grow in step (LAB-7.01).
   - From signing, for violations after signing: willful wage theft costs three times what was owed, wage claims can go as class actions, and forced arbitration of them ends. Violators are published, which one study found cut violations at nearby employers; no second study confirms it (LAB-7.03 to 7.05).
   - A heat standard: if no final rule exists on January 20, 2029, OSHA finalizes it from its 2024 record; the act's own minimums (water, shade, rest, a plan) apply 180 days after signing; OSHA's final standard is due within a year of signing (LAB-6.01, 6.02).
   - Threatening to report a worker's immigration status for complaining is illegal retaliation, from signing (LAB-7.06).
   - Pay ranges in job postings at employers of 15 or more, and schedules posted 14 days ahead at large retail, food-service and warehouse employers, from January 1, 2031. Posted pay ranges raised wages 1.3-3.6% in one unpublished working paper (Arnold, Quach and Taska, 2025) [EV-WRK-407]. Every covered worker may discuss their own pay, from signing (LAB-7.08, 7.09, LAB-15.01).
   - The overtime salary line is set in law by the 2024 method, never below $1,128 a week, against $684 today (Federal Register, 2024; Department of Labor, 2025) [EV-WRK-221] [EV-WRK-223]: about $1,310-1,370 a week in 2030 on our estimates, from January 1, 2030 (LAB-12.02). Home care workers stay under wage and overtime law, from signing (LAB-12.01).
   - 90 days' notice of a large layoff, counted across an employer's sites and enforced by the Labor Department, from 180 days after signing (LAB-13.01).
4. **Help when a job ends** (the 2029 reconciliation act, by about mid-2029).
   - **Unemployment insurance** in every state, for benefit weeks from January 1, 2031: at least 26 weeks, a benefit of 75% of a worker's high-quarter weekly pay up to at least two-thirds of the state's average wage, part-time workers covered. Whether to keep that 75% from 2031 or phase it in is still with Espen (provisional; below). Extended Benefits become fully federal and switch on automatically in a downturn (LAB-11.01, 11.04). Claimants attest that they are able and available; there are no weekly employer-contact quotas (LAB-11.07).
   - **Wage insurance**, for job losses from January 1, 2031: a worker with three years in the job (a quarter on protected leave does not break the three years, and years at a predecessor employer count where the state's records link the two) who takes a lower-paid one gets half the hourly drop for each hour worked, up to $10,000 a year, for two years. Nobody is asked what caused the job loss; a worker fired for something other than misconduct qualifies (LAB-18.01 to 18.03).
   - **Apprenticeship:** new registered apprentices from 311,035 in FY2025 (Labor Department figures in a Nevada State Apprenticeship Council report, 2026) [EV-WRK-349] to 600,000 a year by FY2034, in construction, energy, care and advanced manufacturing, with grants to sponsors of up to $12,000 per apprentice and support for child care, tools and transport (LAB-8.01, 8.03, 8.04). The 600,000 target is Espen's decision; no evidence in the register shows it can be met.
   - **National service:** 150,000 more full-time positions a year by 2034, ramped from 2030, authorized by a bipartisan bill. If none passes by December 31, 2030, the reconciliation act funds the same scale from FY2031. Up to 50,000 more service-to-apprenticeship places, 200,000 in all, open only once the first 150,000 are filled and completing, on the agency's published report: that is 2035 on the working ramp (LAB-9.01, 9.03, 9.06).

**What it costs, on our estimates.** About **$0.22 trillion over FY2030-39** (range about $0.10-0.44 trillion) and about **$31 billion a year from FY2040** (range about $14-57 billion) on the federal general fund, before the plan's financing (section 6). No official score exists yet; the plan asks CBO and the Joint Committee on Taxation to score the main pieces in 2027-28. These figures hold under stated conditions:
- they assume **no recession**, as CBO's projections do. A moderate two-year downturn would add about $120 billion (range about $100-150 billion) of fully federal Extended Benefits;
- they include the floor's indirect effect on the budget, about $32 billion over FY2030-39 and $5.5 billion in FY2040, which exists only if the labor act passes (CBO's net method, scaled to the plan's smaller bite; range zero to $80 billion over the decade);
- they include wage insurance, about $16 billion over FY2031-39 (range about $8-26 billion), about $2 billion a year once it is running, on our assumption that about 60% of those eligible take it up, a figure no study confirms;
- they leave out the gated service places (up to about $13 billion), the items still with Espen (about $2 billion, plus a standby trigger of up to $5 billion a year when on), the national service grantees' match (about $13 billion over FY2030-39, on AmeriCorps' existing rule, a working assumption; raised by host organizations and states, not federal; it would be federal if Congress waived it) and the agencies' new staff, which the plan's capacity budget carries;
- unemployment benefits rise about $541 billion over FY2031-39 (range about $324-858 billion), paid from the state trust funds and matched over the cycle by employers' state taxes on a larger wage base, so they are not general-fund costs. That is about $42 billion a year more at FY2025 levels (range about $25-66 billion), about twice the $15-25 billion first shown, and roughly doubles employers' state unemployment taxes. Whether to keep the 75% standard from 2031 or phase it in is still with Espen (provisional). Social Security's funds lose about $43 billion over the same years (range about $16-86 billion).

**What the plan is honest about.**
- **Whether a higher floor costs jobs is contested.** CBO estimated 1.3 million to 1.4 million fewer jobs for $15 by 2025 (Congressional Budget Office, 2019 and 2021) [EV-WRK-029] [EV-WRK-032]. A study of 138 state increases found low-wage job counts essentially unchanged (Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer, 2019) [EV-WRK-033]; a broad review found most estimates negative (Neumark and Shirley, 2022) [EV-WRK-034]. The plan's $17 comes in 2035, when on our estimates it is about half the median wage, a smaller bite than CBO's $15 in 2025.
- **One family type keeps too little of a raise in 2030-34.** On our estimates, a full-time single adult who buys coverage on the marketplace in a state that has not expanded Medicaid, and whose pay rises from $7.25 to $12.50 in 2032, keeps about **39% of the raise under the plan, against 59% under 2029 law** (section 7, part 1B). The plan's larger tax credit for workers without children and its food-aid deduction phase out over the same range. The adult is not worse off in dollars than under 2029 law, apart from about $90 a year for paid leave. From 2035, when the single health plan removes the premium, the share is 52-59%. The finding has gone to the owners of the plan's income-support rules and to the financing settlement, which may fix it (section 8, part 6). It is not fixed yet.
- **The labor act is the hardest vote in the plan.** The project's earlier vote simulation gave it a 13% chance in the plan's first Congress (the project's legacy vote simulation) [EV-WRK-712]. Without it, the reconciliation act's parts still run: wage insurance, apprenticeship, the 14(c) grant, service, and unemployment insurance's standards unless the Senate parliamentarian strikes them from it, in which case they move to the labor act and wait with it (section 4). The executive steps last only while an administration keeps them.
- **The state unemployment agencies are the largest delivery risk.** In the pandemic, 17 of 50 states said their systems could not carry out the new federal programs (Department of Labor's Office of Inspector General, 2021) [EV-DEM-295]. The plan gives them a systems grant and a full legislative session before 2031.

## The case in one paragraph

The federal minimum wage has been stuck at $7.25 since 2009, the labor board was without a quorum for most of 2025, and the people who enforce the rules are half as many as in 1978. This plan raises the floor in six yearly steps to $17 in 2035 and then ties it to the median wage, so it never erodes again, with a pause if a recession starts and a commission that watches where it bites. It lets workers form a union by signing cards, gets them a first contract on a deadline, and puts penalties in federal court so the law holds whoever runs the board. It doubles the wage inspectors, if Congress pays for them, and triples the damages for willful wage theft. And from 2031, when a job ends, unemployment insurance reaches more people for 26 weeks, and a worker who takes a lower-paid job gets half the drop for two years, whatever caused the loss.

## Hard questions, plain answers

- **"Won't $17 kill jobs?"** It may cost some. CBO put $15 by 2025 at 1.3 million to 1.4 million fewer jobs (Congressional Budget Office, 2019 and 2021) [EV-WRK-029] [EV-WRK-032]; other careful studies find little or no loss in the range states have tried (Cengiz and others, 2019) [EV-WRK-033]. The plan's floor reaches $17 in 2035, when on our estimates it is about half the median wage, in six yearly steps, with a pause if unemployment rises and a commission that reports county by county where it bites, may recommend a higher target there and may advise a slower step (advice only; Congress decides). A CBO score of this design is asked for in 2027-28.
- **"Won't a raise just be eaten by lost benefits?"** For most families, no, on our estimates. A full-time single parent moving from $7.25 to the floor keeps 81-89% of the raise under the plan, and a married couple with one earner 90-98%; a worker starting at $12.00 keeps about half to three-quarters (section 7, part 1B, in 2029 dollars, with hours held fixed). The weak case is the single adult without children on the marketplace in a state that has not expanded Medicaid: from $7.25 to $12.50 in 2032 they keep about 39% under the plan, against 59% under 2029 law, because two of the plan's own supports phase out over the same range. That case goes to the secure floor's owners and the financing settlement to fix, and it ends in 2035 when the single health plan starts, when the share is 52-59%.
- **"Who pays for the raise?"** Mostly employers and customers, not taxpayers. A peer-reviewed study finds a 10% minimum-wage rise lifts grocery prices about 0.36% (Renkin, Montialoux and Siegenthaler, 2020) [EV-WRK-065]. CBO's 2019 analysis of $15 found real income gains for families below three times the poverty line and losses above it, most of them above six times the line and mostly in business income (Congressional Budget Office, 2019) [EV-WRK-450].
- **"Doesn't card check end the secret ballot?"** No. A union can still choose an election, held within 20 days, and the board checks every card against payroll within 14 days (LAB-1.01, 1.02). Employers can't hold mandatory anti-union meetings, and violations carry penalties.
- **"Why override right-to-work states?"** Every study in the register finds right-to-work laws lower unionization (Fortin, Lemieux and Lloyd, 2022; Eren and Ozbeklik, 2016) [EV-WRK-165] [EV-WRK-166]; their effect on wages is contested. The fee title is separate: if it is dropped or struck down, the rest of the act stands.
- **"Will this crush small business?"** Every employer follows one published schedule, with no size tiers to track. Employers under 50 workers get a temporary credit for the first three steps, about $3 billion on our estimates, and its size is still with Espen. No study shows a credit like this softens job effects, so it is a cushion, not a remedy (section 7, part 6).
- **"Can state unemployment offices handle it?"** It is the plan's biggest delivery risk. States get a one-time systems grant, a floor under their administration money, a full legislative session before January 1, 2031, and a cure period before any penalty. Identity and wage checks use records the government already holds, the lesson of pandemic fraud, which GAO estimated at $100 billion to $135 billion (Government Accountability Office, 2023) [EV-WRK-378].
- **"What about AI?"** No broad displacement shows yet (Yale Budget Lab, 2025 and 2026) [EV-WRK-357] [EV-WRK-358]. One payroll study finds workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed jobs 19% below trend (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab, 2026) [EV-WRK-355]; a job-postings study finds little sign (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, 2026) [EV-WRK-361]. Wage and unemployment insurance never ask what caused a loss, so they reach AI-displaced workers on the same terms. Measuring displacement by occupation every year, and help that grows automatically where it rises, are recommended and still with Espen (memo 10).
- **"What if Congress won't pass the labor act?"** Then the floor, the organizing rules and the enforcement changes wait. The reconciliation act's parts (wage insurance, apprenticeship, the 14(c) grant and service) need only 51 votes and still run, and so do unemployment insurance's standards unless the parliamentarian strikes them, in which case they move to the labor act and wait with it. The January 2029 executive steps hold only while an administration keeps them.

## Stories (composite illustrations, not real people)

On our estimates, in 2029 dollars, with hours held fixed (section 7, part 4):
- **A single mother of two in Texas, full time at $7.25.** By 2035 her gross pay rises from about $13,190 to $30,925. With the plan's other supports she keeps about $14,350 a year more, and her family's net resources reach about 167% of the poverty guideline, before child care and health care, which the plan also provides.
- **A warehouse worker at $12.00 in a state that indexes its minimum, single.** The floor adds about $5,965 a year before tax, and he keeps about $4,510.
- **A machinist laid off at $32 an hour in 2031** after years at one plant, who takes a $24 job. He gets $4 for every hour he works, up to $10,000 a year, for two years (LAB-18.02).
- **A line cook in a state that has not expanded Medicaid, single, full time at $7.25, buying coverage on the marketplace** (section 7, part 1B). When the floor reaches $12.50 in 2032 she keeps about 39% of her raise, against 59% under 2029 law, because the plan's tax credit for workers without children and its food-aid deduction phase out over the same range. That is the case the plan has sent back to be fixed; from 2035 she keeps more.
