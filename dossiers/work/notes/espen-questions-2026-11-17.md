## a. Memo 1 part 2: the 2007 small-business package (`DEC-2026-11-17-02`)

**What it was.**
- The Small Business and Work Opportunity Tax Act of 2007 was the tax title of the Iraq war supplemental, P.L. 110-28, signed May 25, 2007 (The Tax Adviser, 2007; Tax Notes). The same law raised the federal minimum wage from $5.15 to $7.25 in three steps, the last on July 24, 2009 [EV-WRK-002].
- The Joint Committee on Taxation put its tax cuts at about **$4.84 billion over FY2007-16**, against about $4.44 billion of offsets (JCT's table, as reported by Accounting Today).
- It was a general small-business package, not a credit for the cost of the raise:
  - Section 179 expensing raised to $125,000, with a $500,000 phase-out, for 2007-2010;
  - the Work Opportunity Tax Credit extended 44 months and widened, for example to more veterans;
  - the tip credit held at its $5.15 basis, so the raise would not shrink it;
  - both credits allowed against the alternative minimum tax;
  - S-corporation and other technical changes (The Tax Adviser, 2007).
- Only the tip-credit hold was tied to the new wage. JCT's estimate counted only that piece as the raise's revenue effect.

**What is known of its effects.**
- The search found no evaluation of what the package did for small employers' jobs or for minimum-wage workers. The register has none either: no study evaluates a small-business wage credit (`../2-what-works.md`, A.3).
- Espen mentions studies of pairing a wage increase with tax incentives. None was found in the register or the search. If Espen has one in mind, it goes into the register, and memo 1's evidence line changes.
- So the 2007 package is a **political precedent**: the pairing helped a minimum-wage bill pass a split Congress. It is not evidence that a credit softens job effects.

**Does it change the cost range?** No.
- The 2007 package scaled to the 2030s is about $0.01 trillion over a decade (judgment: $4.84 billion grown with nominal GDP, roughly 2-2.5 times from 2007).
- That is below memo 1 C's **$0.02-0.04 trillion**, which was sized as a payroll-linked credit at a third of the legacy credit's range [EV-WRK-703].
- The range stands. The 2007 figure supports its low end: a modest, temporary package is enough for the political purpose.

**For section 4.**
- Write C as a refundable payroll-tax credit for employers under 50 workers.
- It covers a share of the added wage cost for workers below each new step, capped per employer, and ends after the third step.
- Claimed on the quarterly payroll return (Form 941), as memo 1 C says.
- It replaces the budget model's LAB-5 row ($0.10 trillion) at booking.

## b. Memo 6 part 3: the evidence on a right to discuss pay (`DEC-2026-11-17-09`)

**What the study found, plainly** (Cullen and Pakzad-Hurson, *Econometrica*, 2023) [EV-WRK-408].
- *What it studied:* U.S. state laws that protect private-sector workers' right to tell coworkers what they earn. It did not study the posting laws memo 6 A recommends.
- *How:* an event study. Wages in states that adopted these laws were compared before and after adoption, against states that had not yet adopted them. A theoretical model came first.
- *The size:* average wages about **2% lower** after the laws.
- *Who:* the fall was smallest where workers have little individual bargaining power. The authors' reading is that when coworkers can compare, a raise for one worker invites claims from the rest. So employers hold the line harder in one-on-one bargaining, and workers who bargained individually lose most.
- *How strong:* one peer-reviewed study, a credible design, not repeated in the register. It measures average pay, not pay gaps or fairness.
- *Against it:* posting laws, a different law, raised wages 1.3-3.6% with no loss of jobs (Arnold, Quach and Taska, working paper) [EV-WRK-407]. No study in the register tests the two laws together.

**Espen's position.** Espen chose both because discussing pay is a right whatever its average wage effect. The evidence does not contradict that: it measures a price, about 2% on average for some workers, not a harm to the right.

**What C means in the bill.**
- *Posting:* employers of 15 or more post a good-faith pay range in every job posting (memo 6 A).
- *Discussion:* every worker the wage law covers may ask about, discuss and disclose their own pay.
  - No retaliation, and no pay-secrecy clauses in contracts or handbooks.
  - Enforced with memo 6 part 1's retaliation remedy and private right of action.
- *Who gains:* the labor act already protects this for most private-sector workers (memo 6). The new right reaches the workers that act leaves out, such as supervisors and managers, farm workers and domestic workers.
- *One exception*, as such laws usually allow: staff whose job gives them access to other people's pay records may be barred from disclosing *others'* pay, though never their own.
- *Cost:* zero to the federal budget.

## c. Memo 8 part 1: do completion bonuses work? (`DEC-2026-11-17-11`)

**Finishing matters.**
- About 42% of registered apprentices finish within a year of schedule (GAO, 2025) [EV-WRK-350].
- Completers' estimated career earnings gain is about $240,000, against about $99,000 for all participants (Mathematica for the Labor Department, matched comparison) [EV-SEC-144].

**Bonuses: thin evidence.**
- *Canada's Apprenticeship Completion Grant*, a cash payment to apprentices on certification within up to $4,000 of combined grants, is the nearest test. The government's 2024 evaluation found recipients progressed and certified at higher rates than similar non-recipients. It said the gap may reflect other factors, such as motivation, so the gap cannot be credited to the grant (Employment and Social Development Canada, 2024). Take-up of the completion grant fell from 85.9% to 73.3% between 2011 and 2018, and Canada ended the grants in March 2025 (ESDC, 2019; Newfoundland and Labrador).
- *What the evidence points to instead:* support while training. In Oregon's highway construction program, non-financial support, supplies and child care were each linked to higher completion (peer-reviewed, observational) [EV-SEC-643].

**Recommendation.**
- C already pays its grants per apprentice started **and per completion**. That is a completion bonus paid to the program sponsor, which controls the training. Keep it, and make it a real share of the grant (judgment: about a third, paid at completion).
- Do not add a separate cash bonus to apprentices.
- Spend the same money on supports: child care, tools, transport and mentors. Memo 8 already names mentors as the fix if completion falls.
- Test a worker-side bonus in a randomized pilot during implementation, using the completion data programs already report. The cost stays inside C's range (about +$0.04-0.07 trillion over FY2030-39).

## d. Memo 8 part 2: what "displaced" means, administration, and the AI question (`DEC-2026-11-17-12`)

**How memo 8 B defines "displaced".**
- B says: "any worker displaced from a job held three years or more, whatever the cause". That follows the Labor Department's displaced-worker survey, which counts people who lost a job held three years or more [EV-WRK-315].
- The memo does not define it further. That is section 4's job.

**A working definition for section 4:**
1. *Separated against their will* for a reason other than misconduct: a layoff, a closure or an abolished position. This is the same separation finding a state already makes on every unemployment claim.
2. *At least three years with that employer*, read from the state's quarterly wage records.
3. *Reemployed at a lower hourly wage*, with prior pay up to twice the median.

The cause is never asked, so no one must prove trade or AI caused the loss (the legacy AI-4A principle) [EV-WRK-711].

**Administration: Espen's doubt is fair on one point.**
- State unemployment offices already hold the separation finding and the wage records, so tests 1 and 2 use existing machinery (`DEC-2026-11-13-08`).
- The hard part is **hourly pay**. Most states' wage records hold quarterly earnings, not hours. So "half the hourly gap, for the hours worked" needs the hourly rate at both jobs from employers or pay stubs.
- TAA's version also needed documentation, and only 11% of TAA participants collected it [EV-WRK-332].
- Three ways to do it:
  - *(i) hourly, as B says:* employers report hours and rate at the new job each quarter. The most accurate, and the most paperwork.
  - *(ii) on quarterly earnings:* pay half the drop in quarterly earnings, with a minimum-hours test. Automatic, but it also pays when someone works fewer hours.
  - *(iii) hours added to every state's wage records*, through memo 7's data agreement. Slower, but it also gives the plan its best measure of displacement.
- Recommended: (i) at the start, with (iii) built alongside so it moves to automatic payment within a few years.

**Is wage insurance a patch? Partly, yes.**
- Wage insurance is the best-tested help for one problem: a long-tenured worker who takes a lower-paid job. It raised employment 8-17 points and paid for itself for older trade-displaced workers (Hyman, Kovak and Leive, regression discontinuity) [EV-WRK-365]. Keep it as a base, whatever the cause of job loss.
- It is not an AI plan:
  - So far the evidence shows no broad AI displacement [EV-WRK-357] [EV-WRK-358].
  - The early signal is at **entry level**: workers aged 22-25 in exposed occupations are 19% below trend [EV-WRK-355].
  - Young entrants have no three-year job to lose, so B misses them.
  - The plan also has nothing that measures AI's effect, and nothing that scales help up if displacement grows.

**What a strategic plan would have, and what already exists.**

| Piece | In the plan already | Missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measure it and warn early | Point 4's 90-day notice (decided) | A list of exposed occupations and displacement reporting (AI-4D, an option in `agenda/ai`) |
| Income when work stops | Unemployment insurance under memo 7 B (decided): wider reach, automatic Extended Benefits | — |
| Income when new work pays less | Memo 8 B (provisional); clean power's 90% guarantee for its workers (`DEC-2026-11-13-06`) | — |
| A way into new work | Memo 8 C apprenticeships (decided); LAB-10's paid on-ramps (`DEC-2026-11-12-06`); memo 9 service | An entry-level route: AI-4E's hiring credit (option); AI-4F's job guarantee, which memo 9 proposed not to take |
| A voice in how technology arrives | Memo 3 D bargaining; memo 5 boards | Technology change named as a subject of bargaining or of boards' standards |
| Scale up if it grows | Extended Benefits' automatic trigger (memo 7), for recessions only | A standby trigger: when measured displacement in an occupation or region crosses a threshold, training slots, wage insurance's length or a placement offer expand automatically |
| Share the gains | — | Financing and competition, for stage G and the AI topic |

**Next step: options and a recommendation.**
- *(i)* A short memo in work's section 4 now on measurement (AI-4D) and a standby displacement trigger. Both ride the same labor bill and unemployment data.
- *(ii)* The whole AI-and-work strategy (AI-4A to 4F) as its own topic in a later stage. `agenda/ai` has no dossier yet.
- *(iii)* Both. **Recommended (iii):** keep memo 8 B as the general base, put measurement and the trigger to Espen in work's section 4, and ask the lead to name the AI topic as a later-stage board step.

## e. Memo 9: a target between C and A (`DEC-2026-11-17-13`)

**What the evidence says about blending.**
- Service's evidence is civic: members chose public-service careers more often, with no measured earnings result (Abt Associates, matched comparison) [EV-WRK-391].
- Earnings gains come from training built with employers and from apprenticeship (Katz and others; Mathematica) [EV-SEC-143] [EV-SEC-144].
- The nearest tested blend of service and training, YouthBuild, gives a mixed result. A national randomized trial of about 4,000 young people raised education and survey-reported earnings, but not employment in employer records, and its benefits did not exceed its costs within four years (MDRC for the Labor Department, 2018).
- Pre-apprenticeship did widen who enters the trades in Oregon [EV-SEC-643].

**So, argued both ways.**
- *For:* a bridge year can carry young people into memo 8's apprenticeships, especially women and rural youth.
- *Against:* turning service into a jobs program weakens its civic purpose, which is the evidence's one finding. Memo 9 D's own warning is "a jobs program with a flag on it". If the aim is earnings, a dollar does more in memo 8 directly.

**Recommendation: about 200,000 positions by 2034, the extra 50,000 gated.**
- *150,000 civic service positions,* as C.
- *Up to 50,000 service-to-apprenticeship positions:* a service year in construction, energy retrofits, disaster recovery or care, run with memo 8's registered programs as pre-apprenticeship, ending in an apprenticeship offer. They count toward memo 8's 600,000 target only when the person starts an apprenticeship.
- *The gate:* the second tranche opens once the agency places the first 150,000 well. That is memo 9's own trigger for growing toward A. Where places are oversubscribed, they are assigned by lottery so the track can be evaluated.
- *Subsidized internships and work placements* go through LAB-10's paid on-ramps (decided), not service.

**Cost (judgment):**
- C's 150,000 cost about $5 billion a year at full scale (memo 9, on AmeriCorps' cost rules) [EV-WRK-398] [EV-WRK-399].
- The bridge track adds about $1.8-2.3 billion a year: 50,000 positions at about $35,000-45,000 each, which is service's about $32,600 plus training.
- So about **+$0.04-0.065 trillion over FY2030-39** with the ramp (the tranche from about 2033), and **about +$7-8.5 billion in FY2040**.
- That compares with C's +$0.03-0.05 trillion and A's legacy $0.12 trillion [EV-WRK-705].

## f. Point 2: the care wage floor and federal wage law (`DEC-2026-11-17-15`)

**Yes, the care floor is higher.**
- The care dossier's floor (`DEC-2026-11-11-04`, care memo 14) is $20 an hour in 2026 dollars, indexed to average wage growth from 2027. It applies in publicly paid home care and nursing homes, and is never below a higher state or local minimum.
- At 3.5% a year that is about **$23 in 2030 and about $27 in 2035** (project arithmetic).
- Memo 1 B's floor is $9.50 in 2030 and $17 in 2035 [EV-WRK-042].

**Point 2 still matters,** because the care floor is a pay rate in public programs, not coverage by wage law:
- *Overtime:* the care floor sets an hourly rate but not time-and-a-half after 40 hours. Aides who work long weeks across clients get overtime only from federal wage law. The 1975 exemptions the Labor Department has proposed to restore remove it, along with the minimum wage, for agency-employed aides [EV-CAR-616] [EV-WRK-225].
- *Hours-worked rules,* such as pay for travel between clients, come from wage law too.
- *Privately paid care* is outside the care floor (care memo 14).
- *A worker's own right to sue* for back pay comes from wage law. The care floor is enforced through payers' rates and payroll checks.
- *Timing:* the care floor starts in Medicaid from 2030 and in the single plan from 2035.

**Recommended final wording:** "Home care workers employed by agencies and other third parties are covered by the federal minimum wage and overtime law. The labor bill writes the 2013 rule's coverage into the Fair Labor Standards Act: the companionship and live-in exemptions do not apply to third-party employers, whoever pays for the care. The care wage floor ($20 in 2026 dollars, indexed) is a higher rate for publicly paid care that sits on top of this coverage and does not replace it."

## g. Point 3: is $1,128 a week the right overtime threshold? (`DEC-2026-11-17-16`)

**Where it came from.**
- $1,128 a week ($58,656 a year) is the 2024 rule's level. It was set at the 35th percentile of weekly earnings of full-time salaried workers in the lowest-wage Census region, and was due in January 2025 [EV-WRK-221].
- A court vacated the rule in November 2024 [EV-WRK-222], and the Labor Department rescinded it in May 2026 [EV-WRK-224].
- Today's $684 dates from 2019 and has not risen since [EV-WRK-223].
- Other levels on the record: the 2016 rule's $913 a week (Quach, working paper), which never took effect; and the 2024 rule's $1,128.

**The evidence.**
- The newest version of Quach's study (NBER working paper, 2026) uses payroll records and state threshold changes from 2014 to 2021. It finds that raising the threshold raised earnings with **no loss of jobs**. Many employers raised salaries above the threshold to keep workers exempt.
- An earlier version, on the 2016 rule that never took effect, found employers raising pay to $913 anyway, and about 10 of every 100 affected jobs moved from salaried to hourly. A 2021 draft had found some loss of jobs, so the author's own estimate moved.
- One study, not yet peer reviewed. It supports a raise at about this level; it does not pick between $1,128 and something higher.

**Recommendation: yes, with the method in statute rather than the 2024 number.**
- A fixed $1,128 in a 2029 law would bite less than intended: wages will have grown about five years since it was set.
- Write the 2024 method into the statute: the 35th percentile of full-time salaried weekly earnings in the lowest-wage region, computed from the latest data at enactment, updated every three years, and never below $1,128.
- On 3-4% wage growth that is about **$1,310-1,370 a week in 2030** (project arithmetic).
- A statute also answers the court's objection, that salary rather than duties became decisive, because Congress may set the test itself.
- Federal cost: none.

## h. Point 10: does dropping weekly contact counts save administrative time? (`DEC-2026-11-17-23`)

**Time saved, unmeasured.**
- No source in the register or the search counts the staff hours states spend checking job contacts.
- The hours exist:
  - contacts are logged, sampled, checked with employers and turned into denials and overpayment findings;
  - in Maryland, more than half of the agency's checks of claimants' contacts went unverified by the employer (Maryland Department of Labor, 2024);
  - GAO found that states that verify contacts and warn early have lower work-search overpayment rates (GAO).
- So point 10 removes a verification and adjudication task, and the overpayments it generates, which count in state improper-payment rates.
- But point 10 also **adds** an offered reemployment session, which takes staff time. States already run these sessions with federal reemployment money.
- On balance, any saving is probably small: the time shifts from checking to helping (judgment).

**What it costs in longer claims.**
- Reemployment assessments shortened claims by about a week, and less than half of that came from the help itself [EV-WRK-380] [EV-WRK-381].
- Washington's 1986-87 randomized trial is the nearest test of dropping the work test altogether (Lachowska, Meral and Woodbury, *Labour Economics*, 2016):
  - claimants with the usual work test drew about three fewer weeks and $429 less in benefits in the first year than claimants with none;
  - long-run employment and earnings were no different.
- Point 10 keeps attestation, registration and the offered session, so the effect should fall between those two results.
- Nevada's trial is the caution: some claimants pushed off benefits went onto disability insurance [EV-WRK-382].

**Recommendation.**
- Keep point 10 as decided.
- Treat the administrative saving as something to study in implementation: compare staff time and claim length in the first states, from the claim and quality-control data states already report. No new reporting is needed.
