# Memo 5: Sectoral standards boards

Role: decision memo for Espen on industry standards boards (agenda LAB-3, with its option LAB-3B, multi-employer sectoral bargaining), answering section 2's question 6. Status: **proposed**, for Espen's decision. The legacy design (federal boards for home care and fast food) is a working choice, not Espen's (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`; `legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`, LAB-3). The care wage floor is decided and not reopened here (`DEC-2026-11-11-04`, HLT-10). Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **B, a standing federal framework for binding industry standards boards: the Labor Secretary convenes a board for a low-wage industry on workers' petition or on her own finding, with worker, employer and public members; its wage, benefit and scheduling floors take effect by rule unless the Secretary rejects them in 60 days; the first board, for fast food and limited-service restaurants, is named in the statute and must issue its first standard within 18 months. Home care stays with the care dossier's payer floor, and a board's recommendations for publicly paid care go to the payer's rate setting.** Cost: about **$1-3 billion over FY2030-39 and about $0.2-0.4 billion in FY2040** for the boards' staff and data (judgment). Evidence: cross-country associations (OECD); U.S. boards' process record (they bind only with legal power and a funding route); New Zealand's repeal and California's stalled council as failures to avoid.

## The question

Single-workplace bargaining cannot reach industries of small, high-turnover worksites, such as fast food, home care and domestic work (`domain-labor.md`, LAB-3). Boards that set floors for a whole industry are one answer; sectoral bargaining (LAB-3B), where unions bargain for a sector, is a bigger one. In January 2029 California's Fast Food Council loses its powers [EV-WRK-151]; it had not met as a full body in nearly two years (KCRA news, 2026) [EV-WRK-152].

## Why it matters

- **Coverage.** U.S. bargaining coverage is 11.1% of employees, with no legal mechanism to extend agreements (OECD/AIAS database) [EV-WRK-104].
- **For a household.** A fast-food cook at a franchise of a national chain has no union and a floor set for every industry alike. A board could set a higher floor, a schedule rule and paid sick days for every restaurant of that kind at once.

## What the evidence says, in four lines

1. **Abroad, coordinated and sectoral bargaining go with higher employment and narrower pay gaps,** and with lower productivity growth where coverage is centralised and high (OECD, 2019, cross-country association) [EV-WRK-173]. Australia sets minimums in 121 industry awards, paid to about 20.7% of employees (Fair Work Commission, 2025) [EV-WRK-183].
2. **At home, boards bind only where a law gives them power and a funding route.** Minnesota's nursing-home board set binding minimums from September 2026 [EV-CAR-618]; New York's farm board's recommendations were adopted [EV-WRK-182]; Nevada's and Colorado's home care boards advised, and their legislatures set less (Colorado's board asked for $25 and got $17) (Economic Policy Institute's inventory, 2026, labor-aligned) [EV-WRK-178].
3. **Durability is part of what works.** New Zealand's Fair Pay Agreements Act was repealed a year after it began, with no agreement finalised (Institute for Government) [EV-WRK-175]; California's council stalled [EV-WRK-152].
4. **No evaluation of a U.S. board's effects on wages or jobs** was found; New York's 2015 fast-food board set $15 for chains of 30 or more [EV-WRK-186], unevaluated.

## Options

**A. The legacy LAB-3: federal boards for home care and fast food,** setting floors above the federal minimum, subject to the Labor Secretary's approval.
- *What changed since:* the care dossier decided a $20 floor (2026 dollars, indexed) for publicly paid home care and nursing homes, set through the payer (`DEC-2026-11-11-04`). A separate home care board would set a second floor for the same workers, which the payer must fund anyway.

**B. A standing framework, first board for fast food, home care through the payer (recommended).**
- *What it does:* the Secretary convenes a board on a petition from workers in an industry (Detroit's ordinance uses a 150-worker petition) [EV-WRK-181] or her own finding of low pay; nine members, three each for workers, employers and the public; floors for wages, benefits, scheduling and training, binding by rule unless rejected in 60 days, reviewed every three years. The statute names the fast-food board and sets an 18-month deadline for its first standard, so a standard exists before an election can undo it.
- *Home care:* no separate board; a care board may recommend pay above the $20 floor, and the care payer decides it in its rates (HLT-10).
- *Cost:* about $0.1-0.3 billion a year for staff, wage surveys and hearings (judgment): $1-3 billion over FY2030-39.
- *Burden:* employers in a covered industry follow one more published floor; enforced by the Wage and Hour Division with the general floor.

**C. Sectoral bargaining (LAB-3B):** unions certified for a whole sector bargain with employer associations; the Secretary extends agreements to the sector, with representativeness and hardship tests (the OECD's conditions) [EV-WRK-174].
- *What it buys:* the deepest change, closest to coordinated systems abroad [EV-WRK-173].
- *What it costs:* no U.S. statutory precedent since the 1930s codes; New Zealand's experience shows how long a first agreement takes [EV-WRK-175].

**D. No federal boards: fund state boards.** Grants of about $0.05 billion a year to states that set binding boards, and the federal floor and care floor for everyone else.
- *What it costs:* no help in states that will not act, which overlap the 20 at $7.25 [EV-WRK-013].

## Recommendation: B

- **A standing framework with binding power and a deadline** answers the two failures on the record: boards that only advise [EV-WRK-178], and systems undone before they produce anything [EV-WRK-175].
- **Home care is already covered** by a decided floor that runs through the payer, the route that works in Minnesota [EV-CAR-618]; a second floor would add a step without new money.
- **Sectoral bargaining (C) stays an option** for a later Congress, once boards show what floors by industry do here.

**What would change this:** evidence from the first board that floors by industry cut jobs (narrow its scope); unions in a sector able to show majority support across it (C).
