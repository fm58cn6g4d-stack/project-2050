**Decision line.** Recommended: **C. End the overtime exemptions on the Fairness for Farm Workers Act's phase-in (overtime after 55 hours in the first year, down to 40 in the fourth, small farms three years later), end the live-in domestic exemption, and bring both groups inside the labor act, with domestic workers in private homes reached through a national domestic work standards board rather than workplace-by-workplace bargaining.** Cost to the federal budget: about **zero to slightly positive** (higher wages raise payroll and income tax; judgment, no score exists); the cost falls on farm and household employers. Evidence: statute text; official counts and wages; state laws in California and New York; one unpublished study of California's farm overtime law, which found hours and weekly earnings down. No CBO score of either federal bill was found.

## The question

Two exclusions date from the 1930s and still stand in January 2029's law:
- **The labor act** defines "employee" to exclude "any individual employed as an agricultural laborer, or in the domestic service of any family or person at his home" (29 U.S.C. 152(3)) [EV-WRK-480]. So farmworkers and household workers have no federal right to organize, and the decided card check, penalties and first-contract arbitration (memo 3 D) do not reach them.
- **Wage law** exempts agricultural employees from overtime (29 U.S.C. 213(b)(12)) [EV-WRK-481], exempts small farms' workers from the minimum wage and overtime (213(a)(6), farms using no more than 500 man-days a quarter) [EV-WRK-482], and exempts live-in domestic workers from overtime (213(b)(21)) [EV-WRK-483].

Section 4 left both "a gap for later work, not a decision". The floor and the end of the youth wage reach both groups; the pay-discussion right (LAB-7.09) reaches both; and **the heat standard reaches farmworkers only** (LAB-6.01; the #448 review). Domestic workers in private homes are outside the OSH Act's inspection in practice, so no option here gives them the heat standard; that would be a separate question.

## Why it matters

- **Who.** About 1.2 million hired farmworkers in 2024 (BLS, via CRS) [EV-WRK-487], 1.18 million farm wage and salary jobs (USDA's Economic Research Service) [EV-WRK-484], plus about 363,000 H-2A visa workers certified in FY2024 [EV-WRK-487]. About 580,000 people work for private households (BLS, 2025; 88.8% women) [EV-WRK-493]; the domestic workers' bill counts about 2.2 million domestic employees in private homes, a broader count with no stated method [EV-WRK-492].
- **Pay.** Nonsupervisory farmworkers earned $18.12 an hour in 2024, about 60% of the nonfarm figure [EV-WRK-485]. About 42% of hired crop workers had no work authorization in 2020-22 [EV-WRK-486], so immigration's firewall and deferred action matter for any right to organize (IMM-4.11, 4.17).
- **For a household.** A strawberry picker working 55-hour weeks in the season gets straight time for every hour; under C, by the fourth year, 15 of those hours pay time and a half. A nanny living in the employer's home gets no overtime today, whatever the hours; under C she does.

## What the evidence says, in four lines

1. **States have done both.** California's agricultural labor board has protected farmworkers' collective action since 1975 [EV-WRK-494]. New York's 2019 law gave farmworkers the right to organize and bargain and phased in overtime, after 52 hours from January 1, 2026 [EV-WRK-495], toward 40 by 2032 on its wage board's schedule [EV-WRK-182]. Washington and California also require farm overtime, and New York pairs it with a tax credit for farmers (CRS) [EV-WRK-488].
2. **Overtime may cut hours as much as it raises pay.** One study of California's law, presented in 2025 and not yet peer-reviewed, finds crop workers' hours down about 4 a week and weekly earnings down about $65, with no fall in employment and some shift toward H-2A workers [EV-WRK-496]. It is one study of one state, in slides.
3. **Federal bills exist for overtime, not for organizing.** The Fairness for Farm Workers Act (S. 5304, 2026) phases farm overtime from 55 hours to 40 over four years, small employers three years later, and repeals the small-farm exemption; it does not touch the labor act [EV-WRK-489] [EV-WRK-490]. The Domestic Workers Bill of Rights Act (H.R. 3971, 2025) repeals the live-in exemption [EV-WRK-491]. No federal bill to end the labor act's exclusion was found.
4. **Bargaining one household at a time does not work.** Domestic workers usually have one employer each; Seattle uses a standards board of workers, hiring entities and the public instead [EV-WRK-180], the model memo 5's boards already follow (LAB-3).

## Options

**A. Leave both for later work** (the former recommendation, `review-milestone.md`, M-3).
- *What it does:* nothing beyond the floor, the youth wage's end, the heat standard for farmworkers and the pay-discussion right.
- *Cost:* zero.
- *What it leaves:* about 1.2 million farmworkers and at least half a million household workers outside card check, the penalties and first contracts, and on straight time for long weeks.

**B. Overtime only.**
- *What it does:* repeal 213(b)(12)-(16), the small-farm exemption (keeping the immediate-family exemption) and the live-in exemption, on S. 5304's phase-in: overtime after 55 hours from the first January after enactment, 50, 45, then 40 in the fourth year; employers of 25 or fewer start three years later [EV-WRK-489] [EV-WRK-490] [EV-WRK-491]. The labor act's exclusion stays; organizing stays with the states.
- *Cost:* about zero to the federal budget (judgment). Employers pay a premium on peak-season hours, or cut hours, as California's study suggests [EV-WRK-496].
- *Burden:* farms and households track weekly hours, which wage law already requires for the minimum wage.

**C. B plus organizing rights (recommended).**
- *What it adds:* strike "agricultural laborer" and "domestic service" from 152(3), so the labor act as decided (card check, penalties in court, first-contract arbitration) reaches farmworkers, farm labor contractors' crews included. For domestic workers in private homes, where bargaining with one household is impractical, the Secretary sets up a **national domestic work standards board** under memo 5's framework (LAB-3), with workers, household employers and agencies, to recommend minimum terms (working choice). Agency-employed domestic workers organize under the act like anyone else.
- *Seasons and retaliation:* a farm election or card check runs on the payroll of the peak-season week (working choice), and the immigration firewall covers retaliation by immigration threat (LAB-7.06; IMM-4.11).
- *Cost:* about zero to slightly positive for the federal budget (judgment). The labor board's added workload (perhaps a few hundred farm units a year; judgment) sits inside CAP-7's NLRB line (LAB-2.05).
- *Burden:* the board and the new units; employers face organizing campaigns as other employers do.

**D. C plus a federal farm overtime credit** on New York's model [EV-WRK-488]: a refundable credit for the overtime premium farms pay in the phase-in years.
- *Cost:* about $0.5-1 billion a year while it runs (judgment: about a third to two-fifths of 1.2 million workers working about eight overtime hours a week for about 20 weeks, at a premium near $9 an hour). Not priced in the tool.
- *What it buys:* softer costs for farms in the transition; *what it costs:* a new claim, and the plan's other transition credit (LAB-5.01) has no evidence that it softens job effects either.

## Recommendation: C

- **The plan's labor design is universal everywhere else.** Leaving out the two groups the 1930s laws left out keeps the plan's largest coverage gap, and the decided card check and penalties are most needed where workers are poorest and least protected.
- **The states show it can work:** California since 1975, New York since 2020 [EV-WRK-494] [EV-WRK-495].
- **A board, not bargaining, for household workers** follows memo 5's decided framework and Seattle's practice [EV-WRK-180].
- **The phase-in** follows the one federal bill and gives farms four years (seven for small ones).
- **What it costs in burden:** hours records farms and households already keep for the minimum wage; a board's hearings; the labor board's farm cases.
- **Against C:** the one study of farm overtime finds hours and weekly pay down, not up [EV-WRK-496]; if later evidence confirms it, the overtime part should slow, not the organizing part. D is held back until that evidence shows a credit is needed.

**What would change this:** peer-reviewed evidence that farm overtime cuts annual earnings (slow the phase-in, or take D's credit); a large shift to H-2A workers, whose wage rules follow the decided floors (immigration, shared rule 8).
