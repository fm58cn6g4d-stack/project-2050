# Memo 3: Organizing rules, remedies and who enforces labor law

Role: decision memo for Espen on the PRO Act core (agenda LAB-1, options LAB-1A to 1C, apart from fair-share fees, which are memo 4) and enforcement after *Slaughter* and *Jarkesy* (LAB-2), answering section 2's question 4. Status: **decided** (`DEC-2026-11-17-04`), **not as recommended**: **D**, B plus card check (a union certified on a majority of signed authorization cards). The text below is the memo as Espen decided it; its recommendation was B. Section 4 carries card check into the design. The legacy designs are working choices, not Espen's (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`; `legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`, LAB-1 and LAB-2). Coverage tests (the ABC test and joint employment) are memo 6, part 2. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **B, the PRO Act core written as statute with enforcement moved to the courts: civil penalties of up to $50,000 per violation ($100,000 for repeat or serious ones) sought in federal district court with a jury available; a private right of action for workers and unions; injunctions under the ordinary "reasonable cause" standard; bargaining orders when an employer's violations taint an election; a ban on captive-audience meetings; first-contract mediation then binding arbitration on the Faster Labor Contracts Act's timetable; and a ban on permanently replacing strikers.** Cost: about **zero over FY2030-39 and in FY2040** to the general fund (CBO: $4 million to run, penalty revenue of tens of millions); the labor board's added staff are counted in the agency rebuild (CAP-7, democratic renewal). Evidence: quasi-experimental Canadian studies (election rules, first-contract arbitration), split evidence on replacement bans, CBO's scores, primary law; nothing measures how penalties change employer behavior.

## The question

The labor law's remedies carry no penalty: the board can order a violator to stop and to reinstate workers with back pay, and its orders need a court of appeals to enforce them (the NLRA; CRS) [EV-WRK-129]. In January 2029 the board's members are removable at will in practice after *Trump v. Slaughter* and two circuit rulings [EV-WRK-136] [EV-WRK-134] [EV-WRK-137], its *Cemex* bargaining-order rule has been rejected by the Sixth Circuit [EV-WRK-138] and its General Counsel has asked it to reverse *Cemex* and its captive-audience rule [EV-WRK-128]. So what the board decides can change with each president. The question is which organizing rules to put in statute, and who enforces them.

## Why it matters

- **Most people approve of unions; few have one.** 71% approve (Gallup, 2026) [EV-WRK-105]; 10.0% of workers are members, 5.9% in the private sector (BLS, 2025) [EV-WRK-100] [EV-WRK-101].
- **Campaigns.** EPI finds employers charged with violating the law in 41.5% of elections in 2016-17 (Economic Policy Institute, labor-aligned) [EV-WRK-121]; the Heritage Foundation reads the board's data as firings in 6% of elections (Heritage, conservative) [EV-WRK-123]. Both are analyses with a stake.
- **First contracts.** A new union waits 463 days on average for a first contract (Bloomberg Law, 2026) [EV-WRK-126]; 43% have none after two years in one study (EPI's review) [EV-WRK-125].
- **For a household.** A warehouse worker fired for organizing today can wait years for back pay, less what she earned meanwhile, and the employer pays no fine. Under B she can sue in federal court, and the employer faces a penalty.

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Election rules move outcomes.** British Columbia's switch from card check to mandatory votes cut certification success 19 points; its return restored it (Riddell, peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental) [EV-WRK-168].
2. **First-contract arbitration works and is rarely used:** first-agreement strikes fell by at least half where Canadian provinces adopted it (Johnson, 2010) [EV-WRK-169]. In the U.S. the mediation service gets about 500 first-contract notices a year (CBO) [EV-WRK-177].
3. **Replacement bans: two views.** A general ban goes with higher wages and longer strikes (Cramton, Gunderson and Tracy) [EV-WRK-170]; banning *permanent* replacement, the PRO Act's rule, shows little effect (Budd, 1996) [EV-WRK-171].
4. **Penalties:** CBO scored the 2019 PRO Act's penalties at about 120 cases a year and $39 million of revenue over ten years (CBO, 2019) [EV-WRK-176]. No study measures their deterrence.
5. **What unions do:** over the century they raise pay and narrow inequality (Farber, Herbst, Kuziemko and Naidu, 2021) [EV-WRK-164]; at the margin of a close election, effects on firms split [EV-WRK-161] [EV-WRK-162] [EV-WRK-163].

## The options

**A. The full PRO Act core, enforced through the board as today, with penalties assessed by the board.** The 2019-2025 bills as written: penalties, bargaining orders, a captive-audience ban, first-contract mediation and arbitration, a permanent-replacement ban, and the coverage changes [EV-WRK-142].
- *Risk:* penalties imposed by an agency for wrongs like common-law ones face *SEC v. Jarkesy*'s jury-trial rule [EV-WRK-131]; employers have raised it against the board, so far without success [EV-WRK-132]. And a board removable at will can slow enforcement.

**B. The core as statute, with enforcement in court (recommended; the legacy LAB-1A's core with LAB-2).**
- *Organizing:* a ban on mandatory captive-audience meetings; bargaining orders on the *Cemex* standard written into the act, so no board or circuit can undo them by reading the law; elections within a set number of days.
- *First contracts:* bargaining within 10 days of a request, mediation after 90 days, binding arbitration by a three-member panel if mediation fails within 30, the Faster Labor Contracts Act's timetable, which passed the House 230 to 193 with 20 Republicans in June 2026 [EV-WRK-145] [EV-WRK-144].
- *Strikes:* no permanent replacement of strikers.
- *Remedies:* civil penalties of up to $50,000 per violation and $100,000 for repeat or serious ones, as in the introduced PRO Act [EV-WRK-142], with personal liability for officers who direct them; sought by the government in federal district court, with a jury available.
- *Private right of action:* a worker or union may sue in district court for damages, injunctions and fees after a short wait for the General Counsel, so the law does not depend on who controls the board. Minnesota's captive-audience law uses one [EV-WRK-185].
- *Injunctions:* 10(j) injunctions under "reasonable cause", reversing *Starbucks v. McKinney*'s four-factor test [EV-WRK-130].
- *Board:* partisan balance and staggered terms, without relying on removal protection (the executive-power file's structure).
- *Cost:* CBO scored the 2023 Senate bill's administration at $4 million over 2023-2028 (CBO, 2023) [EV-WRK-189] and the 2019 penalties at $39 million of revenue (CBO, 2019) [EV-WRK-176]: about zero. The board's caseload is the real cost, counted in CAP-7 (legacy: about $4 billion over FY2030-39 by judgment, to be rebuilt from workload) [EV-WRK-706].
- *Burden:* on employers, no captive meetings and deadlines in bargaining; on workers, nothing new. Courts take cases the board handled alone.

**C. The legacy LAB-1C: remedies and first-contract arbitration only.** Penalties in court and the Faster Labor Contracts Act's timetable; no captive-audience ban, bargaining orders or replacement ban.
- *What it buys:* the two pieces with the broadest support (the arbitration bill's House vote) and the best evidence (Canada's arbitration) [EV-WRK-169].
- *What it costs:* campaigns stay as they are; the board can still drop *Cemex* and captive-audience protection.

**D. B plus majority sign-up (card check):** the board certifies a union when a majority of workers sign cards, as the Employee Free Choice Act proposed in 2009.
- *What it buys:* the instrument with the strongest evidence of effect on organizing [EV-WRK-168].
- *What it costs:* the hardest vote in the bill; the PRO Act's bargaining order already certifies on cards where an employer's violations taint the election.

## Recommendation: B

- **Statute and courts are the durable instruments after *Slaughter*.** A board that changes with each president cannot be the only enforcer; a private right of action and court-imposed penalties keep the law working whoever holds the board.
- **The pieces carried are the ones with evidence or a broad vote:** first-contract arbitration [EV-WRK-169] [EV-WRK-144], bargaining orders as the PRO Act's form of card-based certification [EV-WRK-168], penalties where today there are none [EV-WRK-129].
- **The replacement ban is kept on the PRO Act's narrower terms;** the evidence on it is split [EV-WRK-170] [EV-WRK-171], so this part rests on judgment.
- **What it costs in burden:** none on workers; on employers, deadlines and no captive meetings; on courts, new cases.

**What would change this:** a vote count that cannot carry the campaign rules (C); a later Court ruling that bargaining orders need a jury (move them to district court too).
