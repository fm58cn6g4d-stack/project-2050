# Memo 6: Enforcing wage and safety law, who is covered, and pay transparency

Role: decision memo for Espen on wage theft and misclassification (agenda LAB-7 and its option LAB-7B, the ABC test in wage law), the PRO Act's coverage tests (part of LAB-1) and the pay-transparency floor, answering section 2's questions 7 and 8. Status: **decided** (`DEC-2026-11-17-07` to `-09`, `-35`): part 1 **B** and part 2 **B**, as recommended; part 3 **C, not as recommended**: both posted pay ranges and a statutory right to discuss pay without retaliation. The evidence on the second is stated plainly in `../notes/espen-questions-2026-11-17.md`, part b. The text below is the memo as Espen decided it; its recommendation was part 3 A. The legacy designs (doubled liquidated damages, a Wage and Hour surge, a pay-transparency floor, the ABC test in the labor act with the wage-law version as an option) are working choices, not Espen's (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`; `legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`, LAB-7). The heat standard (LAB-6) is point 1 in `../3-design.md`. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **part 1 B, targeted enforcement: rebuild Wage and Hour investigators to their 1978 number over five years, direct inspections to high-violation industries by statute instead of waiting for complaints, publish violators, treble damages for willful wage theft and a private right of action with fees, and a stronger retaliation remedy that covers immigration threats; part 2 B, the ABC test for the labor act and for unemployment insurance, and a codified economic-realities test for wage law; part 3 A, salary ranges in job postings (Colorado's model) for employers of 15 or more.** Cost: about **zero to the general fund here;** the staff need, about $0.3-0.5 billion a year more by FY2035 (judgment), goes to the agency rebuild (CAP-7, democratic renewal) [EV-WRK-706]. Evidence: a randomized safety study and a regression-discontinuity study of publicity; state panels on treble damages; contested studies of California's ABC test; a quasi-experimental study of posting laws, and a peer-reviewed one showing the other kind of transparency law lowers wages.

## The question

The Wage and Hour Division had 611 investigators in May 2025, against 1,232 in 1978, about one for every 278,000 workers (Economic Policy Institute, 2025) [EV-WRK-207] [EV-WRK-208]. It recovered $259 million in back wages in FY2025 (Labor Department) [EV-WRK-200], against estimates of $13-15 billion a year lost to minimum-wage violations alone (Rutgers and Northwestern researchers; EPI) [EV-WRK-211] [EV-WRK-210]. The remedy is the unpaid wages plus an equal amount [EV-WRK-205]. Federal OSHA could reach each workplace once in 191 years (AFL-CIO's count) [EV-WRK-232]. In 2029 the 2024 independent-contractor rule is likely replaced by the 2021 test [EV-WRK-219]. The question is how to enforce, whom the laws cover, and which transparency law to adopt.

## Why it matters

- **Complaints miss the worst violations;** 43% of low-wage workers who complained faced retaliation in a 2008 three-city survey (National Employment Law Project and partners) [EV-WRK-254] [EV-WRK-253].
- **Misclassification:** 10-30% of audited employers misclassified workers in nine states' unemployment insurance audits (a Labor Department-commissioned study) [EV-WRK-216].
- **For a household.** A drywall installer paid as a "contractor" gets no overtime, no unemployment insurance and no workers' compensation; if his boss shorts his pay, the most he can recover today is double what he is owed, years later.

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Inspections pay for themselves:** randomly inspected California workplaces had 9.4% fewer injuries and no loss of jobs (Levine, Toffel and Johnson, *Science*, randomized) [EV-WRK-249] [EV-WRK-250].
2. **Publicity multiplies them:** one OSHA press release cut violations at nearby peers 73%, as much as 210 inspections (Johnson, *American Economic Review*) [EV-WRK-251]. The two studies share an author.
3. **Treble damages deter** in state panels, where political support and real enforcement exist (Galvin, observational) [EV-WRK-252]. No study of doubling federal damages was found.
4. **The ABC test is contested:** after California's AB 5, self-employment fell 10.5% and employment 4.4% in occupations not exempted, while in the most contractor-heavy quarter of occupations payroll jobs rose 10.2% (Mercatus Center, free-market, working paper; its authors call the estimates not causal) [EV-WRK-259] [EV-WRK-260]. A single sector can vote itself out, as app drivers did with Proposition 22 [EV-WRK-262].
5. **Two kinds of pay transparency:** salary ranges in postings raised wages 1.3-3.6% with no change in employment (Arnold, Quach and Taska, working paper) [EV-WRK-407]; laws protecting the right to discuss pay lowered wages about 2% (Cullen and Pakzad-Hurson, *Econometrica*) [EV-WRK-408].

## Part 1. Enforcement

**A. The legacy LAB-7:** doubled liquidated damages for willful wage theft, a stronger retaliation remedy and a Wage and Hour surge, with staff in CAP-7.
- *Gap:* "doubled" is untested [EV-WRK-252]; the surge is unsized; targeting is not in the law.

**B. Targeted enforcement in statute (recommended).**
- *Staff:* investigators back to about 1,200 by FY2034 (the 1978 level) [EV-WRK-207]; OSHA inspectors grown in step; sized from workload for CAP-7 (judgment: about $0.3-0.5 billion a year more at full strength).
- *Targeting:* a share of inspections set by statute for industries with high violation rates, chosen from data the agencies hold, not complaints [EV-WRK-253]; publication of violators' names and amounts [EV-WRK-251].
- *Damages and penalties:* treble damages for willful violations [EV-WRK-252]; civil penalties for repeated or willful violations raised from $1,100 per violation [EV-WRK-206] and indexed; child-labor penalties raised with them (violations involving 5,272 minors in FY2025) [EV-WRK-203].
- *Private enforcement:* a private right of action with fees, and grants to community organizations for outreach (co-enforcement, unevaluated; section 2, C.2).
- *Retaliation:* reinstatement and damages, with immigration threats counted as retaliation and deferred action for a worker while a case runs, the labor-standards half of immigration's decided E-Verify mandate (`DEC-2026-11-12-07`, memo 5 part 2).
- *Burden:* on employers, the risk of inspection and publicity; on workers, nothing new.

**C. Enforcement mostly by private suit:** treble damages, fees and class actions, with little added staff.
- *What it costs:* workers who fear retaliation do not sue [EV-WRK-254]; arbitration clauses block many suits.

## Part 2. Who is covered

**A. The legacy set:** the ABC test for the labor act (LAB-1) only; the wage law keeps the economic-realities test.

**B. ABC for the labor act and unemployment insurance; a codified economic-realities test for wage law (recommended).**
- *ABC:* a worker is an employee unless free from control, outside the usual course of the business, and in an independently established trade. For the labor act it decides who may organize; for unemployment insurance it decides who is covered, as the 2025 unemployment insurance bill would require [EV-WRK-394].
- *Wage law:* the 2024 rule's economic-realities test put in statute [EV-WRK-217], so no rescission [EV-WRK-219] can undo it.
- *Joint employment:* reserved or indirect control counts, as in the PRO Act.
- *Why:* it fixes the unemployment insurance losses that misclassification causes [EV-WRK-216] and organizing rights, while wage law keeps a test that sorts real contractors case by case, given the contested AB 5 evidence [EV-WRK-259].

**C. The legacy LAB-7B: the ABC test everywhere,** wage law included.
- *What it buys:* one test across all laws; reaches app-based work directly.
- *What it costs:* the AB 5 dispute [EV-WRK-259] [EV-WRK-260]; the strongest opposition of any coverage choice.

**D. Codify the economic-realities test everywhere** (no ABC test).

## Part 3. Pay transparency

**A. Salary ranges in job postings (recommended):** employers of 15 or more post a good-faith pay range, the model of Colorado and the states that followed [EV-WRK-407].
**B. A right to discuss pay,** protected from retaliation. It lowered average wages in the one strong study [EV-WRK-408].
**C. Both.** Discussion is already protected for most private-sector workers under the labor act's concerted-activity rule (no register entry), so C adds little to A.

## Recommendation: part 1 B, part 2 B, part 3 A

- **Enforcement that goes looking** is what the best studies support [EV-WRK-249] [EV-WRK-251] [EV-WRK-253]; it costs staff, not rules for workers.
- **Coverage follows the purpose of each law:** the ABC test where misclassification shifts costs onto public insurance and organizing, a codified test elsewhere while the AB 5 evidence is disputed.
- **Posting ranges is the transparency law that raised pay** [EV-WRK-407]; the other kind lowered it [EV-WRK-408].

**What would change this:** an independent causal study of AB 5 showing payroll gains without job loss (extend ABC to wage law, option C); evidence that posting ranges compresses pay at the top without raising the bottom (review in year three).
