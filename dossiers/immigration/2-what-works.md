# A new consensus on immigration: what works

Role: section 2 of the immigration dossier. It covers the research, the programs at home and abroad, and the failures to avoid, behind I3's design choices. It runs part by part, as in section 1:
- A. status for long-settled residents, with the fiscal and labor-market evidence;
- B. legal immigration;
- C. asylum, adjudication and the courts;
- D. detention, its alternatives and workplace verification;
- E. citizenship and integration;
- F. delivery.

The bills are compared, each against the legacy design and the January 2029 law, in part G.

Status: draft (stage E, board row I2). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`). Each claim that may carry a recommendation names its evidence strength (randomized trial, quasi-experimental, observational, program evaluation, official estimate, simulation, international practice or judgment) and where it was tried. Analysis from the second Trump administration is presented as its view and carries no recommendation on its own (`DEC-2026-10-30-01`). No design choice is made here; that is I3's job. The design lens applies throughout: each test, document, deadline and verification step must earn its place against its burden (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`).

## In brief

- **Legalization raises the earnings of the people legalized and helps their children, on decades of evidence.**
  - IRCA's legalization raised wages about 6% (peer-reviewed, observational) [EV-IMM-232] and cut crime 3 to 5% (quasi-experimental) [EV-IMM-234].
  - It did not change long-run unauthorized inflows from Mexico (quasi-experimental, contested framing) [EV-IMM-233].
  - DACA moved 50,000 to 75,000 people into work [EV-IMM-235] and reduced anxiety and adjustment diagnoses among citizen children of eligible mothers (quasi-experimental) [EV-IMM-236].
  - Spain's 2005 regularization raised payroll tax by about €4,189 per person legalized, with no magnet effect (quasi-experimental) [EV-IMM-455] [EV-IMM-456].
- **The fiscal sign depends on what travels with legalization.**
  - S. 744 (2013), which bundled legalization with large new legal and guest-worker flows, cut deficits by $158 billion to $197 billion in its first decade on CBO's estimates [EV-IMM-200] [EV-IMM-205]. On-budget deficits still rose [EV-IMM-208].
  - CBO scored the narrow 2021 bills (Dreamers and TPS holders; farmworkers) as small deficit increases [EV-IMM-213] [EV-IMM-215].
  - **The legacy plan's "$175B saving" is CBO's preliminary figure for the amended S. 744 (June 24, 2013), superseded by $158 billion as passed** [EV-IMM-207] [EV-IMM-205] (`legacy-research.md`, finding 1). It scores a bill much larger than IMM-1, so neither the size nor the sign of IMM-1's score can be assumed.
- **Wages and budgets.** The National Academies find immigration's effect on natives' wages "very small" over ten years and its long-run federal fiscal effect positive. Its state and local effect is negative, and the second generation is among the strongest contributors [EV-IMM-222] [EV-IMM-223] [EV-IMM-224].
- **The alternative costs more.** Mass deportation over ten years would add about $862 billion to primary deficits and cut GDP 3.3% by 2034 (Penn Wharton, simulation) [EV-IMM-225].
- **Counsel changes outcomes and appearance.**
  - With a lawyer, the odds of obtaining relief are five and a half times higher (observational, 1.2 million cases) [EV-IMM-314].
  - 96% of represented non-detained people attended all hearings [EV-IMM-315].
  - New York's public defender program projected a 48% success rate against 4% before it (program evaluation) [EV-IMM-327].
- **Case management works at a fraction of detention's cost, on selected groups.** The Family Case Management Program cost $38.47 a family a day against $237.60 for family detention, with about 99% hearing compliance (program data, an advocacy evaluation and CRS) [EV-IMM-355] [EV-IMM-356]. GAO warns that ICE's own appearance and absconding figures leave groups out [EV-IMM-353] [EV-IMM-354].
- **E-Verify's record is weak where it matters most.** It wrongly confirmed about half of unauthorized workers, mainly through identity fraud (Westat, program evaluation) [EV-IMM-393]. CBO scored a mandate as a $39 billion revenue loss as work moves off the books [EV-IMM-395]. State mandates cut unauthorized populations, with contested effects on other workers [EV-IMM-399] [EV-IMM-401] [EV-IMM-402].
- **Fees deter citizenship.** A fee voucher doubled naturalization applications among low-income immigrants (randomized) [EV-IMM-425], while DHS proposes nearly doubling the fee and ending waivers [EV-IMM-423].
- **Abroad.**
  - Canada runs high, mostly economic immigration through an independent refugee tribunal and private sponsorship [EV-IMM-451] [EV-IMM-466].
  - Spain's 2026 regularization drew 1,174,978 applications [EV-IMM-454].
  - The EU's new asylum procedure caps border decisions at 12 weeks [EV-IMM-458].
  - Australia's offshore detention cost about A$5.6 million a person in 2024-25 (advocacy, citing government figures) [EV-IMM-462].

## Part A. Status for long-settled residents

### A.1 IRCA (1986) and the legalizations since

- **What it did.** IRCA legalized people who entered before January 1, 1982 [EV-IMM-536]. It also moved the registry date to January 1, 1972 [EV-IMM-591]. Its size is cited at "almost 3 million" (Baker) [EV-IMM-234]. The official count of the general and agricultural programs is not yet read at its source (Evidence still needed).
- **Wages.**
  - In a sample of legalized men, being unauthorized carried a wage penalty of 14% to 24%, and legalization raised wages about 6% [EV-IMM-232].
  - *Strength:* peer-reviewed, observational (fixed effects); one cohort, mostly Mexican men, in a 1990 labor market.
- **Crime.**
  - Legalization reduced crime by 3 to 5%, mostly property crime, about 120,000 to 180,000 fewer crimes a year, largely through better job prospects [EV-IMM-234].
  - *Strength:* peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental (variation in legalized share across counties).
- **Later flows.**
  - Border apprehensions fell right after passage, rose back during the application period, and the amnesty did not change long-run unauthorized immigration from Mexico [EV-IMM-233].
  - *Strength:* peer-reviewed time series. The authors' sample cannot separate legalization from the employer sanctions passed with it. Read it as "no detectable magnet effect", not proof of none.
- **Employer sanctions.**
  - GAO found in 1990 that about 10% of employers began one or more discriminatory practices because of IRCA's sanctions, and 65% reported compliance [EV-IMM-237].
  - *Strength:* official survey (GAO), 1990. This is the original warning on workplace enforcement (part D.3).
- **The later, narrower laws.** NACARA (1997) gave permanent residence to Nicaraguans and Cubans here by December 1, 1995 [EV-IMM-537]. Section 245(i) let people adjust status for a large fee [EV-IMM-538]. Both were targeted at groups, with no general route.
- **What has not happened since 1986.** The registry date has not moved since 1986 [EV-IMM-591]. The registry grants permanent residence for long residence, good moral character and no listed bars [EV-IMM-590]. It is the one standing route in law that works by date rather than by application program: moving the date is a one-line change. Whether it is the right form is I3's question (fork 2).

### A.2 DACA

- **Work.**
  - DACA raised the chance of working, through higher participation and lower unemployment, and moved 50,000 to 75,000 people into jobs [EV-IMM-235].
  - *Strength:* peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental (difference-in-differences on eligibility).
- **Children.**
  - Mothers' DACA eligibility reduced adjustment and anxiety disorder diagnoses among their U.S.-citizen children, in Oregon Medicaid records [EV-IMM-236].
  - *Strength:* peer-reviewed, quasi-experimental (a regression discontinuity on the birth-date cutoff); one state.
- **The lesson for design.** Temporary, executive status brought most of the work gains. An appeals court has since held the program unlawful, leaving current recipients protected only while the courts act [EV-IMM-027]. Durability is part of what works.

### A.3 Abroad: regularization

- **Spain, 2005.**
  - The regularization covered about 600,000 working-age immigrants and raised payroll tax by €4,189 per legalized immigrant at the province level [EV-IMM-455]. It found no magnet effect, comparing non-EU with EU immigrants [EV-IMM-456].
  - *Strength:* quasi-experimental, a working paper later peer-reviewed.
  - Spain tied status to a job contract, the opposite of the design lens on documentation. The evaluation does not say how much the contract test cost in reach.
- **Spain, 2026.**
  - An extraordinary regularization, open April 16 to June 30, drew 1,174,978 applications. 79.6% applied on grounds of strong ties and 20.4% as protection applicants [EV-IMM-454].
  - *Strength:* official count (through a fetch extract; wording to be confirmed). Outcomes are not yet known.
  - It shows a large regularization can be run in one window by a national administration.

**What the table means for I3.**
- **The 2013 savings came mostly from added workers, not from legalization.** S. 744 added about 10 million people by 2023 [EV-IMM-203]; S. 2611's guest-worker program alone added 2.7 million [EV-IMM-212]. The legacy design has no guest-worker expansion on that scale (`domain-immigration.md:57`).
- **The narrower bills are the closer guide.** Their scores are small net costs within ten years [EV-IMM-213] [EV-IMM-215]. The direct spending (benefits for newly eligible people) arrives before the revenue grows.
- **The sign turns on the design.** What IMM-1 costs depends on three choices:
  - the benefit bars (part G of section 1, the five-year bar);
  - the legal-immigration changes travelling with it (IMM-2);
  - the window: the fiscal gain grows in the second decade [EV-IMM-200] [EV-IMM-201].
- **The fiscal rule.** On the project's fiscal rule, this is reported, not gated (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`). Choosing a legalization that costs more early because it includes people in the floor is not a defect.
- **The scores are CBO's alone.** They are official estimates with no independent second score in the register. The National Academies and ITEP support the direction (more revenue when status raises earnings) but not the size.
- **Wage effects on natives are small** (consensus review) [EV-IMM-223]. This is the best-supported finding in the part, and it holds across the restrictionist and expansionist literatures the Academies reviewed.

The Senate parliamentarian ruled against three attempts to put legalization in the 2021 reconciliation bill, the first a path to citizenship for 8 million people (NPR) [EV-IMM-238]. The rulings are not published; this is a news account, and it supports the legacy reading that legalization needs the regular floor (`domain-immigration.md:59`). ## Part B. Legal immigration

- **Lost numbers are real, and capacity causes them.** About 62,000 employment green cards were lost in FY2021 because USCIS lacked staff to use numbers moved over from family [EV-IMM-123]. *Strength:* official data (CRS). Recapture works only with adjudication capacity behind it.
- **The caps were set in 1990.** The employment limit was set in 1990 [EV-IMM-120]. The per-country limit makes a single country's queue decades long [EV-IMM-121] [EV-IMM-128] [EV-IMM-129]. NFAP's 179-year projection is a no-change extrapolation, an advocacy group's arithmetic and not a forecast [EV-IMM-126].
- **The 2024 border bill would have raised the numbers.** It would have raised family preference numbers from 226,000 to 258,000 and employment numbers from 140,000 to 158,000 from FY2025 [EV-IMM-535]. This was the first bipartisan Senate text since 2013 to raise legal immigration.
- **Workforce visas.** The Farm Workforce Modernization Act's certified worker status is the only recent bill-scored route for a sector (part G) [EV-IMM-216]. The register holds no evaluation of a care-worker visa. The care workforce's needs are in section 1, part B.3 and care's point 8.
- **Canada.**
  - Canada runs a planned, mostly economic intake. Its current plan holds permanent residents at 380,000 a year, 64% economic [EV-IMM-451], after cutting the target from 500,000 [EV-IMM-450]. It also set a goal of temporary residents at 5% of the population [EV-IMM-452].
  - *Strength:* official plans (through a fetch extract).
  - *Lesson:* levels set in advance, by category, with political room to adjust. The register holds no causal evaluation of the points system.
- **Australia.**
  - A 2023 government review found its migration program "not fit for purpose". It counted more than 1.8 million temporary migrants able to work and warned against a "permanently temporary" underclass [EV-IMM-467] [EV-IMM-468].
  - *Strength:* official review.
  - *Lesson:* temporary work status without a route to permanence builds a class of long-term residents with fewer rights, which is the U.S. TPS problem in another form.

## Part C. Asylum, adjudication and the courts

### C.1 Who decides: asylum officers and judges

- **The 2022 asylum-officer rule.**
  - It lets asylum officers decide the claims of people who pass a credible-fear screening, in a non-adversarial interview within 45 days [EV-IMM-300] [EV-IMM-301].
  - DHS estimated in 2022 that full use would need about 800 new staff and $180 million for about 75,000 cases a year [EV-IMM-302]. It began with a few hundred referrals a month [EV-IMM-303].
  - *Strength:* the rule is law; its cost is an agency projection (Biden administration, checked as usual). **Its results (grant rates, timelines, appearance) are not yet in the register** (Evidence still needed). Advocates' and MPI's summaries could not be read at source.
- **What asylum officers grant.**
  - Officers granted 40% of the cases they decided in 2022, down from 44% in FY2016 (Human Rights First) [EV-IMM-321].
  - Of affirmative cases officers referred to court, judges granted 76% in FY2022-23 [EV-IMM-322] [EV-IMM-312].
  - *Strength:* an advocacy group's tabulation of official data, with TRAC's matching figure. Selection (who appeals) means this is not a measure of officer error, but it shows that a referral to court is often a second full hearing, not a review.
- **Triage instead of decision.** The July 2026 rule lets USCIS send affirmative cases to court without an interview [EV-IMM-305]. DHS's own analysis says it will raise court referrals at first [EV-IMM-308], could reach up to 444,724 pending applicants [EV-IMM-307], and puts the affirmative wait at over 7.3 years if nothing changes [EV-IMM-319]. That is the administration's analysis, and its direction runs opposite to the legacy design (officers decide, courts review).
- **Timelines as policy.** MPI's 2018 report called for managing asylum resources to meet the statute's six-month standard [EV-IMM-318]. The EU's new procedure caps the border procedure at 12 weeks (16 if the person is transferred) [EV-IMM-458]. It applies only where a nationality's EU-wide recognition rate is 20% or lower [EV-IMM-459], and it applies from June 12, 2026 [EV-IMM-457]. *Strength:* law; its results are not yet known.
- **The UK cut its backlog fast.** Initial decisions pending fell 56% in a year, to about 40,000 at the end of June 2026, and hotel use fell by half [EV-IMM-461]. The grant rate fell from 48% to 38% [EV-IMM-460]. *Strength:* official statistics (through a fetch extract). Speed came with a lower grant rate; the cause is not separated.
- **Canada's tribunal.** Canada decides refugee claims in an independent tribunal, which had about 298,000 claims pending and an 18-month average wait in February 2026 [EV-IMM-453]. Independence alone does not prevent a backlog.

- **Read together.** Counsel is strongly associated with relief and with appearance, across two national studies and one program evaluation. None is randomized, and people with stronger cases find lawyers more easily, so the true effect is smaller than the raw gap. Still, the 2019 orientation study is the only contrary finding, from an administration then working to end the program, and it measures detention days, not outcomes. It is presented beside the others.
- **Children.** Counsel for children and for people with serious mental illness is the legacy court design's addition (`legacy-research.md`, section 3). The 2026 court bill does not appoint counsel [EV-IMM-114].
- **Fast dockets without counsel produce removal orders, not decisions on the merits.** In the first 449 cases completed on the Los Angeles dedicated docket, 99.1% ended in removal orders, 72.4% in absentia [EV-IMM-317]. In absentia orders were 69% of removal orders in FY2023 [EV-IMM-310]. *Strength:* early observational data (UCLA) and official counts.

### C.3 How many judges, and where they sit

- **More judges alone do not clear the backlog.** CRS projected that 300 more judges would not clear the backlog through FY2033 [EV-IMM-112]. That is an estimate on FY2023 inflows. New receipts have since fallen by more than two-thirds [EV-IMM-102], so the need on today's inflow is smaller and the projection overstates it.
- **Independence.** The legacy court design (CRT-5A) and the 2026 bill put the courts outside the executive [EV-IMM-113]. No country or U.S. precedent in the register measures independence against outcomes. The case for it rests on the 2025-26 firings (section 1, part C.3), on Canada's model and on judgment. CRT-5's design stays with democratic renewal.

### C.4 Refugees

- **The legacy floor's basis.** Ceilings over the decade to 2016 ranged from 70,000 to 85,000, then rose to 110,000 [EV-IMM-463]. So a 50,000 floor sits below every ceiling of that decade. The 1980-2005 range is not yet read at source.
- **Admissions fall below ceilings.** Admissions fell to 27,131 in FY2002 after the September 11 attacks [EV-IMM-464] and ran at 56,424 and 58,238 in FY2011-12, below ceilings of 80,000 and 76,000 [EV-IMM-465]. A floor in statute binds only if it binds admissions, not just the ceiling.
- **Private sponsorship.** Canada admitted 110,147 privately sponsored and 88,838 government-assisted refugees in 2016-22. Government-assisted refugees were less satisfied with their first housing [EV-IMM-466]. *Strength:* official evaluation (through a fetch extract); no earnings comparison read.
- **Offshore processing.** Australia spent about A$560 million in 2024-25 on offshore processing in Nauru for about 100 people [EV-IMM-462]. *Strength:* an advocacy group citing government estimates. It is the costliest model in the register, and the reason "regional processing" (IMM-3) has to be defined as access to a claim, not detention abroad.

  - *Cost:* alternatives cost a few dollars a day against $137 to $238 for detention. This finding is strong (official data, three sources).
  - *Appearance:* high appearance under case management is shown for people enrolled in full-service programs, measured mid-case. It is not shown for everyone released, and ICE's own figures overstate it. This finding is moderate.
  - *Design implication:* case management, not electronic monitoring alone, carries the evidence. That supports alternatives as the default only with case management and counsel, which is the pairing the legacy design and the counsel evidence (part C.2) point to.
- **A recent pilot is not yet in the register.** The 2023-24 Case Management Pilot Program (FEMA and DHS's civil rights office) has not been read (Evidence still needed).
- **The budget's direction.** ICE's FY2027 request funds 41,500 beds with $3.7 billion in custody operations [EV-IMM-362]. That is on top of the reconciliation money running to September 2029. It is the administration's request, not a measure of need.

### D.2 What detention costs in lives

Thirty-seven deaths in FY2026 made it the deadliest year on record (news tallies from ICE notices) [EV-IMM-179] [EV-IMM-360]. The register has no DHS Inspector General inspection reports yet (Evidence still needed). Enforceable standards (IMM-4) have no tested U.S. model in the register.

- **Read together.**
  - E-Verify works on the people least likely to need it: authorized workers pass, at small error rates that fall hardest on lawful noncitizens [EV-IMM-391].
  - It fails on the people it targets, because a borrowed identity passes [EV-IMM-393].
  - Mandates push some people out of a state and some work off the books: the population [EV-IMM-399] [EV-IMM-400] and the revenue [EV-IMM-395]. The effect on native workers is contested between two peer-reviewed studies [EV-IMM-401] [EV-IMM-402].
- **The burden.** Under the design lens, a mandatory check on every hire is a verification step whose burden falls on every worker and employer, and hardest on lawful noncitizens. Its benefit (fewer unauthorized hires) is real but partial. After a legalization, the population it would catch is smaller and those left are the least protected. This bears on fork 9: the legacy pairing was a political trade, and the evidence on its policy value is mixed.
- **Its older lesson.** IRCA's employer sanctions led about one employer in ten to discriminate [EV-IMM-237].

## Part E. Citizenship and integration

- **Integration happens, over generations.**
  - Children of immigrants meet or exceed the schooling of third-generation natives, though second-generation Mexicans and Central Americans do not reach parity [EV-IMM-429].
  - Immigrant men work at 86%, the second generation at 83%; immigrant women at 61% against 72% for native-born women [EV-IMM-430].
  - Foreign-born young men are incarcerated at a quarter of natives' rate [EV-IMM-431], and immigrants learn English as fast as or faster than earlier waves [EV-IMM-476].
  - *Strength:* consensus review (National Academies, 2015), read through its release.
- **Fees deter citizenship, and a voucher overcomes it.**
  - A fee voucher doubled naturalization applications among low-income immigrants in New York; nudges did not raise applications among those eligible for a waiver [EV-IMM-425].
  - *Strength:* randomized field experiment (peer-reviewed, one state).
  - It is the strongest evidence in the section, and it bears on the legacy waiver below 200% of poverty and on the June 2026 proposal to end waivers [EV-IMM-423].
- **Eligible people do not apply.** About 9 million permanent residents were eligible to naturalize in January 2023 [EV-IMM-420], while 823,702 applied in FY2023 [EV-IMM-428]. Processing was 5.5 months at the median in early FY2025 [EV-IMM-426]. The 2024 fee rule's half-price fee covered households at 150% to 400% of poverty [EV-IMM-424].
- **Integration services.** The register has no evaluation of credential recognition, English classes or state Offices of New Americans (Evidence still needed). The legacy Office of New Americans (IMM-6) rests on judgment.

## Part F. Delivery

- **A fee-funded agency cannot staff ahead of demand.** USCIS lost 62,000 green cards to staff shortages in one year [EV-IMM-123]. It is about 96% fee-funded [EV-IMM-145]. Its backlog doubled in clearance time in a year of falling receipts (section 1, part F). Legalization means millions of applications in one window: Spain's 2026 window drew over 1.1 million in eleven weeks [EV-IMM-454], and CBO counted more than 300,000 workers and 200,000 dependents for the farmworker bill alone [EV-IMM-216].
- **The design lens favors evidence the government already holds.** The register has no evaluation of records-based proof of residence. Its case rests on the design lens and on other dossiers' delivery evidence (`dossiers/secure-floor/2-what-works.md`, part B.1). I3 should name it as judgment.
- **Durability.** DACA (part A.2), the 2022 asylum rule (part C.1) and every 2025-26 executive change show that executive design is reversed by the next administration. Statute, and an agency funded to carry it out, is what lasts.

### G.3 What the comparison shows

1. **No enacted or passed bill matches the legacy tiers.** The closest are the two House-passed 2021 bills (tier A only) and S. 744 (tiers A and B, with much more). The U.S. Citizenship Act is the only one to reach everyone present.
2. **Cost to the applicant is a design choice the bills split on.**
   - The U.S. Citizenship Act charges cost recovery and lets fees be capped or waived [EV-IMM-505].
   - S. 744 and the farmworker bill add a $1,000 penalty.
   - The Dignity Act charges $7,000 and 1% of income for status without citizenship.
   - Under the design lens, the penalty and the levy are burdens to justify, and the fee evidence (part E) says they cut take-up.
3. **Enforcement pairing varies.** It runs from none (2021) to triggers (2013) to mandatory E-Verify (Dignity Act). Part D.3's evidence weakens E-Verify's case on the merits; the case for the pairing is political.
4. **Citizenship is the dividing line.** The Dignity Act's 2025 version is the only one with no route to a green card, and its 2023 version had one [EV-IMM-517]. The movement on the bipartisan side has been away from citizenship.

## Failures to avoid

- **Status that never ends.** TPS holders of two decades [EV-IMM-020] and Australia's temporary class [EV-IMM-468] show what temporary status becomes without a route to permanence.
- **Fast dockets without counsel.** They yield removal orders in absentia, not decisions [EV-IMM-317] [EV-IMM-310].
- **Executive-only design.** DACA, the 2022 asylum rule and the 2025-26 reversals.
- **A one-time window with no standing route.** Since IRCA, a long-settled population has rebuilt for want of a date that moves (part A.1).
- **Recapture without capacity.** Numbers made available and then lost [EV-IMM-123].
- **Verification that burdens the lawful and misses the rest** [EV-IMM-391] [EV-IMM-393].
- **Penalties and fees that cut take-up** [EV-IMM-425].

## Evidence still needed

- **Asylum-officer results** under the 2022 rule (cases, grants, timelines): USCIS's data, DHS's Inspector General report OIG-24-36, MPI's 2023-24 analyses.
- **IRCA's official counts** (general and agricultural programs) and agricultural-program fraud (DHS statistics office or INS reports), and the share who naturalized.
- **A score of a legalization like the legacy tiers** on today's baseline. None exists; I3 can bound one from the 2021 and 2013 scores, labeled as an estimate.
- **The Case Management Pilot Program's evaluation** (2023-24) and DHS Inspector General inspections of detention.
- **Integration services**: credential recognition, English classes, state Offices of New Americans, and MPI's "brain waste" estimates.
- **Abroad**:
  - Germany's 2023 skilled immigration law;
  - Canada's Express Entry and its refugee tribunal's statute;
  - Australia's offshore costs from an official audit;
  - the OECD's *International Migration Outlook*.
- **E-Verify**: its current statutory authorization (CRS's 2018 text is stale [EV-IMM-397]) and current error rates.
- **Refugee ceilings 1980-2005**, to finish checking the legacy floor.
- **Public opinion** on each design element, from probability samples, read at source.
