# A new consensus on immigration: the problem

Role: section 1 of the immigration dossier. It covers what is wrong, for whom and how large, and how the law will stand in January 2029 (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`), for the flagship's six parts (`README.md`, "Scope"):
- A. status for long-settled residents;
- B. legal immigration and the backlogs;
- C. asylum, the border and the courts;
- D. interior enforcement and detention;
- E. citizenship;
- F. delivery.

Part G covers eligibility for other programs (shared rule 3). Status: draft (stage E, board row I2). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`, or another dossier's register where it already holds the fact). Figures from the second Trump administration's agencies are its own data or claims, labeled as such (`DEC-2026-10-30-01`). Claims still missing evidence are listed at the end and are not made here. The legacy record is restated with file and line in `legacy-research.md`; this section corrects it where the sources differ.

Outcomes this section frames (`charter/outcomes.md`):
- OUT-07, a humane, orderly and lawful immigration system with broad public consent (all parts);
- OUT-06, fair pay and real power at work (parts A, B and D);
- OUT-01 and OUT-03, child poverty and care, through eligibility (part G).

## In brief

- **About 14 million people live in the United States without status, and most have been here a long time.**
  - Estimates for 2023-24 run from 13.7 million to 15.8 million [EV-IMM-005] [EV-IMM-001] [EV-IMM-002].
  - 45% had lived here 20 years or more in 2023 [EV-IMM-005]. 5.3 million U.S.-citizen children live with an unauthorized parent [EV-IMM-007].
  - Whether the number fell in 2025-26, and by how much, is contested. Estimates of departures run from 790,000 to 1.9 million (MPI), against a DHS claim of more than 3 million [EV-IMM-012].
- **The temporary protections have mostly been ended.** TPS covered about 1.4 million people in January 2025 and about 274,000 now [EV-IMM-022]. The Supreme Court held that courts cannot hear non-constitutional challenges to a termination [EV-IMM-023]. DACA covers 436,350 people, under a court ruling that it is unlawful [EV-IMM-016] [EV-IMM-027].
- **Every queue is long.**
  - The immigration courts hold 3.3 million pending cases [EV-IMM-100].
  - USCIS holds more than 12.8 million, with a net backlog above 7.7 million [EV-IMM-140].
  - Asylum offices hold about 1.5 million affirmative cases [EV-IMM-146].
  - A new Indian applicant in the second employment category faces a wait NFAP puts at 179 years if the law does not change [EV-IMM-126].
- **The border is not the 2029 problem.** Southwest border encounters fell from a record 2,475,669 in FY2023 to 443,671 in FY2025 [EV-IMM-161]. DHS reports 379,243 nationwide in FY2026, preliminary and its own figure [EV-IMM-160]. The 2029 problem is the interior system and the backlogs, as the legacy file said (`legacy-research.md`, section 0).
- **Enforcement is funded outside annual appropriations until September 30, 2029.**
  - Two reconciliation laws carry the money: the 2025 law, with $45 billion for detention and $29.85 billion for ICE [EV-IMM-174], and the June 2026 Secure America Act, with about $70 billion more for ICE, CBP and investigations [EV-IMM-039] [EV-IMM-040].
  - The money lapses nine months into the plan. From November 1, 2028, the law also caps immigration judges at 800 [EV-IMM-105].
- **Detention and deaths are at record levels.** ICE held about 73,000 people in mid-January 2026 [EV-IMM-172] and 65,765 on July 11, 2026, 70.6% of them with no criminal conviction [EV-IMM-171]. Thirty-seven people died in ICE custody in FY2026, the most on record (a news tally) [EV-IMM-179].
- **Birthright citizenship holds, on one vote's margin of reasoning.** *Trump v. Barbara* (June 30, 2026) struck down the executive order on the Fourteenth Amendment for five justices [EV-IMM-029] [EV-IMM-030]. A sixth rested only on the statute, "unless and until" Congress changes it [EV-IMM-031].
- **Status now sets access to the floor.**
  - The 2025 law limits SNAP, Medicaid (from October 2026), Medicare and the premium credits (from 2027) to citizens, permanent residents and two small groups [EV-IMM-046] [EV-IMM-047] [EV-IMM-048] [EV-IMM-049].
  - It bars the child credit unless a parent has a work-eligible Social Security number [EV-IMM-050].

Every one of these is a model estimate (the residual method on Census surveys), not a count, so they are graded as analysis. The 2023 estimates agree within about 0.3 million.

**The 2025-26 trend is contested.**
- Pew finds the population "probably started to decline" in 2025, still above 2023 levels by mid-year [EV-IMM-003]. The Census survey shows the whole immigrant population falling by more than 1 million from January to June 2025 [EV-IMM-004].
- CIS's estimate shows a 2.3 million fall [EV-IMM-011].
- MPI estimates 790,000 to 1.9 million departures of people with at least a year's residence, deportations and self-departures together, against DHS officials' claim of more than 3 million [EV-IMM-012]. MPI notes DHS has not published detailed enforcement data since January 2025 (context of EV-IMM-012).

The administration's figure is its claim. *Working assumption for I3:* a January 2029 population of about 12 million to 14.5 million. The low end applies the larger departure estimates; the high end applies Pew's and MPI's cautious readings. Long-settled residents are the least likely to have left. The answer changes the size of IMM-1's registration and USCIS's workload, not the design choice, so it is a working assumption, not a research task (`DEC-2026-11-10-07`).

**The long-settled core.** The 45% with 20+ years is MPI's figure for 2023 [EV-IMM-005]. No source in the register states the share with 10 or more years, which is the legacy tier B threshold. MPI finds 80% had 5+ years, in the same passage [EV-IMM-005], so the 10-year share lies between 45% and 80%. I3 needs it to size tier B (Evidence still needed).

- **The courts' role is now small.** *Mullin v. Doe* (June 25, 2026, 6-3) held that the TPS statute "bars all" non-constitutional claims against a termination [EV-IMM-023] [EV-IMM-024]. The statute's review bar is the reason [EV-IMM-026]. The legacy file had the date and result right but not the case name.
  - The House passed a Haiti extension in April 2026 (context of EV-IMM-026); no Senate action is recorded.
  - So the status of people with years of TPS is a question only Congress can settle.
- **Parole.** The administration ended parole and work permits for about 500,000 people from Cuba, Haiti, Nicaragua and Venezuela in 2025 (Pew; context of EV-IMM-009). No public source gives the total number of people with parole grants [EV-IMM-017].
- **Fees.** The 2025 law raised the TPS application fee from $50 to $500 and set a parole fee of at least $1,000, both indexed [EV-IMM-044].

### A.3 DACA

- 436,350 people held DACA on June 30, 2026 (CRS, citing USCIS) [EV-IMM-016].
- The Fifth Circuit held DACA unlawful in January 2025. It found the work permits severable, limited the injunction to Texas and left current recipients protected while the district court acts [EV-IMM-027]. The 2026 status of that order is reported only by advocates and needs the district docket (Evidence still needed).
- The 2025 marketplace rule removed DACA recipients from health coverage through the marketplaces [EV-IMM-028].
- The legacy file's counts of pending renewals (125,282) and Texas recipients (82,410) are not confirmed by the sources read and are not used (`legacy-research.md`, finding 4).

### A.4 What it costs to have no route

There is no standing route to status for most of this population.
- The registry provision, which grants permanent residence for long residence, covers only people who entered before January 1, 1972 [EV-IMM-590]. Its date was last moved in 1986 [EV-IMM-591].
- The other routes run through family or employer petitions, whose queues are in part B, or through relief in removal proceedings. The fee for cancellation of removal, the main relief for long residents, rose to $1,640 [EV-IMM-045].

Section 2, part A weighs what legalization does for earnings, children and public budgets.

## Part B. Legal immigration and the backlogs

### B.1 The numbers in the law

- **Employment.** 140,000 green cards a year, set in 1990, replacing 54,000 set in 1965 [EV-IMM-120].
- **Family and the per-country limit.** With 226,000 family preference numbers and 140,000 employment numbers, the 7% per-country ceiling limits a single country to 25,620 a year at first [EV-IMM-121].
- **Diversity.** The diversity lottery is 55,000, reduced to 50,000 by a 1997 law [EV-IMM-122].
- **Concentration.** Five countries made up 48% of employment immigrants in FY2023 [EV-IMM-124].

### B.2 The queues

| Queue | Size or wait | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family preference waiting list | About 3.8 million, as of November 2023 (State, via NFAP) | [EV-IMM-127] |
| Employment backlog, first three preferences | 1,264,495 in December 2025, up 20.6% since 2020 (NFAP estimate) | [EV-IMM-125] |
| EB-2, India, November 2026 | Final action date November 1, 2013 (China October 1, 2021; others April 1, 2025) | [EV-IMM-128] |
| Siblings of citizens (F4), November 2026 | Mexico April 22, 2001; Philippines May 15, 2008; India December 15, 2006 | [EV-IMM-129] |
| Spouses and children of permanent residents (F2A) | Mexico April 22, 2026; others October 22, 2026 | [EV-IMM-130] |
| Projected wait, new Indian EB-2 filer | 179 years if the law does not change (NFAP, a no-change extrapolation) | [EV-IMM-126] |

- **Numbers have been lost to capacity.** 122,000 family numbers went unused in FY2020 and moved to employment, raising FY2021's limit to 262,000. About 62,000 of those went unused and were lost because USCIS lacked staff [EV-IMM-123]. Recapture (IMM-2) is aimed at exactly this.
- **Visa bulletin dates came through a commercial mirror** and should be checked against the State Department's own bulletin (Evidence still needed).

### B.3 The H-1B fee and the workforce visas

- **The $100,000 H-1B charge.**
  - A September 2025 proclamation conditions entry on a $100,000 payment, for 12 months [EV-IMM-055]. A second proclamation extends it to September 21, 2027 [EV-IMM-056].
  - A federal court in Boston struck it down on June 8, 2026 as a tax Congress had not authorized. The administration appealed, and the ruling is reported stayed [EV-IMM-057].
  - **The legacy file's August 2026 proposed DHS rule for a $103,265 fee was not found in any source** and is dropped (`legacy-research.md`, finding 4).
- **In January 2029 the charge is a lapsed proclamation that a court has struck down.** It can be renewed by proclamation and is in litigation. Fees of this size are for Congress to set, as the legacy file said; ledger IF1 is that channel.
- **Care and construction.** Immigrants are 30% of direct care workers [EV-CAR-614] and 34% of construction trades workers [EV-HOU-055]. Enforcement has measurably cut staffing. One study finds the Secure Communities program reduced nursing home staff hours per resident day by about 2% [EV-CAR-635]. No visa category exists for care occupations below the professional level; the care dossier's asks are in the README's seams table.

| FY2026 (preliminary; DHS) | 379,243 nationwide encounters; fewer than 99,000 Border Patrol apprehensions on the southwest border | [EV-IMM-160] |

- **How it fell.**
  - The June 2024 rule restricted asylum when encounters averaged 2,500 a day over seven days [EV-IMM-164].
  - Proclamation 10888 (January 2025) suspended entry for people "engaged in the invasion" [EV-IMM-165] [EV-IMM-053]. The Migrant Protection Protocols returned, and the CBP One app's scheduling ended [EV-IMM-167].
  - On April 24, 2026, the D.C. Circuit held the proclamation and guidance unlawful to the extent they displace the law's removal procedures and the right to apply for asylum; the entry suspension itself was not challenged [EV-IMM-054] [EV-IMM-166]. The government is reported to be seeking Supreme Court review (Evidence still needed).
- **The FY2026 figures are the administration's, preliminary and promoted.** The pre-2025 series is from CRS and a committee staff brief. The CBP series is not yet read at its source (Evidence still needed). Either way, the January 2029 problem is the people already here and the systems that decide their cases.

### C.2 Asylum in the system

- **Asylum offices.** Pending affirmative cases passed 1 million by the end of FY2023 and reached 1,525,933 through most of FY2025, on DHS's count [EV-IMM-146].
  - A July 2026 rule lets USCIS refer affirmative cases to the immigration courts without an interview [EV-IMM-305]. DHS expects referrals to the courts to rise at first [EV-IMM-308], and it projects waits of more than 7.3 years [EV-IMM-319].
  - The 2022 rule letting asylum officers decide the merits of border cases is still on the books, as far as the sources show [EV-IMM-300] [EV-IMM-304].
- **The courts' asylum docket.**
  - 2,424,061 asylum applications were pending at the end of FY2025 [EV-IMM-111].
  - Grants fell to 9.9% of decisions in FY2025 [EV-IMM-111]. Through June 2026, judges recorded 7,231 grants and 89,740 denials [EV-IMM-109].
  - The legacy file's "94% of asylum cases denied" counts only grants against denials, leaving out abandoned, withdrawn and other outcomes (`legacy-research.md`, section 3).
- **The new fees.** An asylum application now costs at least $100 plus $100 for each year it is pending [EV-IMM-043], with $550 more for the first work permit [EV-IMM-045]. They are permanent and indexed, with no sunset found.
- **Refugees.**
  - The FY2026 ceiling was set at 7,500 and raised to 17,500 in May 2026. 7,730 were admitted through June 30, 2026, with the program otherwise suspended [EV-IMM-052]. Nearly all were South African (context of EV-IMM-052).
  - The FY2025 ceiling, set before the change of administration, was 125,000 (context of EV-IMM-052).

- **Judges.** CRS counts 735 at the end of FY2024, 634 at the end of FY2025 and 557 at the end of December 2025 [EV-IMM-104]. EOIR's own table reports 312 hired in FY2026 and 753 on board [EV-IMM-103].
  - The two cannot both describe early 2026 unless hiring was very fast. They are shown side by side and need reconciling (Evidence still needed).
  - The legacy file's "520 judges and 33 assistant chief judges" (February 2026) is from news via an earlier legacy note. It matches neither and is not used.
  - Firings began in February 2025 [EV-IMM-150].
- **The 800-judge cap.** From November 1, 2028, the 2025 law says the immigration courts may have "not more than 800" judges [EV-IMM-105]. It is a ceiling, not funding for 800, and it binds the plan from its first day unless repealed.
- **What it would take.** CRS projected, on FY2023 data, that 300 or more additional judges would only begin to reduce the backlog and would not clear it through FY2033 [EV-IMM-112]. That projection predates the fall in new cases, so it overstates the need on today's inflow.
- **Why completions rose.** Completions rose even as judges fell. CRS cautions that an unknown share are terminations and dismissals that move people into expedited removal (context of EV-IMM-102). Speed here is not the same as fair decisions.
- **The court bill.** The Real Courts, Rule of Law Act (H.R. 7836, March 2026) would create courts under Article I, independent of the executive, with an appellate division of 21 judges and 15-year terms [EV-IMM-113].
  - **As introduced, it does not appoint counsel.** It keeps the existing right to counsel "at no expense to the Government" [EV-IMM-114].
  - The legacy CRT-5's appointed counsel for children and people with serious mental illness is therefore an addition to the bill, not in it (`legacy-research.md`, section 3).

- **The Secure America Act came out of a funding lapse.** The Act was a second reconciliation law. - **ICE is funded until 2029.** MPI says Congress has given DHS $240 billion through FY2029, and that ICE hired 12,000 officers in 2025 [EV-IMM-060]. The Brennan Center reads the 2025 money as funding ICE "until 2029" [EV-IMM-176].

- **What this means for January 2029.** Enforcement money is still available to obligate for the plan's first nine months, then lapses together. From FY2030, detention and ICE's size are appropriations choices, or a third reconciliation law. So the legacy file's conclusion stands, with a sharper date. IMM-4's cap books no savings against CBO's baseline, but a smaller detention system is an annual appropriations choice from October 2029.

  - The register does not yet hold the January 2025 rescission of the protected-places policy; it is named under Evidence still needed.

### D.3 Workplace verification today

About 1.39 million employers use E-Verify, and 22 states require it for some employers (SHRM, citing DHS) [EV-IMM-190]. That is up from 779,722 employers in 2018 [EV-IMM-191]. Its record and effects are in section 2, part D.

## Part E. Citizenship

- **Birthright citizenship.**
  - Executive Order 14160 (January 2025) told agencies not to recognize the citizenship of children whose mother was unlawfully or temporarily present and whose father was neither a citizen nor a permanent resident [EV-IMM-032].
  - In *Trump v. CASA* (June 2025), the Court limited universal injunctions without deciding the merits [EV-IMM-033].
  - In *Trump v. Barbara* (June 30, 2026), the Court held that children born here to parents unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens under the Fourteenth Amendment [EV-IMM-029]. The Chief Justice wrote for five; Justice Kavanaugh concurred only on the statute; three dissented [EV-IMM-030].
  - Kavanaugh's opinion rests on 8 U.S.C. 1401(a) "unless and until" Congress changes it [EV-IMM-031]. Codifying the holding in 1401 (IMM-5) closes that route; a constitutional majority of five is the margin.
- **Naturalization.**
  - About 8.5 million permanent residents were potentially eligible to naturalize in January 2025 [EV-IMM-018]; 818,500 naturalized in FY2024 [EV-IMM-421].
  - The fee is $760 on paper and $710 online [EV-IMM-422]. DHS proposed in June 2026 to raise it to $1,330 and end fee waivers and the reduced fee [EV-IMM-423]; the rule is not final.
  - USCIS completed 19,562 naturalization cases in August 2026, against 78,923 a year earlier (Niskanen, from USCIS data) [EV-IMM-149]. Neighborhood investigations resumed in August 2025 [EV-IMM-427].

## Part F. Delivery: USCIS

- **Workload.**
  - More than 12.8 million cases were pending at the end of June 2026, with a net backlog above 7.7 million (Niskanen, from USCIS data) [EV-IMM-140].
  - Pending cases rose from 9.7 million to 11.3 million over the year to December 2025 while receipts fell 33.6% and completions 40.8%. The average time to clear the backlog doubled, from 9.4 to 18.6 months [EV-IMM-141] [EV-IMM-142].
  - For context, forms received ran 6.9 million to 10.4 million a year over FY2013-23 [EV-IMM-144].
- **Why it slowed.** Processing slowed as applications fell, which points to policy rather than volume:
  - adjudication holds on nationals of 19, then 39, countries [EV-IMM-148], vacated and enjoined by courts in 2026 [EV-IMM-149];
  - staff firings [EV-IMM-150];
  - more vetting.

  The administration attributes the slowdown to vetting. That is its account; the register holds no independent analysis of the cause.
- **Funding.** Fees made up about 96% of USCIS's FY2021 spending authority [EV-IMM-145], and appropriations are typically under 5% of its budget [EV-IMM-144]. A fee-funded agency shrinks when applications fall and cannot staff up ahead of a surge such as IMM-1's registration. That is why the legacy plan funds the hiring surge from the capacity line (`legacy-research.md`, section 2).
- **Asylum offices** are in part C.2.

## Part G. Eligibility for other programs (shared rule 3)

The 2025 law made the following changes:

| Program | Who qualifies under the 2025 law | From | Evidence |

| SNAP | Citizens and nationals, permanent residents, Cuban and Haitian entrants, Compact of Free Association residents | Enactment (July 2025) | [EV-IMM-046] |

- **Who it hits.**
  - Refugees, asylees, TPS holders and other lawfully present immigrants lose access they had.
  - Noncitizens of working age were already the least covered group: 28.9% uninsured in 2025 [EV-CAR-103].
  - About 500,000 children could lose the child credit under the number rule, on a Tax Policy Center estimate relayed by the Bipartisan Policy Center [EV-SEC-016].
- **Executive changes beyond the law.**
