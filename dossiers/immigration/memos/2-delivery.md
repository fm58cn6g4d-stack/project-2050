# Memo 2: How status is delivered

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 2 of the immigration dossier (`../README.md`, "Forks for I3"; `../3-design.md`). This is the delivery-and-form fork the design lens asks for (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`): not how many people get status, but what each person must do, prove and pay to get it. Status: **decided** (`DEC-2026-11-12-07`): **C**, the records-first standing registry, as recommended. Specified in `../4-specification.md` (IMM-1.05 to 1.12, IMM-6.06); priced in `../6-cost.md`. The text below is the memo as Espen decided it. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **C, a records-first standing registry: the registry statute (8 U.S.C. 1259) gets a rolling date of ten years before the day a person applies; residence is shown from records the government already holds (tax filings, Social Security earnings, school, court and DHS records), with a sworn statement and one document where records run out; people USCIS already knows (DACA, TPS and parole holders) start from a notice; biometrics and a criminal check stay; no penalty; the fee is waived below 200% of poverty and capped per family above it; a work permit and protection from removal from the day of filing; no closing window.** Cost: about **$3-6 billion over FY2030-39 (judgment) for the waived fees and the records-matching build, and about $0.2-0.5 billion in FY2040**; USCIS's adjudication capacity is memo 7's. Evidence: official counts of IRCA and DACA; a randomized trial on fees; existing law; no evaluation of records-based proof of residence (judgment).

**This memo decides the form of every status route in memo 1.** It applies to tiers A and B and to registration under any of memo 1's options except C (the Dignity Act model), which carries its own fees.

## The question

Every legalization bill since 1986 has been an application program: a window, a form, a fee, often a penalty, and documents each applicant gathers to prove years of presence. The legacy design follows that model with an 18-month window opening within 180 days of enactment and fees at cost (`legacy/domains/domain-immigration.md`, lines 42-44).

There is another form already in the law. The registry provision grants permanent residence to a person who entered before a date, has lived here continuously since, is of good moral character and is not barred on criminal, security or smuggling grounds [EV-IMM-590] [EV-IMM-623]. Its date has been January 1, 1972 since 1986 [EV-IMM-591]. Moving it is a one-line change. A 2025 Senate bill would make it roll, seven years before each application [EV-IMM-622]; Cato proposes a permanent ten-year version [EV-IMM-612].

So the choice has three parts: an application program or a standing route; documents from each applicant or records the government holds; and what the applicant pays.

## Why it matters

- **The scale is IRCA's or larger.** About three million people applied under IRCA and nearly 2.7 million were approved [EV-IMM-635]. Memo 1's tiers reach about 8-11 million eligible people, with 6-8 million green cards over the decade (judgment). Spain took 1,174,978 applications in eleven weeks in 2026 [EV-IMM-454].
- **Documents are the burden that falls hardest on the people with the longest, most informal lives here.** DACA asked for rent receipts, utility bills and letters to show residence [EV-IMM-639]; about 9 in 10 initial requests accepted were approved (827,119 of 914,640) [EV-IMM-637].
- **The government already holds much of the proof.** Unauthorized workers' payroll taxes exceeded the benefits paid on their earnings by about $12 billion in 2010 (SSA's actuaries) [EV-IMM-606], and they paid $96.7 billion in taxes in 2022 (ITEP, left-leaning) [EV-IMM-228]. Those payments leave filing and earnings records with the IRS and SSA.
- **Fees cut take-up.** A fee voucher doubled naturalization applications among low-income immigrants (randomized) [EV-IMM-425].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Large one-time programs can be run** (official counts): IRCA approved nearly nine in ten of its applicants for temporary residence [EV-IMM-635]; DACA approved 827,119 initial requests [EV-IMM-637].
2. **Fees and penalties are a take-up barrier** (randomized, one state): the fee voucher trial [EV-IMM-425]. No trial isolates a legalization penalty, but the direction is the same.
3. **A window that closes leaves the problem to rebuild** (observation): the population grew again after IRCA's one-time window, and the registry date has not moved since [EV-IMM-591].
4. **Records-based proof of residence has no evaluation in the register** (judgment). Its case rests on the design lens and on other dossiers' delivery evidence: automatic enrollment from records reached children a filing-based design missed (`dossiers/secure-floor/memos/2-delivery.md`).
5. **Vetting stays.** Every option keeps biometrics and a criminal check; the 1259 bars apply in every option [EV-IMM-623].

## Options

**A. An application program (the legacy design and the bills).**
- **What it does.** A USCIS application in an 18-month window; a fee at cost (an adjustment application now costs $1,440 [EV-IMM-627]); in S. 744 and the farmworker bill, a $1,000 penalty [EV-IMM-525] [EV-IMM-522]; back taxes; documents gathered by each applicant to prove presence.
- *For people:* a house cleaner paid in cash for fifteen years hunts for old leases and a pastor's letter, and pays about $2,400 with the penalty.
- *Reach:* high among people with records and money; lower among the poorest and most informal. Late arrivals to the decision miss the window.
- *Cost to government:* fee-funded, so close to zero on the budget; the penalty raises a little.
- *Burden:* the document hunt, the fee and the penalty, all up front.

**B. A rolling registry date, as the 2025 Senate bill writes it.**
- **What it does.** 8 U.S.C. 1259's date becomes a rolling seven years before application [EV-IMM-622]; the ordinary adjustment form, fee and documentary proof apply.
- *What it buys:* a standing route in a law that already exists, with its bars [EV-IMM-623]; no window, no penalty.
- *What it costs:* the same document hunt and fee as A; seven years is shorter than memo 1's ten.

**C. A records-first standing registry (recommended).**
- **What it does.**
  - *The route:* 8 U.S.C. 1259 with a rolling date ten years before the day of application, carrying memo 1's tiers: tier A's groups on their own tests, tier B at ten years, and registration for everyone else present before the cutoff.
  - *Proof of residence:* USCIS checks the years against records the government holds: tax returns filed with an individual taxpayer number, Social Security earnings posted to any number, school enrollment, court and DHS files. Where records run out, a sworn statement and any one document per year cover the gap, with fraud penalties for false statements.
  - *Known people start on a notice:* DACA, TPS and parole holders, whose biometrics and files USCIS already holds, get a notice of what the records show and confirm it.
  - *Vetting:* biometrics and a criminal check for everyone, with 1259's bars (point 2 in section 3 narrows them).
  - *Cost to the applicant:* no penalty; no fee below 200% of poverty, a fee at cost above it, capped per family (the U.S. Citizenship Act lets fees be capped or waived [EV-IMM-505]). Back taxes only as the IRS assesses them on income already earned, as the legacy says (`domain-immigration.md`, line 43).
  - *From the day of filing:* a work permit and protection from removal.
  - *No window:* the route stays open.
- *Where tried:* the registry itself, since 1929; records-based enrollment in the plan's other programs. Records-based proof of residence is untested at this scale (judgment).
- *For people:* the house cleaner's fifteen years of tax filings under her taxpayer number show her residence. She confirms her address, gives fingerprints and is approved, without a fee.
- *Cost:* about $3-6 billion over FY2030-39 for the waived fees and the IRS-SSA-USCIS matching build (judgment: roughly half of 8-10 million filings below 200% of poverty, at a per-case cost between DACA's $495 in fees [EV-IMM-638] and an adjustment's $1,440 [EV-IMM-627]); about $0.2-0.5 billion in FY2040, as people reach ten years each year.
- *Political risk:* data sharing between the IRS and USCIS raises fears the data will be used for enforcement. The answer is memo 6's firewall: what a person gives or what records show for status is used only for status, as IRCA's own confidentiality rule did.
- *Burden:* fingerprints, one confirmation and gaps filled by a statement. What it buys: reach to the people with the least paper.

## The smaller choices inside the form

| Choice | Recommended | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Criminal bars | 1259's grounds, with no bar for immigration-only offenses (unlawful entry and reentry) and a waiver for old or minor convictions | A bar should screen for danger, not for how a person crossed (point 2) |
| Work permit | From the day of filing | Months of waiting without work is the cost of a slow agency, not a test (point 3) |
| Penalty | None | The bills' $1,000 penalties are a take-up barrier [EV-IMM-425]; the public interest is in registration, not in the payment |
| Back taxes | Only as assessed | A full reconstruction would be a bar for people paid in cash (legacy) |
| Window | None; a standing route | The rolling date is the window (memo 1) |

## Consequences at a glance

| | A. Application program | B. Rolling registry, ordinary form | **C. Records-first registry** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What a long resident does | Gathers years of papers, pays fee and penalty | Gathers papers, pays fee | Confirms what records show, gives fingerprints |
| Who is missed | The poorest and most informal; late deciders | The poorest and most informal | People with no records and no document, covered by the sworn-statement route |
| After the window | No route | Standing route | Standing route |
| Cost to government FY2030-39 | About zero (fee-funded) | About zero | About $3-6 billion |
| Main risk | Low take-up; a population that rebuilds | Low take-up below the poverty line | Fraud in the gap-filling statement; data-use fears |

## Recommendation: C

- **It reaches the people with the least paper,** whose long, informal lives the government's own tax and earnings records already show [EV-IMM-606] [EV-IMM-228].
- **It is a standing route in an existing law,** so the problem does not rebuild after a window closes [EV-IMM-591].
- **It drops the charges that cut take-up** [EV-IMM-425], while keeping the vetting every option shares [EV-IMM-623].
- **It follows the design lens** (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`): records the government already holds, self-attestation where they run out, and correction by monitoring (a published fraud audit with a review trigger) rather than proof up front.
- **What it costs in burden:** fingerprints and one confirmation. What it buys: take-up close to the eligible population at about $3-6 billion over the decade.

**What would change this:** a fraud audit showing the sworn-statement route abused at scale (require two documents per gap year); a court or law barring IRS-USCIS matching (fall back to B with fee waivers); a vote count that needs a penalty (a flat amount, waived below 200% of poverty).
