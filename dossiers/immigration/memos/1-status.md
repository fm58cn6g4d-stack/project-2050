# Memo 1: Who gets status, and on what path

Role: decision memo for Espen, forks 1 and 3 of the immigration dossier (`../README.md`, "Forks for I3"; `../3-design.md`). Status: draft for decision (board row I3, question 0p); nothing here is decided. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions, with their basis in `../3-design.md`, "What the options cost" and "IMM-1's score, re-derived".

**Decision line.** Recommended: **A, the legacy tiers (IMM-1A), with three changes: tier B's ten years are counted to the day a person applies, so people now short of ten years qualify when they reach it (memo 2's rolling registry date); everyone present before the cutoff can register now for a work permit and protection from removal while they wait; and tier A's work-history route covers care and construction as well as farm work. Part 2: a green card on approval for tiers A and B, citizenship on the normal clock (three years for tier A). Part 3: anyone who has held TPS, DACA or humanitarian parole is in tier A, and time in any temporary status counts toward the ten years.** Cost: about **+$0.05 trillion over FY2030-39 on the general fund (range −$0.05 to +$0.15 trillion) and about +$10 billion in FY2040 (range −$5 to +$20 billion)**, before memo 6's eligibility choices; Social Security's funds gain separately. Evidence: quasi-experimental and observational studies of IRCA and DACA; CBO's scores of the 2013 and 2021 bills; SSA's actuaries on S. 744.

**This memo decides who is eligible for permanent status and how long the path is.** Memo 2 decides how a person proves it and what they pay; memo 6 decides which programs the new statuses open.

## The question

About 14 million people lived here without status in 2023 [EV-IMM-002] [EV-IMM-005]. The working assumption for January 2029 is 12 million to 14.5 million, after contested 2025-26 departures (section 1, part A.1; [EV-IMM-012]). They are long settled: 45% had lived here 20 years or more in 2023 [EV-IMM-005], and about 60% ten years or more in 2021 (MPI) [EV-IMM-617]. There is no standing route: the registry date is January 1, 1972 [EV-IMM-590].

The temporary protections have mostly been ended. TPS covered about 1.4 million people in January 2025 and about 274,000 now [EV-IMM-022], and courts cannot review terminations [EV-IMM-023]. DACA covers 436,350 people under a ruling that it is unlawful [EV-IMM-016] [EV-IMM-027]. Parole and work permits for about 500,000 people were ended in 2025 (section 1, part A.2).

The legacy design has three tiers (`legacy/domains/domain-immigration.md`, lines 32-55): A, Dreamers, TPS holders and farmworkers, a green card on approval; B, residents of ten years or more with clean records, five years of provisional status, then a green card; C, everyone else, left to a later bill. It is a working choice, not Espen's decision (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`). Legacy D1 lists IMM-1 among the bills that must pass in the first trifecta; that is a modeling stipulation, not a design.

## Why it matters

- **5.3 million citizen children live with an unauthorized parent** (MPI) [EV-IMM-007].
- **About 9.6 million are in the labor force** [EV-IMM-008], 5.6% of the workforce [EV-IMM-010].
- **Deportation reaches long residents.** 71% of people deported after two or more years here had never been convicted (MPI) [EV-IMM-014]. Mass deportation over ten years would add about $862 billion to primary deficits (Penn Wharton, simulation) [EV-IMM-225].
- **Temporary status that never ends is the failure to avoid**: two decades of TPS [EV-IMM-020] and Australia's "permanently temporary" class [EV-IMM-468].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Status raises earnings** (peer-reviewed, observational): IRCA's legalization raised wages about 6% [EV-IMM-232]; DACA moved 50,000 to 75,000 people into work (quasi-experimental) [EV-IMM-235].
2. **It helps children and communities** (quasi-experimental): fewer anxiety diagnoses among citizen children of DACA-eligible mothers [EV-IMM-236]; crime down 3 to 5% after IRCA [EV-IMM-234].
3. **No detectable magnet** (quasi-experimental, contested framing): IRCA did not change long-run unauthorized inflows from Mexico [EV-IMM-233]; Spain's 2005 regularization showed none [EV-IMM-456].
4. **The budget sign turns on the design** (official estimates): S. 744, with large new flows, cut deficits $158-197 billion in its first decade [EV-IMM-200] [EV-IMM-205]; the narrow 2021 bills raised them slightly [EV-IMM-213] [EV-IMM-215]. Social Security gains either way: S. 744 would have added $284 billion to its reserves by 2024 (SSA's actuaries) [EV-IMM-604].
5. **Durability is part of what works.** DACA brought most of the work gains and is now held unlawful [EV-IMM-027]; a green card is not revocable by a later Congress.

## Part 1. Who is eligible

**A. The legacy tiers, IMM-1A (recommended, with three changes).**
- **As written.** Tier A: Dreamers (arrived as children, with a schooling, work or service test), TPS holders and farmworkers with a work history. Tier B: ten years' continuous presence before the cutoff, clean record. Tier C: later.
- **The three changes.**
  1. *Ten years counted to the day of application, not to a fixed past date.* Tier B becomes a standing route: a person with eight years in 2029 qualifies in 2031. This is memo 2's rolling registry date. It is Cato's proposal [EV-IMM-612] and the logic of the 1929 registry law, whose date has not moved since 1986 [EV-IMM-591].
  2. *Registration now for everyone present before the cutoff.* People short of ten years, and not in tier A, register, are checked, and get a renewable work permit and protection from removal until they reach ten years. That answers tier C without a second bill and without a cliff.
  3. *Tier A's work-history route covers care and construction as well as farm work*, on the farmworker bill's model (1,035 hours or 180 days over two years [EV-IMM-522]). This is care's first ask (`DEC-2026-11-11-04`, point 8) and housing's workforce condition (shared rule 8).
- *Who it reaches* (working assumption, judgment): tier B about 7-9 million at enactment, on MPI's 60% with ten years in 2021 [EV-IMM-617] moved to 2029; tier A outside tier B about 1.5-3 million; registered and waiting about 2-4 million. With take-up of about 70%, the figure Penn Wharton took from CBO's work on S. 744 [EV-IMM-609], about 6-8 million green cards over the decade, close to the nearly 8 million SSA's actuaries expected for S. 744 [EV-IMM-603].
- *For people:* a cook in Houston who arrived in 2012 applies in 2030 and gets a green card on approval. His neighbor, who arrived in 2022, registers, works legally, and applies in 2032.
- *Political risk:* the rolling date is open to the charge of "permanent amnesty". The answer is the ten-year wait, the criminal bars (memo 2), and that the registry has been law since 1929.

**B. Tier A only (IMM-1B).**
- *What it buys:* the likeliest version to find Republican votes; the Dream and Promise Act passed the House 228-197 [EV-IMM-530].
- *What it costs:* it leaves most of the long-settled population out, including most of the 45% here 20 years or more.
- *Budget:* the H.R. 6 pattern, about +$0.04-0.06 trillion over FY2030-39 (project arithmetic on [EV-IMM-213], grown).

**C. Status without citizenship (IMM-1C, the 2025 Dignity Act model).**
- *What it does:* a seven-year renewable status, no green card except through military service, $7,000 in total, a 1% income levy and no federal means-tested benefits [EV-IMM-511] [EV-IMM-512] [EV-IMM-513].
- *What it costs:* it builds the permanent non-citizen class Australia's review warns against [EV-IMM-468]; the payments cut take-up, as fees cut naturalization [EV-IMM-425].
- *Use:* only as a fallback in a Senate without the rules change.

**D. Everyone present before a cutoff (IMM-1D, the U.S. Citizenship Act).**
- *What it does:* six years' status, a green card after five once taxes are settled, citizenship three years later for those with prior status [EV-IMM-500] [EV-IMM-506].
- *What it buys:* the most complete reach at once.
- *What it costs:* the hardest vote, and still a one-time cutoff: the population rebuilds afterwards for want of a date that moves, as after IRCA (section 2, part A.1).

## Part 2. The time to a green card and to citizenship

| Path | Tier A | Tier B | Registered, under ten years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Recommended** | Green card on approval; citizenship after three years | Green card on approval; citizenship on the five-year clock | Work permit now; green card at ten years; then the five-year clock |
| Legacy | Green card on approval; three years | Five years provisional, then a green card; then five years | Not covered |
| U.S. Citizenship Act | Green card on approval; three years | Five years, then a green card; then three years | Covered on the same path |

- *Why a green card on approval for tier B:* the legacy's five-year provisional stage is a second adjudication for 7-9 million people, which roughly doubles USCIS's work (memo 7), and the 1996 law already holds new permanent residents out of most means-tested programs for five years unless memo 6 changes it. A person with ten years here has already waited.
- *The cutoff date for registration:* the bill's introduction date in 2029, so the bill does not draw arrivals during debate.

## Part 3. TPS, DACA and parole holders in 2029 (fork 3)

- **Recommended: anyone who has held TPS, DACA or humanitarian parole at any time is in tier A**, if present before the cutoff. By 2029 most TPS and parole grants will have been terminated [EV-IMM-022]; a "current holder" test would reward the timing of a termination, not the person's tie to the country. USCIS already holds their biometrics and files, so memo 2's records-first form starts them on a notice, not an application.
- **Temporary statuses lead to permanence.** TPS stays as an emergency tool, but time in TPS, deferred action or the new registered status counts toward tier B's ten years, so no temporary status becomes permanent by default.
- **DACA's court case** ends with status in statute; the work permits the Fifth Circuit found severable [EV-IMM-027] continue until each holder is approved.

## Recommendation: A, with the three changes

- **It reaches the long-settled population now and everyone else over time,** without a second bill or a cliff, which is the lesson of the years since IRCA.
- **It keeps the legacy's political logic** (tiers, with the longest-settled first) while ending the "tier C later" gap that has no vehicle.
- **It answers care's and housing's workforce asks** (shared rule 8) through tier A's work route.
- **Budget:** a small general-fund cost in the first decade on the 2021 bills' pattern, falling as earnings rise, with Social Security's funds gaining (`../3-design.md`, "IMM-1's score, re-derived"). Reported, not gated (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`).
- **What it costs in burden:** one registration and one check (memo 2). What it buys: legal work and a green card for the people the plan's other programs already count as residents.

**What would change this:** a vote count that cannot carry the rolling date (fall back to a fixed date ten years before enactment); evidence that the registration draws new unauthorized arrivals (shorten registration's cutoff, keep the ten-year line).
