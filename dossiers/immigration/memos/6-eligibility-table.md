**Decision line.** Recommended: **C, residence for the plan's new universal programs and status for the older means-tested ones: every resident, whatever their status, in the single health plan, the long-term care benefit, child care and pre-K, shown by records or attestation; the child benefit by the child's own ID (secure floor memo 1, part 2 B, taken here as the working assumption while question 0o is open); the pre-2025 law restored for SNAP, SSI, TANF and Medicaid, with memo 1's new statuses counted as qualified and no five-year wait; the new statuses carry work-eligible Social Security numbers from registration; housing's owner programs, its emergency fund and the property-tax match carry no new status test; one data firewall for all of it.** Cost: about **$0.05-0.11 trillion over FY2030-39 and about $8-15 billion in FY2040 (judgment)** beyond what other dossiers already priced, mostly health coverage for people without status from the single plan's first wave (2031) and the ended five-year wait. Evidence: the 2025 law and the 1996 law; Census data on coverage; no score of the table (judgment).

**This memo decides the table every other dossier cites,** and answers the open items they left here.

## The question

Today the same family gets different answers from different programs. The 2025 law limits SNAP, Medicaid, Medicare and the premium credits to citizens, permanent residents and two small groups [EV-IMM-046] [EV-IMM-047] [EV-IMM-048] [EV-IMM-049], and bars the child credit unless a parent holds a work-eligible Social Security number [EV-IMM-050]. Housing uses Section 214's list with proration for mixed-status families (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`). Care proposed every resident in the single plan and every resident child in child care, leaving people without status to this flagship (`DEC-2026-11-10-09` point 3; `DEC-2026-11-11-03`). The legacy bars memo 1's tier B from means-tested federal benefits for five years, on the S. 744 model (`domain-immigration.md`, line 57), a working choice.

Memo 1 also creates new statuses: tier A and B permanent residents and the registered status for people waiting to reach ten years. Unless the law lists them, they fall outside every program's rules.

## Why it matters

- **Noncitizens of working age are the least insured group:** 28.9% uninsured in 2025 [EV-CAR-103].
- **About 500,000 children lose the child credit** under the parent number rule (Tax Policy Center, via the Bipartisan Policy Center) [EV-SEC-016].
- **5.3 million citizen children live with an unauthorized parent** [EV-IMM-007]: a rule that tests the parent reaches the child.
- **The legacy's five-year bar mostly repeats the 1996 law,** which already holds new permanent residents out of most means-tested programs for five years.

## What the evidence says, in four lines

1. **A parent test reaches citizen children** (official estimate): the 2025 parent number rule removes the credit from about 500,000 children [EV-SEC-016]. Whether status checks also deter eligible members of mixed-status families is widely argued, but no measured estimate is in the register (Evidence still needed).
2. **The fiscal cost of opening programs arrives before the revenue** (official estimates): the 2021 Dreamer and TPS bill's on-budget direct spending, mostly marketplace credits, Medicaid and refundable credits, was $36.5 billion over 2021-2031 [EV-IMM-601] [EV-IMM-602].
3. **Over a long horizon the federal effect of immigrants is positive, the state and local effect negative** (consensus review) [EV-IMM-222], which is why states watch these rules.
4. **No score of a table like this exists** (judgment); section 6 prices it with the household and coverage models.

## Options

**A. Current law applied to the new statuses.** The 2025 law's limits stay; the new statuses are not "qualified" and are excluded until they become permanent residents, then wait five years.
- *Cost:* lowest; legalized families pay taxes and get little.
- *Burden:* every program checks status; mixed-status families fall through. It contradicts care's and housing's decided directions.

**B. The legacy: restore the pre-2025 law, bar the new statuses for five years.** The S. 744 model (`domain-immigration.md`, line 57); the health plan and the child benefit follow their own rules; people without status are excluded.
- *Cost:* about $0.02-0.04 trillion less than C over FY2030-39 (judgment), with health coverage for people without status left out.
- *Burden:* a five-year clock checked by every means-tested program.

**C. Residence for the plan's universal programs; status, restored, for the older means-tested ones (recommended).** The table below.
- *Cost:* about $0.05-0.11 trillion over FY2030-39 and $8-15 billion in FY2040 (judgment), of which:
  - health coverage for people without status in the single plan from its first wave on January 1, 2031: about 1.5-3 million people left without status after memo 1 (judgment), at about $3,000-5,000 a person, well below the average of $15,474 [EV-CAR-120] for a younger, working population (judgment), part of whom would be covered anyway: about $3-9 billion a year;
  - no five-year wait for the new permanent residents in SNAP, SSI and TANF and in Medicaid before its wave: about $2-4 billion a year from 2031 (judgment).
- *What other dossiers already priced:* care's restoration of lawfully present immigrants (HLT-1.02, decided), the secure floor's SNAP restoration (memo 3, part 2 B, open) and the child benefit's ID rule (memo 1 part 2, open, about $0.02-0.03 trillion). They are not counted again here.

**D. Every resident in every program, including SNAP and SSI for people without status.**
- *Cost:* C plus about $1-3 billion a year (judgment), on a small group after memo 1.
- *What it costs politically:* cash and food aid for people without status is the hardest sell in the table, for little reach beyond C.

## The four open items other dossiers left here

1. **The child benefit's number rule (secure floor memo 1).** The table takes memo 1 part 2 B, the child's own ID, as the working assumption while question 0o is open. Separately, the new statuses carry work-eligible numbers from registration (point 5 in section 3), so most families affected by the 2025 rule qualify even if Espen keeps it.
2. **Coverage for people without status (care, point 3).** Covered as residents from wave 1, January 1, 2031, by the S. 1506 residency rule, without new papers. Care's coverage model carries a 1% placeholder for this group (`legacy-research.md`, finding 7); section 6 prices it with care.
3. **The owner programs (housing).** Mixed-status owner families qualify through the owner of record, as rental help does through proration; FHA's 2025 rule is reversed; the property-tax match is written outside 1611 and 1621.
4. **The emergency fund (HOU-1D).** No status test, as short-term emergency aid.

**The data firewall.** One rule across the plan: what a person gives a plan program or memo 2's registration, and what the plan's shared records hold, may not be used or shared for immigration enforcement, with penalties, except under a criminal warrant for a named person. It matches housing's decided rule (`DEC-2026-11-09-11`) and care's (HLT-7.21), and IMM-4's information sharing is written to match (memo 5).

## Recommendation: C

- **One rule a family can understand:** the plan's new programs ask where you live, not what papers you hold; the older programs ask what they asked before 2025.
- **It reaches citizen children through their own ID,** not their parents' status [EV-SEC-016] [EV-IMM-007].
- **It keeps the line most voters draw** between health care, child care and emergency help for everyone who lives here and cash and food aid tied to status.
- **It follows the design lens** (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`): fewer status checks, residence from records, the owner of record only, the landlord checked instead of the tenant.
- **What it costs in burden:** almost none on families. What it buys: one answer per family. **What it costs in money:** about $0.05-0.11 trillion over the decade, reported to the financing settlement (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`).

**What would change this:** a vote count that needs a waiting period (keep the five-year wait for SNAP and SSI only, about $0.02-0.04 trillion less); evidence that residence-based coverage draws arrivals (no such evidence is in the register).
