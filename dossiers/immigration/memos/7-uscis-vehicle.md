# Memo 7: Paying for USCIS, naturalization fees, and the vehicle

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 12 and the fee part of fork 10 of the immigration dossier (`../README.md`, "Forks for I3"; `../3-design.md`). Status: draft for decision (board row I3, question 0p); nothing here is decided. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions. Citizenship's other items (codifying *Barbara*, denaturalization, the test) are yes-or-no points in section 3.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **part 1 B, appropriations pay for legalization, asylum and the naturalization discount, fees at cost pay for the rest, a six-month processing target in statute; the naturalization fee held at $760 with a waiver below 200% of poverty and half price to 400%; part 2 B, two bills: the 2029 reconciliation bill carries USCIS's money, the fee changes, memo 6's eligibility restorations and the rescission of unobligated wall money, and one regular-order immigration bill carries status, legal immigration, asylum, enforcement rules and citizenship, scored with CRT-5.** Cost: USCIS's appropriation need is about **$0.02-0.03 trillion over FY2030-39 and about $1.5-2.5 billion in FY2040 (judgment)**, including memos 2 and 4's USCIS parts; this replaces the legacy's $40 billion USCIS line and $6 billion asylum reserve (about $0.046 trillion), so the plan-wide agency rebuild falls by about $0.02 trillion. Evidence: official data on USCIS funding and lost numbers; a randomized trial on fees; CBO's estimate of the 2025 fee title; a news account of the 2021 parliamentarian rulings.

**This memo decides who pays for the agency that carries memos 1 to 4 and naturalization, and which bills carry the flagship.**

## The question

USCIS is about 96% fee-funded [EV-IMM-145]. A fee-funded agency shrinks when applications fall and cannot hire ahead of a surge: it lost about 62,000 employment numbers in FY2021 for want of staff [EV-IMM-123], and its pending cases rose past 12.8 million in 2026 while completions fell [EV-IMM-140] [EV-IMM-141]. Memo 1 adds 8-10 million filings over the decade (judgment), and memo 2 waives fees for the poorest. The legacy funds the surge from the agency rebuild's USCIS line, $40 billion, 7% of it [EV-IMM-573], and proposes $6 billion more for asylum and refugee processing, pending Espen [EV-IMM-578]. The legacy capacity review found the asylum need about $5.4 billion, half of what IMM-3 carried [EV-IMM-574]. None of the three has been reconciled (`legacy-research.md`, finding 3).

Naturalization: the fee is $760 on paper [EV-IMM-422]; DHS proposed in June 2026 to raise it to $1,330 and end waivers [EV-IMM-423]. About 9 million permanent residents were eligible in 2023, against 823,702 applications [EV-IMM-420] [EV-IMM-428].

The vehicle: the legacy plans one regular-order bill of 12 floor days (`legacy-research.md`, section 4). The parliamentarian rejected legalization in the 2021 reconciliation bill three times (news account) [EV-IMM-238], while the 2025 and 2026 reconciliation laws carried immigration money and fees [EV-IMM-619] [EV-IMM-039].

## What the evidence says, in four lines

1. **Fees deter citizenship, and a voucher overcomes it** (randomized, one state) [EV-IMM-425]: the strongest evidence in the dossier on any delivery choice.
2. **Capacity is the binding constraint on legal numbers** (official data) [EV-IMM-123].
3. **Immigration fees can be set in reconciliation** (official estimate): CBO scored the 2025 law's Judiciary title at $66.5 billion of revenue over 2025-2034, mostly fees, and $73.5 billion of outlays [EV-IMM-619]; it nets fee revenue by about 25% for lower tax receipts [EV-IMM-621].
4. **Executive design is reversed by the next administration** (section 2, part F): statute and an agency funded to carry it out last.

## Part 1. Paying for USCIS

**The bottom-up need over FY2030-39 (judgment; replaces the legacy's three unreconciled figures):**

| Item | FY2030-39 | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registration and green-card surge, ahead of fees, with memo 2's waivers | $4-8 billion | 8-10 million filings at a per-case cost between DACA's $495 [EV-IMM-638] and an adjustment's $1,440 [EV-IMM-627], about half waived |
| Asylum officer corps (memo 4) | $6-10 billion | DHS's 2022 estimate scaled [EV-IMM-302]; the legacy review's $5.4 billion [EV-IMM-574]; less the employer-paid Asylum Program Fee [EV-IMM-626] |
| Backlog clearance | About $3 billion | Legacy, about $0.7 billion a year for four to five years [EV-IMM-575] |
| Online filing, the Office of New Americans | About $1 billion | Legacy capacity components (`legacy-research.md`, section 2) |
| Naturalization discount and waivers | $3-5 billion | About 1 million applications a year at a rising rate, a third to a half discounted or waived (judgment) |
| **Total** | **About $17-27 billion** | Against the legacy's $40 billion plus $6 billion |

**A. Fee-funded, with the legacy surge line.** USCIS stays about 96% fee-funded; CAP-7S's $40 billion pays for hiring.
- *What it costs:* the surge line is about twice the bottom-up need; and without appropriations for waived work, fees rise for everyone else to cover it.

**B. Appropriate the public-interest work; fees at cost for the rest (recommended).**
- **What it does.** Appropriations, sized from the table, pay for legalization, asylum and refugee work and the naturalization discount, which serve the public as much as the applicant; fees at cost pay for petitions, renewals and other benefits; the employer-paid Asylum Program Fee stays. A six-month processing target for standard applications in statute, with monthly published times (legacy).
- *Cost:* about $0.02-0.03 trillion over FY2030-39; about $1.5-2.5 billion in FY2040 as the surge ends.
- *What it buys:* an agency that can hire before registration opens, and fees that do not rise to cover waivers.

**C. A fully appropriated USCIS.**
- *What it costs:* about $5-6 billion a year more of general-fund spending for work applicants and employers now pay for (judgment, from fee revenue at today's volumes), and annual appropriations fights over an agency that now runs through shutdowns on fees.

## Part 2. Naturalization fees (fork 10)

- **Recommended:** hold the N-400 fee at $760 [EV-IMM-422], a full waiver below 200% of poverty (the legacy) and half price from 200% to 400% (the 2024 rule gave half price from 150% to 400% [EV-IMM-424]), paid for by part 1 B's appropriation; the June 2026 proposal is withdrawn [EV-IMM-423].
- *Why:* the voucher trial doubled applications among low-income immigrants [EV-IMM-425]; about 8 million eligible people have not applied [EV-IMM-420] [EV-IMM-428].
- *Alternatives:* the 2024 rule as it stands (no cost; lower take-up below 200%); free naturalization for all (about $0.6-0.8 billion a year more, judgment).

## Part 3. The vehicle

**A. One regular-order bill (the legacy).** IMM-1 to IMM-6 in 12 floor days, scheduled for September 2029 (`legacy-research.md`, section 4).
- *Risk:* everything waits on the hardest vote; USCIS has no money until it passes.

**B. Two bills (recommended).**
- *The 2029 reconciliation bill:* USCIS's appropriation (part 1 B) and the naturalization discount; repeal of the 2025 law's asylum-pending and work-permit charges [EV-IMM-043] [EV-IMM-045]; memo 6's eligibility restorations, which reverse limits the 2025 law made by reconciliation [EV-IMM-046] to [EV-IMM-050]; rescission of unobligated wall money [EV-IMM-034]; the H-1B fee (memo 3, ledger IF1). Enforcement money from the two reconciliation laws is not extended (memo 5).
- *The FY2030 Homeland Security appropriations act:* detention at memo 5's cap and case management, in place by October 1, 2029, when the reconciliation money lapses [EV-IMM-035] [EV-IMM-039].
- *The regular-order immigration bill, 2029:* status (memos 1 and 2), legal immigration and the workforce routes (memo 3), asylum and the court requirements (memo 4), enforcement rules (memo 5), the residency rule and the firewall (memo 6), and citizenship (points 10-12). Scored with CRT-5, which democratic renewal may carry in the same bill.
- *What it buys:* USCIS hires in 2029 before status passes; the fiscal pieces pass at 51 votes; the regular-order bill carries rules, not money.

**C. Three regular-order bills** (status; legal immigration and workforce; enforcement and courts).
- *What it buys:* each part finds its own coalition.
- *What it costs:* the pairing that holds the bill together breaks; the status bill alone is the hardest vote, and the enforcement bill may pass without it.

## Recommendation: parts 1 B, 2 and 3 B

- **The agency must exist before the status does.** Recapture without capacity lost 62,000 numbers [EV-IMM-123]; a registration without staff would do the same at scale.
- **Waived fees are a public cost, paid as one.** That keeps fees at cost for everyone else and lets the voucher evidence work for naturalization [EV-IMM-425].
- **The money goes where 51 votes suffice,** as the 2025 and 2026 laws showed [EV-IMM-619], and the rules go where they must, in regular order [EV-IMM-238].
- **It answers the legacy's three unreconciled USCIS figures** with one bottom-up line, about half the legacy's, and folds the pending $6 billion asylum reserve into it (point 9 in section 3).

**What would change this:** a Byrd-rule ruling against the eligibility restorations (move them to the regular-order bill); a vote count that cannot hold the regular-order bill together (option C, starting with tier A and the court).
