# Memo 4: Asylum, the border, and what the system needs from the courts

Role: decision memo for Espen, forks 6 and 7 of the immigration dossier (`../README.md`, "Forks for I3"; `../3-design.md`). Status: draft for decision (board row I3, question 0p); nothing here is decided. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions. Figures and analysis from the second Trump administration's agencies are its own (`DEC-2026-10-30-01`). The immigration court itself (CRT-5) is designed by the democratic renewal foundation; part 2 states only what this flagship needs from it.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **part 1 D, asylum officers decide claims first within 180 days (the 2022 rule in statute), a statutory refugee floor of 50,000, access to claims from the region, and a surge rule that answers high encounters with capacity (a 90-day officer track and reception centers), not a bar on asylum; Proclamation 10888's use and the asylum-pending fee are repealed; part 2 B, the court requirements: the 800-judge cap repealed, judges sized to clear the backlog in about six years, appointed counsel for children, people with serious mental illness and everyone detained, independence through CRT-5.** Cost: about **$0.02-0.04 trillion over FY2030-39 and about $2.5-4 billion in FY2040 (judgment)**: the asylum corps about $6-10 billion, the refugee floor $5-10 billion, regional access $3-5 billion and counsel $6-12 billion over the decade; CRT-5's own court cost (legacy about $0.03 trillion) is scored with democratic renewal. Evidence: official data and rules; observational studies of counsel; one program evaluation; agency projections; international practice.

**This memo decides who first decides an asylum claim, what the plan keeps or ends of the 2024-26 border measures, the refugee floor, and what the immigration system requires of the court.** Memo 5 covers detention.

## The question

The border is not the 2029 problem: southwest border encounters fell from a record 2,475,669 in FY2023 to 443,671 in FY2025 [EV-IMM-161], and DHS reports 379,243 nationwide in FY2026 (preliminary, its own figure) [EV-IMM-160]. The backlogs are: about 1.5 million affirmative asylum cases at USCIS [EV-IMM-146], 2,424,061 asylum applications pending in the courts [EV-IMM-111], and 3.3 million court cases in all [EV-IMM-100]. A July 2026 rule sends affirmative cases to court without an interview [EV-IMM-305], which DHS expects to raise court referrals at first [EV-IMM-308]. From November 1, 2028 the law caps the courts at 800 judges [EV-IMM-105], and judges are removable at will.

The legacy design (IMM-3) has asylum officers decide merits within 180 days, regional processing, a refugee floor of about 50,000 [EV-IMM-572] and port screening (`domain-immigration.md`, lines 73-83). It has no answer to a future surge, which is the question a broad public consent asks first.

## Why it matters

- **Officers grant, and courts often grant again.** Asylum officers granted 40% of the claims they decided in 2022 (Human Rights First) [EV-IMM-321]; judges granted 76% of affirmative cases officers referred in FY2022-23 [EV-IMM-322]. A referral is often a second full hearing.
- **Fast dockets without counsel produce removal orders, not decisions.** 99.1% of the first cases on the Los Angeles dedicated docket ended in removal orders, 72.4% in absentia [EV-IMM-317].
- **The asylum system now charges for its own delay:** $100 a year while a claim is pending, with $550 for the first work permit [EV-IMM-043] [EV-IMM-045].
- **Refugee admissions were nearly shut:** a FY2026 ceiling of 7,500 raised to 17,500, with 7,730 admitted through June [EV-IMM-052].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Counsel is strongly associated with relief and appearance** (observational, national): odds of relief 5.5 times higher with a lawyer [EV-IMM-314]; 96% of represented non-detained people attended every hearing [EV-IMM-315]; New York's public defender program projected 48% success against 4% (program evaluation) [EV-IMM-327]. Not randomized; the true effect is smaller than the gap.
2. **The 2022 officer track ran small** (agency data): through June 2023, of 1,824 USCIS outcomes, 396 were grants and 747 were referred to a judge [EV-IMM-640]; DHS projected about 800 staff and $180 million for 75,000 cases a year at full use [EV-IMM-302]. No grant-rate or timeliness evaluation is in the register.
3. **Speed can be bought, with trade-offs** (official statistics, abroad): the UK cut pending initial decisions 56% in a year while its grant rate fell from 48% to 38% [EV-IMM-461] [EV-IMM-460]; the EU caps its border procedure at 12 weeks [EV-IMM-458].
4. **More judges alone do not clear the backlog** (CRS projection on FY2023 inflows) [EV-IMM-112]; new court receipts have since fallen by more than two-thirds [EV-IMM-102], and legalization (memo 1) takes long-settled residents out of the court's docket.
5. **A floor of 50,000 sits below every ceiling from FY1981 to FY2016:** the lowest was 67,000 in FY1986 [EV-IMM-634], and the decade to 2016 ran 70,000 to 85,000 [EV-IMM-463]. Admissions can fall below a ceiling [EV-IMM-465], so the floor must bind admissions.

## Part 1. Asylum and the border

**A. The legacy IMM-3.** Officers decide merits within 180 days, regional processing, a floor of about 50,000, port screening; the 2024-26 border measures rescinded; no surge rule.

**B. A plus the 2024 bill's border emergency authority.** Required at an average of 5,000 encounters a day, or 8,500 in one day, allowed at 4,000; asylum limited between ports while it runs; a tighter screening standard [EV-IMM-533] [EV-IMM-534].
- *What it buys:* the one bipartisan Senate answer to a surge (cloture failed 49-50 on other grounds [EV-IMM-532]).
- *What it costs:* a bar on asylum between ports during a surge, the measure the D.C. Circuit held unlawful when done by proclamation [EV-IMM-054].

**C. The January 2029 baseline.** The 2026 no-interview referral rule, Proclamation 10888's entry suspension and the 2024 rule's triggers stay [EV-IMM-305] [EV-IMM-053] [EV-IMM-164]; no officer merits track.
- *Evidence against:* DHS's own projection of waits over 7.3 years [EV-IMM-319] and rising court referrals [EV-IMM-308].

**D. A with a surge rule: capacity, not a bar (recommended).**
- **What it does.**
  - *First decision by an officer:* the 2022 rule in statute, extended to affirmative claims; a decision within 180 days; denials go to the court for review on the record, with new evidence allowed.
  - *A surge rule:* when encounters pass a published trigger (the 2024 bill's 5,000 a day is the working number), the law moves resources, not rights: asylum officers and judges are detailed to reception centers near the border, claims there are decided in a 90-day track on the EU's model [EV-IMM-458], and people are released to case management (memo 5) or held only under memo 5's priorities. Asylum at and between ports stays available.
  - *Access from the region:* processing of refugee and parole claims abroad, defined as access to a claim, never detention abroad (Australia's offshore model cost about A$5.6 million a person [EV-IMM-462]).
  - *The refugee floor:* 50,000 admissions a year in statute, funded, with private sponsorship above it (Canada's model [EV-IMM-466]); the President sets the ceiling above the floor.
  - *Ending 2025-26 measures:* Proclamation 10888's use against asylum seekers ends on January 20, 2029 (executive); the 2026 no-interview rule is withdrawn; the $100-a-year pending fee is repealed [EV-IMM-043]; the asylum application keeps a work permit after 150 days without the new $550 charge [EV-IMM-045].
  - *Ports:* screening technology at ports instead of new wall construction (legacy); the unobligated wall balance from the 2025 law, available until September 30, 2029 [EV-IMM-034], is rescinded in the first 2029 bill (point 7 in section 3).
- *For people:* a family that crosses in a surge is screened, released to a case manager and decided by an officer in about three months, with a lawyer if they are detained.
- *Cost:* the asylum corps, about $0.6-1.0 billion a year at full scale, against DHS's 2022 estimate of $180 million for 75,000 cases [EV-IMM-302] scaled to about 250,000-350,000 decisions a year while the backlog clears, and the legacy capacity review's $5.4 billion over the decade [EV-IMM-574] (judgment: about $6-10 billion over FY2030-39). The employer-paid Asylum Program Fee covers part (about $313 million a year [EV-IMM-626]). The refugee floor, about $0.5-1.0 billion a year above a low baseline (judgment). Regional access, about $0.3-0.5 billion a year (judgment).
- *Political risk:* opponents will say a surge rule without a bar invites crossings. The answer is speed: a decision in 90 days, with removal of those denied, is what a bar promises and does not deliver.

## Part 2. What the system needs from the courts (fork 7)

The court's form, tenure and Article III fallback stay with democratic renewal (CRT-5, legacy about $0.03 trillion [EV-IMM-576]). This flagship needs five things from it, whatever its form:

1. **Capacity.** Repeal the 800-judge cap [EV-IMM-105]. Judges sized to clear the backlog in about six years at today's inflow, after legalization removes long-settled residents' cases (judgment: about 1,000-1,200 judges with support staff, set from a docket model in section 6).
2. **Timelines.** Detained cases decided within 90 days; review of an officer's asylum denial within 180 days; non-detained cases within two years once the backlog is cleared.
3. **Notice and in absentia limits.** Hearing notices sent to the address in the records the government holds (memo 2), with a right to reopen an order issued without proper notice; 15% of in absentia orders in 2008-18 were later overturned [EV-IMM-316].
4. **Independence.** Judges not removable at will by the Attorney General; the 2026 court bill's Article I structure [EV-IMM-113] is the base CRT-5 uses.
5. **Counsel** (the fork):
   - **A.** Children and people with serious mental illness (legacy CRT-5; the 2026 bill appoints none [EV-IMM-114]).
   - **B. A plus everyone detained (recommended).** Detention is where representation is lowest, 31% [EV-IMM-324], and where New York's program changed outcomes [EV-IMM-327] [EV-IMM-328].
   - **C. Everyone in removal proceedings.** 2.9 million people faced removal without counsel in FY2024 [EV-IMM-325].
   - *Cost (judgment; no national cost study is in the register):* A about $0.3-0.5 billion a year; B about $0.6-1.2 billion a year with memo 5's 30,000 cap; C about $2-4 billion a year while the backlog lasts.

## Recommendation: part 1 D, part 2 with counsel B

- **Officers deciding first is the only design that both speeds decisions and keeps them full**, on the 2022 rule [EV-IMM-300] and the 76% re-grant rate that shows what referral without a decision costs [EV-IMM-322].
- **A surge needs an answer, and the evidence supports speed over bars.** The UK and the EU show capacity can cut waits [EV-IMM-461] [EV-IMM-458]; the D.C. Circuit shows bars by proclamation fail in court [EV-IMM-054].
- **Counsel for people detained, children and the seriously ill** is where it changes outcomes most and where a court cannot be fair without it [EV-IMM-324] [EV-IMM-327].
- **What it costs in burden:** none on applicants beyond a claim; on agencies, timelines. What it buys: decisions measured in months.

**What would change this:** a surge that overwhelms the 90-day track for months (then the trigger should add temporary judges, not a bar); evidence from a randomized counsel study showing a small effect (counsel A only).
