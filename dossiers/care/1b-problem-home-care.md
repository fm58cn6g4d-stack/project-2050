# Care you can count on, part B (care at home for all ages): the problem

Role: section 1 of the care dossier, part B: what is wrong with how the country pays for and gives help with daily living at home, for whom, how large the problem is, and how the law will stand in January 2029. Status: draft (stage D, step 5). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`, EV-CAR-400 on for part B; earlier tags are health's and housing's). Health's problem section is `1-problem.md`; its part 6 hands long-term care to this part.

Outcomes this section frames:
- OUT-03, care people can count on, for every age;
- OUT-08, time and practical freedom (family caregiving);
- OUT-02, planning beyond the next paycheck (the cost of care in old age).

**What "long-term services and supports" means here.** Help over a long period with daily activities (bathing, dressing, eating, getting around, managing medicines and money) for people of any age with a disability or chronic illness. It is given at home, in adult day programs, in assisted living, or in nursing homes and other institutions. This part is about the help, not the medical care around it, which the single plan covers (`4-specification.md`, HLT-7.05). In this file, "home care" means home and community-based services (HCBS).

## In brief

- **Most people will need it, and no public program insures it.** ASPE estimates that 56% of people turning 65 will need long-term care and 22% will need it for more than five years. The average paid cost is $137,800, and families pay almost 59% of it themselves (ASPE, 2022) [EV-CAR-400] [EV-CAR-401].
- **Medicare does not cover it.** Medicare pays for home health only for people who are homebound and need skilled care. It does not pay for personal care when that is the only help someone needs (Medicare.gov) [EV-CAR-412].
- **Medicaid covers it, after people become poor, and then with waiting lists.** Most states use SSI's $2,000 asset limit (CRS, 2017) [EV-CAR-409]. Home care is mostly an optional waiver service, which lets states cap enrollment (CRS, 2025) [EV-CAR-417]. Almost 650,000 people are on waiting or interest lists in 40 states, and they wait 34 months on average (KFF, 2026) [EV-HOU-691].
- **Families fill the gap.** 63 million people are family caregivers, averaging 27 hours a week. Nearly half report harm to their finances (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025) [EV-CAR-434].
- **Demand roughly doubles by 2040.** The number of people 85 and older grows from about 7.0 million in 2025 to about 13.7 million in 2040 (Census Bureau, 2023) [EV-CAR-403]. The home-care workforce is low-paid: the median wage is $35,800 a year (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-422].

## 1. Who needs help, and for how long

- **Older adults.** ASPE's microsimulation finds that 56% of people turning 65 will develop a disability serious enough to need long-term care, and about 22% will need it for more than five years (ASPE, 2022) [EV-CAR-400]. It projects that the number of people 65 and over with significant disabilities will grow from 7.2 million in 2020 to almost 14.3 million in 2065 [EV-CAR-402]. These are one model's projections, and they depend on how disability is defined. The Census Bureau's projection of the population 85 and older points the same way: about 7.0 million in 2025 and about 13.7 million in 2040 [EV-CAR-403].
- **Children and working-age adults.** Long-term care is not only for old age. In 2021, 40.5 percent of Medicaid home-care users were 65 or older, and 49.7 percent were working-age adults (MACPAC, 2025) [EV-CAR-408]. People with intellectual or developmental disabilities make up 72% of those on waiver waiting lists (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-433]. A design that treats long-term care as an old-age program would miss most of the people Medicaid serves today.
- **Where people get help.** Most Medicaid long-term care is now given at home: in 2023, 87.1 percent of Medicaid long-term care users received home care, and home care took 63.8 percent of Medicaid's long-term care spending. Older adults had the lowest home-care shares, 66.6 percent of users and 46.7 percent of spending (CMS, 2025) [EV-CAR-406]. In 2022, 7.8 million people used Medicaid home care at a cost of $129.4 billion, and 1.5 million used institutional care at a cost of $71.0 billion [EV-CAR-407]. About 1.2 million people lived in nursing facilities in 2024, and Medicaid was the primary payer for 63% of residents (KFF, 2025) [EV-CAR-438].

## 2. Who pays today

- **The total.** $563.7 billion was spent on long-term care in 2023, 13.7% of personal health care spending. Public sources paid 69.4%, and Medicaid alone paid $257.0 billion (45.6%). Out-of-pocket payments were 14.4%, and private insurance 8.7% (CRS, 2025) [EV-CAR-404]. The total includes Medicare's post-acute home health and skilled nursing care: $53.6 billion and $48.0 billion [EV-CAR-405]. It leaves out unpaid family care, the largest source of help.
- **Medicare covers recovery, not daily help.** Its home health benefit requires a person to be homebound and to need part-time or intermittent skilled care. Medicare does not pay for 24-hour care at home, or for custodial or personal care when that is the only help needed (Medicare.gov) [EV-CAR-412]. In 2024 about 2.7 million fee-for-service beneficiaries used home health, at a cost of $16.0 billion (MedPAC, 2026) [EV-CAR-413].
- **Medicaid covers people who are poor or have spent down.** Older and disabled people generally qualify under SSI's rules: $2,000 in countable resources for an individual and $3,000 for a couple. States may cover people in institutions with incomes up to 300% of the SSI payment, and the medically needy qualify only after spending their income and savings on care (CRS, 2017) [EV-CAR-409]. Those resource limits have not been raised since the 1980s [EV-CAR-431]. After death, states recover what they can from estates: about $733.4 million in fiscal year 2019, only about 0.55 percent of fee-for-service long-term care spending (MACPAC, 2021) [EV-CAR-410]. MACPAC recommended making estate recovery optional for states and limiting it.
- **Families pay most of the cost of care at home.** On ASPE's estimates, families pay about 74% of the cost of community care out of pocket [EV-CAR-402], and almost two in ten older adults will spend at least $100,000 of their own money on care [EV-CAR-401]. The private prices are high: $35 an hour for home care, $6,200 a month for assisted living, and $114,975 a year for a semi-private nursing-home room (CareScout, 2025) [EV-CAR-437]. That survey comes from a long-term care insurer, which has a stake in showing high costs.
- **Private insurance is a small and shrinking market.** Sales of individual long-term care policies fell from 754,000 in 2002 to 129,000 in 2014, and ownership among older adults fell from 13.8% to 11.4% between 2008 and 2016 (Federal Interagency Task Force, 2020) [EV-CAR-411].
- **States pay a large share.** States pay about 47 percent of Medicaid's long-term care cost (Urban Institute, 2020) [EV-CAR-448]. This is why state budgets shape who gets home care.

## 3. Waiting lists and uneven access

- **Why lists exist.** Home care under Medicaid is mostly offered through section 1915(c) waivers. Unlike state-plan services, waivers let states cap the number of people served (CRS, 2025) [EV-CAR-417].
- **How long.** In 2026, 40 states kept waiting or interest lists holding almost 650,000 people, who waited 34 months on average for waiver services (KFF, 2026) [EV-HOU-691] [EV-CAR-138].
- **The count is uneven.** 32 of the states with lists screen people for eligibility on at least one waiver. The eight states that screen on none hold 56% of everyone waiting, so the lists mix eligible and possibly ineligible people. More than 5 million Medicaid enrollees use home care [EV-CAR-433]. KFF says the lists are an incomplete measure of unmet need and are not comparable across states [EV-CAR-138].
- **What it means for people.** A family with a child with autism, or an adult with an intellectual disability living with aging parents, can wait years with no help while still meeting every rule. Older adults, who are the most likely to end up in a nursing home, have the lowest home-care shares [EV-CAR-406].

## 4. Family caregivers carry the system

- **How many and how much.** 63 million adults, about a quarter of all adults, were family caregivers in 2025, 59 million of them caring for adults. They averaged 27 hours a week, and 24 percent gave 40 hours or more. Nearly half reported at least one negative effect on their finances, and seven in ten working-age caregivers also held paid jobs (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025) [EV-CAR-434].
- **What it is worth.** AARP values the care family caregivers of adults gave in 2024 at $1.01 trillion, more than all Medicaid spending that year ($932 billion) (AARP, 2026) [EV-CAR-435]. An advocate produced that figure, and its rise from the 2023 edition mostly reflects a new method. ASPE's independent estimate points the same way: over a lifetime, the unpaid care given to an older adult with severe disabilities is worth about $111,000, about the same as the expected cost of paid care [EV-CAR-436]. Nearly two-thirds of older adults living in the community who get help with a disability get only unpaid help (Urban Institute, 2020) [EV-CAR-448].
- **Who carries it.** Unpaid care falls hardest on families without the money to buy care and without Medicaid eligibility: middle-income households and those just above Medicaid's lines. This is the time-and-freedom problem behind OUT-08. Caregivers' lost earnings and retirement credits are a secure-floor question (`charter/outcomes.md`, OUT-08), not this part's.

## 5. The workforce

- **Size and pay.** There were 4,677.1 thousand home health and personal care aide jobs in 2025, with a median annual wage of $35,800 (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-422].
- **Demand.** BLS projects 18.1% growth to 5,524.4 thousand jobs by 2035, with about 760.5 thousand openings a year, mostly to replace workers who leave [EV-CAR-423]. That is BLS's projection, and nothing in the register checks it independently.
- **Why it matters here.** Any promise of care at home is only as good as the workers available to give it. Part D (the care workforce) owns wages, training and supply; shared rule 8 owns immigration and apprenticeships. This section only names the constraint. Section 3 says where the benefit's design meets it.

## 6. The baseline in January 2029

Current law as it will stand when the plan starts, counting enacted laws with later effective dates and scheduled expirations (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`).

**The 2025 reconciliation law (Public Law 119-21):**
- **Retroactive coverage cut.** From January 1, 2027, retroactive coverage is cut to one month before application for expansion adults and two months for others, down from three (CRS, 2025) [EV-CAR-418]. This hits people who apply for nursing-home or home care after care has begun. Health's first bill restores it (`3-design.md`, point 1; `DEC-2026-11-10-09`).
- **Home equity cap.** From January 1, 2028, the home equity limit for long-term care eligibility is capped at $1,000,000, which ends its indexing above that amount [EV-CAR-415].
- **New waiver option.** From July 1, 2028, states may run a standalone home-care waiver for people below the institutional level of care. The conditions are that it does not lengthen waits on other waivers and that its per-person cost stays below institutional care's [EV-CAR-414]. It is an option, not money or an entitlement.
- **Staffing standards on hold.** The 2024 nursing-home minimum staffing standards cannot be enforced until September 30, 2034 [EV-CAR-416].
- **Medicaid cuts.** The law cuts Medicaid by about $989.7 billion over ten years (CBO, via CRS, 2025) [EV-CAR-025]. Home care beyond home health is optional for states, so it is where states can cut when budgets tighten. This is judgment, not a measured effect.
- **Waiver budget neutrality.** From 2027 CMS may approve a demonstration waiver only if it will not raise federal spending [EV-HOU-337]. CMS also withdrew its guidance on housing-related services in March 2025 [EV-HOU-339].

**Expirations before 2029.**
- **Money Follows the Person.** The program has moved 127,184 people out of institutions since 2008 (CMS, 2025) [EV-CAR-421]. It is funded only through September 30, 2027.
- **Spousal protections for home care.** These protections expire with the program after FY2027 unless Congress extends them [EV-HOU-697]. Under current law, neither exists when the plan starts.

**The 2024 access rule.**
- **Worker pay rule.** From six years after its July 9, 2024 effective date, that is July 2030, at least 80 percent of Medicaid payments for homemaker, home health aide and personal care services must go to workers' compensation (CMS, 2024) [EV-CAR-419].
- **Waiting-list reporting.** States must report waiver waiting lists and how quickly services are delivered [EV-CAR-432], starting in 2027 [EV-CAR-433].
- **Planned rollback.** CMS's regulatory agenda lists a proposed rule to rescind or revise provisions of the 2024 Medicaid rules. No proposal had been published as of mid-November 2026 (CMS Unified Agenda) [EV-CAR-420]. The 2029 baseline should therefore assume either provision may be gone.

**Health's decided path.**
- **Medicaid enrollees move, long-term care stays.** Medicaid enrollees move to the single plan in wave 2 (2033-35), and everyone is covered by January 1, 2035. Long-term care stays in Medicaid, with today's eligibility rules and state roles, until this part decides (`4-specification.md`, HLT-7.07; `DEC-2026-11-10-09`, `-11`).
- **What that leaves.** Without part B, a person on the single plan who needs help at home still has to become poor enough for Medicaid, and then wait.

## 7. What this means for the design

- The gap is not a missing program for the poor. Medicaid already pays for most paid long-term care. The gap is a missing insurance for everyone else, and a guarantee that Medicaid's coverage is real rather than a waiting list.
- About half of the people served are under 65, so the design must work for children and working-age adults as well as for older people.
- Families give most of the care, and any benefit either supports them or replaces them. Section 3 has to decide which.
- The workforce is the binding constraint on delivery. Part D sizes it, and this part's options are priced on the assumption that wages rise.

## Evidence still needed

- An independent count of people with long-term care needs of every age living in the community, with and without paid help: the National Health and Aging Trends Study for older adults, and the National Health Interview Survey or MEPS for younger people. It would anchor section 3's working assumption on new users.
- KFF's or CMS's current count of home-care spending by state and share paid by states, to replace the 2016-based 47 percent.
- CBO's December 2020 single-payer working paper (option 5 includes long-term care) and its November 2021 estimate for the Build Back Better Act's home-care provisions, from CBO itself. CBO blocks automated downloads; an upload from Espen would let the register quote them directly instead of through KFF and Urban.
- The OECD's United States figure for long-term care spending as a share of GDP (it is in a chart, not the text).
- BLS's hourly wage series for home health and personal care aides (OEWS); the BLS site refused the download.
- Nursing-home spending by payer and the share that is room and board, for section 3's memo on institutional care.

Claims this section leaves out for lack of evidence: the share of family caregivers who reduce work hours or leave jobs, measured independently; and the effect of the 2025 law's cuts on state home-care spending, which has not yet been observed.
