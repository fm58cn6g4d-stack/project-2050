## 1. What this part is about, in plain language

**Long-term services and supports** means help with everyday living, given over months or years to someone who cannot manage alone because of disability, illness or age: bathing, dressing, eating, getting around, taking medicines, managing money. It is given at home, in adult day programs, in assisted living, or in nursing homes and similar institutions. It is not medical treatment, which health's single plan covers.

**Who uses it.**
- Mostly older adults, but not only them.
- In 2021, 40.5 percent of Medicaid's home-care users were 65 or older, and 49.7 percent were working-age adults [EV-CAR-408]. The rest were children.
- People with intellectual or developmental disabilities are 72% of those on waiting lists [EV-CAR-433].
- About 1.2 million people live in nursing facilities [EV-CAR-438].

**Who pays today.**
- **Medicaid pays most of it,** $257.0 billion in 2023 [EV-CAR-404], but only after people have spent their savings down to about $2,000 [EV-CAR-409]. Home care is mostly optional for states, so almost 650,000 people wait [EV-HOU-691].
- **Families pay the rest** out of pocket, or give the care themselves unpaid [EV-CAR-401] [EV-CAR-434].
- **Private long-term care insurance** is a small and shrinking market [EV-CAR-411].
- **Medicare does not pay for personal care.** It pays only for short skilled care after an illness [EV-CAR-412].

**What "long-term care stays in Medicaid until part B decides" means.** This is HLT-7.07 in `4-specification.md`. It follows from your answer to health memo 4: "long-term care in part B" (`DEC-2026-11-10-09`).
- **The single plan covers medical care, not daily help.** Under the decided health path, people on Medicaid move to the single plan in 2033-35 for their doctors, hospitals and drugs. Their help with daily living does not move. The same Medicaid office keeps paying for their home aide or nursing home, under today's rules: the asset test, the state's waiting list, and the state's share of the cost.
- **The cost is booked where it is now.** After #237, health's cost figures leave that spending in Medicaid at today's cost, so nothing about long-term care is counted twice or repriced.
- **Nothing changes for families yet.** A woman whose mother gets Medicaid home care sees no change in 2035. A family with $60,000 in savings still gets no help at home until the savings are gone.
- **It is a placeholder.** Part B's memos decide whether and how that help later moves into the plan.

### Memo 6 B's cost against Urban's model

All figures here are project arithmetic on [EV-CAR-483], to be redone in section 6.

**Urban's option 1 is close to memo 6 B plus memo 7.**
- Home care for everyone who meets the two-daily-activity test, with no income test.
- A cap of up to $150 a day.
- Medicaid keeps institutional care.

**Its estimate.**
- In 2020 it raises federal spending by $355 billion (best guess), on current-law total spending of $309 billion.
- It serves 10.0 million people averaging $40,900 each.

**Scaled to 2035.**
- At part B's 5.5% a year, 2020 to 2035 multiplies by about 2.2.
- Urban removes all of the states' home-care share; memo 6 B keeps half of it. Taking that half out (about $25 billion in 2020) gives about $330 billion.
- That is about $740 billion in 2035, or about $500 billion on Urban's low-cost case.

**Against memo 6 B.** Memo 6 B says about $215 billion (range $125-370 billion).

**Why they differ.**
- Mainly the cost of new users. Part B assumes $12,000-20,000 each (2022 dollars) because family care continues. Urban's average across all users is $40,900 in 2020.
- Memo 6 B's capped copays and tier budgets would lower Urban's figure somewhat.
- Urban calls its own projections "highly speculative", and it is one modeler's work.

**What this changes.**
- Memo 6 is mainly about money and whom it reaches, so its cost goes to Espen (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`). The cost should come with Urban as the high check, or with a stated reason why it is lower.
- Memo 6 says memo 6 A costs "about a fifth of B's cost". On Urban's basis, A's about $45 billion is about a tenth of the low case (about $500 billion) and a sixteenth of the central one (about $740 billion).

3. **Memo 6 B, cost:** part B's figures are on the low side of the one independent model of nearly the same design (above). The memo cites Urban's "hundreds of billions" as consistent, when Urban's own numbers point higher.
4. **2b's table and memo 9:** *"44 states pay spouses or parents through waivers"*. KFF says 44 states "allow" such payments in at least one waiver [EV-CAR-439]. Over two-thirds of states cannot count how many recipients get paid family care [EV-CAR-493].

## 4. Family caregivers: what exists, what memo 9 A adds, and the options

**What already exists.**
- **Medicaid.** Every responding state pays some family caregivers. 44 states allow paying spouses or parents of minor children through waivers, and 6 under the state plan. 11 states run structured family caregiving [EV-CAR-439] [EV-CAR-493]. In California's IHSS, about 70% of recipients hire a family member [EV-CAR-494]. Over two-thirds of states cannot say how many of their waiver recipients get paid family care [EV-CAR-493].
- **VA.** About 70,000 caregivers are in the comprehensive program, with a stipend pegged to a federal pay grade, out of about 98,000 caregivers served. VA obligated $2.6 billion in FY2025 [EV-CAR-492] [EV-CAR-430].
- **States outside Medicaid.** Washington's caregiver programs give services rather than wages, and Pennsylvania reimburses costs (not in register).
- **Abroad.** In Germany, 3,1 million of 5,7 million people in need of care took only the cash benefit and were cared for mostly by relatives [EV-CAR-485].

**What memo 9 A would add.**
1. **A right in every state,** instead of a state option, including for spouses and parents.
2. **Pay at the benefit's wage** with labor protections, where today's rates vary by state.
3. **Paid family care for middle-income families,** because the benefit has no asset test. This is the largest new piece, and where most of the cost and the political risk sit.
4. **Respite and training** as covered services.
5. **A public payroll agent** in each state.

**Does paid care replace unpaid care (the "woodwork" effect)?** The evidence is mixed, and both views are given.
- **It replaces some unpaid care.** In the Cash and Counseling randomized trial, paid hours rose, but total hours did not change, because unpaid hours fell or did not differ [EV-CAR-486]. An unpublished study of Medicaid home care finds its main effect is to replace family care [EV-CAR-491].
- **It doesn't.** A peer-reviewed study of state home-care expansions finds they raised adult children's informal care by 5 percentage points [EV-CAR-489]. A study of private insurance found no fall in informal care over about eight years [EV-CAR-490].
- **Reading.** Inside a capped budget, as in Cash and Counseling and memo 7 B, substitution changes who is paid more than how much is spent. The cost risk is new users who now get only unpaid care and would claim paid hours. That is memo 6 B's new-user line, and section 2's cost check suggests it may be underpriced.

**Quality.**
- **Better for the people served.** Self-direction gave more paid help and fewer unmet needs, and the fear that it would harm safety was not borne out [EV-CAR-428].
- **Easier on caregivers.** Caregivers reported less physical and financial strain [EV-CAR-487].
- **Harder on relatives as workers.** Paid relatives reported more emotional strain than agency workers [EV-CAR-488].
- **Fraud.** HHS's inspector general has reported fraud in personal care programs, including by family attendants (not in register).
- **Respite.** Respite trials are few and found no significant effect on caregivers [EV-CAR-496]. That is a gap in the evidence, not proof respite fails.

**Public opinion.** One advocate-sponsored poll from March 2026 found 64 percent saying government should pay all family caregivers, not only low-income ones [EV-CAR-495]. It is a single poll.

**Espen's concern, tested.**
- **Paying relatives is not a new idea.** Red and blue states, the VA and a bipartisan tax bill already do it [EV-CAR-493] [EV-CAR-492] [EV-CAR-475].
- **The risk lies elsewhere.** It is in memo 9 A's piece 3: paying middle-income families for care, as a new entitlement, in the same bill as Medicare for All. The evidence supports Espen's caution on scale and timing, not on the principle.
