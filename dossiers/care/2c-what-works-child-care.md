# Care you can count on, part C (child care and pre-K): what works

Role: section 2 of the care dossier, part C. It covers the evidence on child care and pre-K programs at home and abroad, what they cost and the failures to avoid. Status: draft (stage D, step 6). Each finding names its design strength: a randomized trial, a lottery, a quasi-experimental study (regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, sibling comparison), a program evaluation, descriptive data or expert judgment. Tags point to `evidence.json` (EV-CAR-500 to 599).

## In brief

- **Mothers' employment rises when care is cheap, if mothers were held back by price.**
  - *Quebec.* Its low flat fee raised mothers' employment strongly (quasi-experimental) [EV-CAR-530] [EV-CAR-532].
  - *Norway.* Its 1970s expansion mostly replaced informal care, with little employment gain [EV-CAR-544].
- **Children's outcomes depend on quality and on what care replaces.**
  - *Norway.* Long-run gains [EV-CAR-543].
  - *Quebec.* Fast expansion into mostly "fair" quality care produced worse behavior, health and later crime [EV-CAR-531] [EV-CAR-536].
- **Pre-K can change lives, but not every program does.**
  - *Boston's lottery.* Its school-based pre-K raised college-going and cut juvenile incarceration, without lasting test-score gains [EV-CAR-557].
  - *Tennessee's randomized trial.* Its state pre-K left children worse off by sixth grade [EV-CAR-559].
  - *Head Start.* Its test gains fade, but its long-run effects are positive in most quasi-experimental studies [EV-CAR-560] [EV-CAR-562] [EV-CAR-563].
- **Supply is the binding constraint.**
  - *Canada.* Fees fell fast, but space creation lagged and waitlists grew [EV-CAR-538] [EV-CAR-539] [EV-CAR-540].
  - *New York City.* Its pre-K expansion cut infant slots in poor neighborhoods (one working paper) [EV-CAR-569].
- **Pay keeps staff.** DC's pay equity fund is linked to lower turnover (program evaluation) [EV-CAR-550], and the military's pay-for-training model is the best-known U.S. system [EV-CAR-551].

## 1. Child care

### Quebec: the low flat fee (since 1997)

- **What it did.** It set a flat daily fee ($5, later higher) for every child in regulated care, with no income test, and expanded nonprofit centres (CPEs) and subsidized for-profit and home-based care.
- **Mothers' work: strong and lasting gains.**
  - *Labor supply.* Mothers' labor supply rose, with an elasticity of 0.236 (Baker, Gruber and Milligan, Journal of Political Economy, 2008; difference-in-differences) [EV-CAR-530].
  - *An independent study.* A separate team finds a large, significant effect (Lefebvre and Merrigan, Journal of Labor Economics, 2008) [EV-CAR-532].
- **The fiscal return: disputed.**
  - *Pays for itself.* A Quebec simulation says the program paid for itself: about 70,000 more mothers at work and 1.7% more GDP in 2008 (Fortin, Godbout and St-Cerny, 2012; simulation) [EV-CAR-533].
  - *Pays for most of itself.* The Baker team's 2026 working paper finds lifetime fiscal benefits recapture 75-117 percent of the cost [EV-CAR-534]. That is a large share, but not clearly all.
- **Children: worse outcomes, and a quality problem.**
  - *Short run.* Children were worse off in behavior and health, from aggression to illness [EV-CAR-530].
  - *Long run.* Exposed cohorts later had worse health, lower life satisfaction and more crime: accusations rose 19 percent (Baker, Gruber and Milligan, AEJ: Economic Policy, 2019) [EV-CAR-531].
  - *School readiness.* An independent team found the reform did not improve readiness and may have harmed children from low-income families (Haeck, Lefebvre and Merrigan, Labour Economics, 2015) [EV-CAR-535].
  - *Quality.* Quebec's own 2003 survey rated 53% of preschool-age children's care in nonprofit centres as only "fair", and for-profit centres lower (Institut de la statistique du Québec) [EV-CAR-536].
- **Both views.**
  - *The authors.* They read the results as a warning about universal care's effect on children.
  - *Others.* They read them as a warning about expanding fast into low-quality, often for-profit care, with home care as the comparison for many children. Norway's results (below) support the second reading.
  - *Not settled.* Neither reading is settled. The province-level design cannot separate the program from other Quebec changes.
- **Lesson.** A low flat fee moves mothers into work quickly and is popular. If supply is built faster than quality, children can lose.

### Canada: "$10 a day" (since 2021)

- **What it did.** Up to $27.2 billion over five years from Budget 2021, through agreements with provinces to halve fees and reach $10 a day on average by March 2026 (Department of Finance Canada) [EV-CAR-537].
- **What happened.**
  - *Fees.* Fees fell fast. By March 2024 they averaged about $16.50 a day in federally funded spaces, and 5 provinces and 3 territories had met the $10 target (Auditor General of Canada, 2025) [EV-CAR-538].
  - *Spaces.* Spaces lagged: about 112,000 new spaces after three years against a 250,000 target [EV-CAR-538].
  - *Waitlists.* The share of children under six not in care who were on a waitlist rose from 19.1 percent in 2022 to 30.8 percent in 2025 (C.D. Howe Institute, a critic, from Statistics Canada data) [EV-CAR-539].
  - *A shortfall foreseen.* Canada's Parliamentary Budget Officer had projected in 2022 that the money would fund 182,000 fewer spaces than demand [EV-CAR-540].
- **Mothers' work.** Mothers' participation reached record highs after 2021. The register holds no causal estimate yet: one Canadian research centre's difference-in-differences estimate (about 1-2 points) is noted in "Evidence still needed". The pandemic and remote work confound it.
- **Lesson.** Cutting the price before building supply turns a price problem into a waiting problem. Operators' strain and staff shortages follow when fees are capped and grants do not cover costs.

### Norway and the Nordic systems

- **Children gain when care is good.** Norway's 1975 expansion of subsidized, regulated kindergartens raised children's education and adult employment and cut welfare dependency (Havnes and Mogstad, AEJ: Economic Policy, 2011; difference-in-differences) [EV-CAR-543].
- **But employment barely moved.** The same expansion's 17.85-point rise in coverage raised mothers' employment less than one point, because it mostly replaced informal care (Havnes and Mogstad, Journal of Public Economics, 2011) [EV-CAR-544].
- **Both views.** Quebec and Norway disagree on both outcomes [EV-CAR-530] [EV-CAR-544]. Effects depend on what care replaces, how many mothers already work, and quality.
- **Scale.** Nordic countries spend the most: total spending on education for 3-5-year-olds averages 0.59% of GDP across the OECD and 0.87% in Norway (OECD, 2025) [EV-CAR-545]. On all early care and education, Norway's public spending is about 1.7% of GDP (OECD measure, in the entry's context).
- **The model.** One birth-to-school system with a legal right to a place, capped parent fees and public or regulated private providers. A right to a place works there because supply was built first, over decades.

### U.S. states

- **New Mexico: universal and free, from November 2025.**
  - *What it does.* The first state to make child care assistance free for all families, with no income limit. It is funded mainly by a state trust fund and a share of the Land Grant Permanent Fund, which voters approved in 2022.
  - *Cost.* Its legislature's fiscal staff estimate that if 90 percent of privately paid slots join, the cost almost doubles to $849.7 million a year (New Mexico Legislative Finance Committee, 2025) [EV-CAR-547]. The agency projected lower.
  - *Earlier expansion.* The 2019-22 expansion raised eligibility by 70 percent (Urban Institute, 2024; microsimulation) [EV-CAR-548]. The University of New Mexico's data show license-exempt home providers falling as licensed care grew (context of EV-CAR-548).
  - *Status.* No outcome evaluation exists. It is the best current test of a U.S. universal design, and too new to rely on.
- **Washington: a 7% cap, then a pause.**
  - *Design.* The 2021 Fair Start for Kids Act capped copayments at 7 percent of income and planned eligibility to 75 and then 85 percent of state median income.
  - *Pause.* The 2025 budget delayed the expansions to 2029 and 2031 (Washington Legislature, 2025) [EV-CAR-549].
  - *Lesson.* A state-funded entitlement is exposed to state budget cycles.
- **Vermont: a payroll tax for child care.** Act 76 (2023) widened eligibility and raised provider rates, funded by a payroll tax. Families receiving subsidies rose 55 percent, from 5,059 to 7,851, between 2022 and 2025 (Child Trends, 2026; program data, not causal) [EV-CAR-592]. Infant and toddler care remains hard to find.
- **DC: paying educators like teachers.**
  - *Design.* DC's Early Childhood Educator Pay Equity Fund (2022) set minimum salaries of about $51,000-75,000, against an average of about $37,300 before.
  - *Turnover.* Participating subsidy-serving centers had 37 percent turnover against 51 percent at those without it (Urban Institute, 2025; program evaluation, voluntary participation) [EV-CAR-550].
  - *Funding.* It was cut in FY2025-26 and faces possible elimination (context of EV-CAR-550). A wage fund outside a durable financing stream is fragile.

### The military's system

- **What it is.** After the Military Child Care Act of 1989, DoD built a system with income-based fees, accreditation and pay raises tied to training.
- **Results.**
  - *Quality.* RAND found the accreditation requirement alone led to significant quality improvements (RAND, 1998; program evaluation) [EV-CAR-551].
  - *Satisfaction and limits.* GAO (2023) finds families largely satisfied, but with long waitlists, and notes little research on child outcomes (context of EV-CAR-551).
- **Lesson.** Sliding fees plus pay tied to training can build a quality system. It is an employer system funded by appropriations, so it shows the design works, not that it scales to a civilian market.

## 2. Pre-K

### Tulsa and Boston: high-quality public pre-K in schools

- **Tulsa.** Universal pre-K raised kindergarten-entry scores by 0.38 to 0.79 standard deviations (Gormley and others, Developmental Psychology, 2005; regression discontinuity) [EV-CAR-555]. College enrollment was 12 points higher for pre-K alumni (AERA Open, 2023; propensity weighting, a weaker design) [EV-CAR-556].
- **Boston.**
  - *The lottery result.* Admissions lotteries show public preschool raised college attendance, SAT-taking and high school graduation and cut discipline, including juvenile incarceration, with no detectable effect on state test scores (Gray-Lobe, Pathak and Walters, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2023) [EV-CAR-557].
  - *The program.* It used a set curriculum, coaching and teachers paid on the public school scale.
- **Georgia and Oklahoma.**
  - *Gains.* Universal programs raised lower-income children's scores as late as eighth grade and their mothers' work (Cascio and Schanzenbach, Brookings Papers, 2013; difference-in-differences) [EV-CAR-568].
  - *Who gained.* Among higher-income families, the programs mostly moved children from private to public preschool.

### Tennessee: the warning

- **Results.**
  - *The trial.* Children randomly offered Tennessee's Voluntary Pre-K had lower achievement in third through sixth grades, more disciplinary infractions, worse attendance and more special education (Durkin, Lipsey, Farran and Wiesen, Developmental Psychology, 2022) [EV-CAR-559].
  - *Short run.* Early gains had faded by kindergarten.
- **Why: contested.**
  - *Critics.* NIEER and MDRC researchers point to low measured classroom quality and low spending.
  - *The authors.* They say the program met standard structural benchmarks and point to instruction: too much whole-group drill on letters and numbers, too little rich language and play (context of EV-CAR-559).
- **Both views.** Boston and Tennessee are the two strongest designs and disagree [EV-CAR-557] [EV-CAR-559]. The difference is most plausibly curriculum, coaching and teacher support, not the setting or credentials alone. Tennessee's teachers were certified. That is this dossier's judgment, not a measured finding.

### Head Start

- **The randomized trial.** The Head Start Impact Study found gains across domains at the end of Head Start, but few impacts from kindergarten through third grade (HHS, Obama administration, 2012) [EV-CAR-560].
- **Reanalysis.** About a third of the control group attended other preschools, many publicly funded. Counting that, the benefit-cost ratio is well above one (Kline and Walters, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2016) [EV-CAR-561].
- **Long run.** Sibling comparisons find gains in young-adult outcomes about 80 percent as large as Perry Preschool's (Deming, 2009) [EV-CAR-562]. Head Start's 1965-80 rollout raised schooling by 0.65 years and college completion by 39 percent (Bailey, Sun and Timpe, American Economic Review, 2021) [EV-CAR-563]. These are earlier cohorts, when the alternative was usually no preschool.
- **Lesson.** Test-score fade-out does not mean no effect. Long-run gains appear through behavior and attainment. It is also why benefit-cost models built on test scores are uncertain.

### What pre-K returns, and what it costs

- **Returns.**
  - *Model programs.* An intensive birth-to-five program returns 13.7% a year, a 7.3 benefit-cost ratio (García and others, Journal of Political Economy, 2020; small randomized trials) [EV-CAR-564].
  - *State programs at scale.* State pre-K for low-income children returns $4.78 a dollar in Washington State's meta-analytic model (WSIPP, 2024) [EV-CAR-565]. That model rests on test-score effects, which Tennessee and the Head Start trial show can fade or reverse.
  - *This dossier's reading.* Returns at scale are positive but well below the model programs, and depend on quality.
- **Cost.**
  - *Per child.* High-quality full-day pre-K with pay parity costs about $12,500 a child, $9,368 to $16,221 by state, in 2020 dollars (NIEER) [EV-CAR-566].
  - *A whole system.* A high-quality birth-to-five system for all children costs at least $140 billion a year from all sources, about 0.75% of GDP, with public spending rising $5 billion to $53 billion a year over four phases (National Academies, 2018) [EV-CAR-567].

### The under-3 problem

- **The critique.** The legacy file records it: pre-K without an under-3 expansion "actively worsens infant and toddler care by pulling staff toward the better-paid end" (`legacy/domains/domain-family.md`, FAM-2).
- **The evidence.**
  - *New York City.* Its pre-K expansion reduced private center capacity for children under 2 by about 2,700 seats, all in high-poverty areas (Brown, 2018; working paper, difference-in-differences; another version gives a smaller figure) [EV-CAR-569].
  - *The pay gap.* Public pre-K pays about $53,200 against $29,570 in child care [EV-CAR-521].
  - *What is not measured.* No study found measures staff moving directly.
- **Lesson.** The mechanism is real and the size uncertain. Infant rooms lose money and centers cover it with revenue from older children. Pull the 4-year-olds out, or pay their teachers far more, and infant rooms close unless infant care is funded at its full cost at the same time.

## 3. U.S. law and bills: what was tried

- **The CCDBG Act of 2014.**
  - *What it did.* It brought 12-month eligibility, health and safety rules, background checks and a $1 million asset limit [EV-CAR-585].
  - *What followed.* Children served kept falling without matching money [EV-CAR-586] [EV-CAR-508].
- **The 2024 CCDF rule.**
  - *What it did.* A federal 7% copayment cap, payment by enrollment and in advance, and grants and contracts for some supply.
  - *Its end.* Rescinded from July 2026; the administration cited state waivers and cost [EV-CAR-510].
- **The ARPA stabilization grants.** $23.975 billion of operating grants to providers, ended 2023 [EV-CAR-509]. They were the largest federal test of paying for supply.
- **The Build Back Better Act (House-passed, 2021).**
  - *Design.* Child care with a 7% cap phased up the income scale, and universal pre-K for 3s and 4s, both for six years.
  - *Cost.* CBO's official cost was $381 billion, or $752 billion if made permanent (CBO, via the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget) [EV-CAR-587]. Penn Wharton found about $400 billion as written [EV-CAR-588].
  - *How it died.* Senator Manchin's December 2021 no ended it [EV-CAR-589]. He cited inflation, debt and temporary programs written to look cheap.
  - *Design critiques.* Benefits went to participating, wealthier states and to relatively high-income families (Niskanen Center, 2023) [EV-CAR-590]. Providers faced complex rules. CBO assumed a third or more of children would live in states that did not take part [EV-CAR-587].
- **The Child Care for Working Families Act (2025, H.R. 4418 and S. 2295).**
  - *Child care.* Free at or below 85 percent of state median income, rising to a 7 percent cap above 150 percent. A 90 percent federal share for children under 6 whose parents work, study or look for work [EV-CAR-580].
  - *Wages.* Rates must provide a living wage, equivalent to elementary educators' pay.
  - *Pre-K.* A title for 3s and 4s whose federal share steps from 90 to 60 percent [EV-CAR-581].
  - *Cost.* No CBO score. One estimate puts it near $60 billion a year, method not given (PERI, 2026) [EV-CAR-591].
- **The Transforming Education for the Future Act (H.R. 10421, September 2026).** Free universal preschool at 100 percent federal cost for FY2027-29, then stepping down, with at least 1,020 hours a year [EV-CAR-582]. It is the legacy FAM-2D design.
- **Bipartisan bills.**
  - *Enacted.* The Britt-Kaine bill (S. 847) expanding 45F and the dependent care exclusion was largely enacted in 2025 [EV-CAR-583].
  - *Not enacted.* The PACE Act (H.R. 2900) would make the dependent care credit refundable [EV-CAR-584]. The Child Care Modernization Act would reauthorize the block grant with cost-based rates (not in the register).
- **States.** Colorado's universal preschool (15 hours, nicotine tax; $344 million in FY2024-25) [EV-CAR-593]; New Mexico, Washington, Vermont and DC (above).

## Failures to avoid

1. **Price cuts ahead of supply** (Canada, Quebec): waitlists, rationing by luck and pressure to fill slots with low quality.
2. **Rules without money** (the 2014 reauthorization): fewer children served.
3. **Temporary money** (ARPA, BBB's six-year design, DC's fund): programs cannot hire against money that ends.
4. **Pre-K alone** (New York City; the legacy under-3 critique): infant care shrinks where it is scarcest.
5. **Structural benchmarks without good instruction** (Tennessee): classrooms that meet a checklist can still harm.
6. **A benefit states can decline** (BBB's scoring): families' access depends on their state.
7. **Tax credits that skip families who owe no tax** (the 2025 law) [EV-CAR-503].
