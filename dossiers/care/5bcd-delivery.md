# Care you can count on, parts B-D (long-term care, child care and pre-K, the care workforce): delivery

Role: section 5 of the care dossier for parts B, C and D. It says who runs each provision of `4bcd-specification.md`, what capacity it needs, the timeline from enactment to the endpoints, how people get remedies, what states do, and how it reaches rural places. Status: draft (stage D, board row D-9, PR 1), on `DEC-2026-11-11-02` to `-06`. Dates are the decided ones; where a step's date is a working choice, it says so. Health's delivery (`5-delivery.md`) is the frame: CMS runs the single plan, HHS publishes readiness reports that are counts, not gates, and the endpoint is January 1, 2035.

**Agencies already doing the work.** Every job above runs through a channel that exists: Medicaid's match and assessments, fiscal agents, CCDF's lead agencies, Head Start's grants, CMS's certification surveys and the Payroll-Based Journal. The two new national jobs are the plan's long-term care benefit, inside the plan's own build, and ACF's fallback, which has no precedent in child care and is the delivery piece with the least experience behind it (memo 13 says so).

## 2. Capacity

- **CMS, 2033-35.** The benefit starts on the endpoint, January 1, 2035, the busiest date in health's build (`5-delivery.md`, part 2). The load this adds is about 12 million home care users: today's Medicaid users, about 7.8 million in 2022 [EV-CAR-407] [EV-CAR-408], and about 3.9 million new ones, phased by tier over 2035-36, the nursing homes' care-part claims, and fifty states' fiscal agents. Three things spread it:
  - the assessment runs through states, which already assess Medicaid users, and can adopt the national tool early under the bridge (HLT-9.06);
  - today's Medicaid users move with their current service plans and are reassessed only when their needs change (HLT-9.02);
  - new users enter by tier over two years (HLT-9.07).
- **Readiness reports.** HHS publishes one for the long-term care benefit 18 months before January 1, 2035 (by July 2033), with health's endpoint report: the assessment tool in use, fiscal agents contracted, provider enrollment, claims tested. It is a published count, not a gate; Congress decides any delay (section 8).
- **A child care readiness count.** ACF publishes one by spring 2030 and each spring after: state plans approved, the cost-of-care rate model in use, slots and families waiting by state and age. It is a published count, not a gate; section 8's trigger on the July 2032 cap reads from it. Long-term care's readiness report (above) has a child care counterpart from the start, because the free tier begins within about nine to twelve months of R2.
- **ACF.** ACF's child care office runs a block grant today; under FAM-2 it approves fifty state plans, sets a cost-of-care rate model, runs Head Start's rate rule and stands ready to run the program directly. It needs a staffing plan in 2029-30 (working figure: several hundred more staff, sized with CAP-7S's HHS line in the budget model). Memo 13's caution applies: HHS's capacity was cut in 2025-26, so the fallback is built to work first through tribes and large cities, which already contract with ACF, rather than as a statewide federal program from day one.
- **States.** State Medicaid agencies run the bridge (2030-34) and then shrink to room and board and the state option; their assessment staff move to contract work for the plan. State child care agencies grow: from rationing a block grant to running an entitlement with rates set by a cost model.
- **The workforce is the binding constraint.** Part D sizes the added demand at about 2.8 million recruits on the three-activity line (range 0.9-8.8 million) for long-term care, with child care's and pre-K's hiring peaking in 2031-33 (`3d-design-workforce.md`). Pay comes first because it has the best evidence [EV-CAR-624] [EV-CAR-625]; training (HLT-10.06) and immigration (point 8, for that flagship) fill jobs that pay.

**If the rules reform fails.** Health's fallback moves the plan's money to a later reconciliation act (`5-delivery.md`, part 3). The benefit's money (budgets, the care part of stays, the states' payment) can ride that act; the staffing standard, the assessment's rules and the family-caregiver decision point need 60 votes. The bridge would then be extended a year at a time, in reconciliation, until the benefit passes, so no one loses Medicaid's help meanwhile (working choice).

**If R2 is late.** Child care's dates run from enactment with a latest date: the free tier starts with the first school year at least nine months after enactment, and each later date moves by the same amount (FAM-2.03).

## 4. Remedies

- **Long-term care.** A person's tier, budget and start date are appealable through the plan's appeals and the Beneficiary Ombudsman (HLT-7.20). A reassessment request is answered within 30 days (working figure). Nursing-home residents' room-and-board charges are appealable on the same terms. Before 2035, Medicaid's fair hearings apply as today.
- **The hold-harmless.** A person on Medicaid home care on December 31, 2034 may not be moved below their current level of service by a reassessment; the burden is on the plan.
- **Child care.** A family denied the guarantee or charged above the scale may appeal to the state agency and then to ACF; where HHS runs the program, to ACF directly. Copays are corrected only at the next yearly determination, never reclaimed (FAM-2.06).
- **Workers.** A worker paid below the floor may complain to the state Medicaid agency or the plan, which recovers the shortfall from the provider's rate payments; the 80% report and payroll records are the evidence. Labor-law remedies are the work flagship's.
- **Nursing home residents and staffing.** Staffing shortfalls are survey deficiencies with civil money penalties, as today; Payroll-Based Journal data are published quarterly.
- **States.** A state that disputes its payment (HLT-9.09, FAM-2.08) may seek review before the offset, as under the Part D clawback; the offset proceeds with interest if the dispute fails.

## 5. States' roles

| Period | What states do |
| --- | --- |
| 2030-34 | Run Medicaid home care with the bridge's points; meet the entry conditions and report the measures; pay their share of the floor's added cost at the bridge rate (about $31 billion over 2030-34); run child care and pre-K under state plans, paying 10% then 20% (child care) and 10% then 25% (pre-K) |
| From 2035 | Pay half of their current long-term care spending to the plan (about $115 billion in 2035), on health's formula; assess need under contract; run room-and-board help and the state option below the line in Medicaid; keep running child care and pre-K |
| Always | May set higher wages by boards or bargaining, as allowable costs (HLT-10.04); may decline child care or pre-K, but still owe the share (FAM-2.08) |

**The one instrument.** Every state payment in parts B-D uses health's HLT-7.17 instrument: a direct obligation, collected by offset with interest, untested in court. That is one legal risk, not three (review-milestone F-1's lesson: one mechanism, used everywhere). It is stated in sections 4 and 8. In 2030-34 the bridge's points are new money, and their condition (keeping spending) is the ordinary kind a state may decline.

**What a state gains and gives up.** Long-term care: states give up running home care and nursing-home care, and pay half their current spending; in return the plan takes their share of growth and the new benefit. Child care: states run it and pay a fifth; a state that declines saves nothing (the obligation) and only gives up control of rates and providers.

## 6. Rural delivery

Health's rural plan applies: USDA Rural Development's state offices as a front door (HLT-8.01), global budgets for rural hospitals (HLT-7.11), and the rural test housing uses (`DEC-2026-11-10-02`). For parts B-D:
- **Home care.** Rural home care loses time to travel, so the plan's rates carry a rural add-on for home care hours, on its clinician add-on's model (HLT-7.12; working figure 10%), paid inside the tier budget so rural users are not shortchanged (not priced separately; inside section 6's range). Self-direction and paid relatives matter most where agencies are few.
- **Nursing homes.** Rural nursing homes face the staffing standard with the thinnest labor pools; the shortage-area exemptions (HLT-11.03) are written for them, and USDA's Community Facilities lending (HLT-8.01) already reaches rural nursing homes.
- **Child care.** Rural areas are child care deserts; the facilities fund and the operating grants put areas with few slots first (FAM-2.04, 2.05), and family child care homes are paid on the same terms (FAM-2.09). USDA's Community Facilities program already lends for child care centers; the rural front door points providers to both.
- **Workers.** Training through community colleges and American Job Centers reaches rural counties; the floor matters most where care wages are lowest.

## 7. What delivery leaves open

- *The assessment tool* (HLT-9.06): which national tool, chosen in 2030-31 by CMS from the instruments states use; no decision turns on it now.
- *The rural add-on's size* for home care: a working figure, set by rule with the plan's other add-ons.
- *ACF's staffing for the fallback:* sized in the 2029-30 capacity plan with CAP-7S, not now.
- *The protected allowance and room-and-board rates:* set by rule from cost reports before 2035 (HLT-9.08).
- *Fiscal agents:* one per state is the working choice; a state may use several if each is public and under contract.
