# Care you can count on, part D (the care workforce): the problem

Role: section 1 of the care dossier, part D: who does paid care work, why the jobs pay what they pay, how many more workers the plan's care parts would need, and how the law will stand in January 2029. Status: draft (stage D, step 7). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`); figures marked "project arithmetic" come from `tools/workforce_demand.py`, which labels each input as a register figure or a judgment with its range. Claims still missing evidence are listed at the end and are not made here.

## The subject

Part D covers **the paid care workforce** in four settings, and one group of health workers:
- **Home care:** home health and personal care aides, paid by agencies, by Medicaid self-direction or by families, including relatives paid through Medicaid.
- **Nursing homes and similar residences:** nursing assistants, licensed practical nurses and registered nurses who give direct care, and residential care aides.
- **Child care and early education:** child care workers and teachers in centers, family child care homes, Head Start and pre-K, from birth to school entry.
- **Health workers whose demand the decided plan changes:** registered nurses, physicians (through residency slots) and the clinical workforce the single plan's added use of care draws on (HLT-7).

It does not cover:
- unpaid family care itself, which is part B's (memo 9 pays some of it, and those paid relatives count here as workers);
- K-12 teachers and schools of education, which are education's (EDU-4);
- health insurance and billing workers displaced by the single plan, which health already covers (HLT-7.16);
- visas and legal status, which the immigration flagship owns (shared rule 8): this part names what care needs from it.

Outcomes it serves:
- OUT-03, care people can count on, for every age (care exists only if someone is paid to give it);
- OUT-06, fair pay and real power at work (these are among the lowest-paid jobs in the country);
- OUT-08, time and practical freedom (families can rely on paid care);
- OUT-07, a humane, orderly and lawful immigration system (a third of home care workers are immigrants).

## 1. Who the workers are

**Home care, nursing homes and residential care.**
- **How many.** About 5.4 million direct care workers in 2024, up from about 3.5 million in 2014 [EV-CAR-613]. BLS counts 4.68 million home health and personal care aide jobs [EV-CAR-422] and 1.51 million nursing assistant jobs in 2025 [EV-CAR-609].
- **Pay.** The median direct care wage was $17.36 an hour in 2024, and $16.77 in home care [EV-CAR-613]. Aides' median annual wage is $35,800 [EV-CAR-422]; nursing assistants' is $42,260 [EV-CAR-609].
- **Hardship.** More than a third live in low-income households, and 49 percent rely on public assistance [EV-CAR-613].
- **Immigrants.** 30% of direct care workers are immigrants, against 18% of all workers: 33% in home care, 25% in nursing facilities [EV-CAR-614].
- **Relatives.** Many home care workers are family members paid through Medicaid. All responding states pay family caregivers through at least one program [EV-CAR-493], and California counties estimate 70% of IHSS recipients hire a relative [EV-CAR-494].

**Child care and early education.**
- **How many.** About 1.01 million child care worker jobs and 583,200 preschool teacher jobs in 2025 [EV-CAR-612].
- **Pay.** Child care workers' median wage was $16.82 an hour in May 2025 [EV-CAR-516]. On a wider definition, early educators' median was $13.07 an hour, against $31.80 for elementary and middle school teachers [EV-CAR-629]. Pay rises with the setting's public funding: school-based pre-K about $53,200, Head Start about $35,819, child care about $29,570 (2022) [EV-CAR-521].

**Nurses and physicians.**
- BLS counts 3.47 million registered nurse jobs in 2025 [EV-CAR-610]. HRSA projected a 10 percent shortage of registered nurses in 2026 and 2031, easing to 9 percent by 2036 [EV-CAR-611].

## 2. Why the jobs pay what they pay

- **Public money sets most direct care pay.** Medicaid paid $257.0 billion of the $563.7 billion spent on long-term care in 2023 [EV-CAR-404], and it is the primary payer for 63% of nursing home residents [EV-CAR-438]. A worker's wage is mostly a share of a state's Medicaid rate.
- **The rates are low.** More than half of the 34 states reporting hourly personal care rates paid less than $20 an hour in 2024 [EV-CAR-627]. That rate has to cover the wage, payroll taxes, training, supervision and the agency's overhead.
- **Temporary money bought temporary raises.**
  - The American Rescue Plan's one-year, 10-point home care match increase went mostly into the workforce [EV-CAR-425].
  - Afterwards, states were sustaining only about one-third of the workforce activities, by a CMS official's account [EV-CAR-628]. Thirty states hoped to keep their higher rates, and five planned to end them [EV-CAR-627].
  - Where a legislature made the raise permanent, it held: Colorado raised direct care wages from $12.41 to nearly $18 an hour [EV-CAR-628].
- **Child care pay comes out of what families can pay.** Programs pay staff from fees capped by families' budgets, so wages stay near the minimum; part C prices this (`1c-problem-child-care.md`).
- **Bargaining is rare and constrained.**
  - Where the state is the employer of record for bargaining, as for Washington's individual providers [EV-CAR-620], wages are set publicly. Washington's 2025-27 agreement starts at $22.52 an hour from July 1, 2025 [EV-CAR-621].
  - The Supreme Court held in Harris v. Quinn that Medicaid-funded personal assistants cannot be required to pay agency fees [EV-CAR-623]. That makes bargaining units harder to sustain, though bargaining itself stays lawful.

## 3. What low pay costs the people served

- **Turnover.**
  - In San Francisco, the annual retention rate of new home care providers rose from 39 percent to 74 percent after wage and benefit increases [EV-CAR-625].
  - In DC, child care centers in the pay equity fund had 37 percent turnover, against 51 percent at similar centers without it [EV-CAR-550].
- **Quality and safety.**
  - A ten percent higher minimum wage cut nursing home separations, and Ruffini estimates it would prevent at least 15,000 deaths [EV-CAR-624].
  - Nursing home quality rises with staffing, with no plateau [EV-CAR-606], and registered nurse staffing matters most [EV-CAR-608].
- **Unfilled care.** Part B's waiting lists (almost 650,000 people) [EV-HOU-691] and part C's scarce infant slots are partly a staffing problem: a waiver slot or a subsidy is worth nothing without a worker.

## 4. Nursing home staffing now

- In May 2024, 59 percent of nursing homes met a 3.48 hours-per-resident-day total, 50 percent the 0.55 registered nurse minimum and 30 percent the 2.45 nurse aide minimum [EV-CAR-604].
- Fewer than one in five (19%) met all three at once [EV-CAR-605].
- CMS estimated that its 2024 standards would have raised staffing in more than 79 percent of facilities, required about 16,000 more registered nurses and cost $43.0 billion over 10 years [EV-CAR-601].

## 5. How many more workers the plan would need

**Under current law.** BLS's projections assume no new program:
- home health and personal care aides grow 18.1% to 5.52 million by 2035, with about 760,500 openings a year [EV-CAR-423];
- nursing assistants grow 2.6% [EV-CAR-609];
- registered nurses grow 5.6% [EV-CAR-610];
- child care worker jobs fall 2.0% to about 990,400 [EV-CAR-612].

**What the plan adds** (project arithmetic, `tools/workforce_demand.py`; on parts B and C as Espen decided them, November 11, 2026, with part B at three daily activities, `DEC-2026-11-11-02`; repriced in step 8):

| Source of new demand | Option it depends on | Added workers at maturity (central, low-high) | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Home care for new users, the waiting lists and richer budgets | Part B memo 6 B, from 2035 (decided), at three activities | about 3.2 million (1.0-9.8 million); 4.3 million at two activities | Part B's 2.8-5.6 million new users at about $31,000-83,000 each in 2035 dollars at three activities (5-8 million at $28,000-66,000 at two), waiting lists included [EV-HOU-691], plus about $30 billion of richer budgets for today's users (`3b-design-home-care.md`), at $43-60 an hour of paid care in 2035 dollars and 1,200-1,700 paid hours a worker a year (judgment) |
| ... of whom new recruits, not relatives already caring | Part B memo 9 E (decided); memo 9 A | about 2.3 million (0.6-7.9 million) under 9 E; about 1.9 million under 9 A (two activities: 3.0 and 2.6 million) | 20-40% of the new hours given by relatives already caring, where states allow it now, under 9 E (California counties: 70% of IHSS recipients hire family [EV-CAR-494]); 30-50% under 9 A's national right (Cash and Counseling [EV-CAR-488]); judgment |

| Health care under the single plan | HLT-7 (decided) | about 170,000 registered nurses and 50,000 physicians (100,000-280,000 and 30,000-80,000) | 3-8% more clinical work demanded, read from RAND's gap between supply-constrained and unconstrained spending (1.8% against 9.8%) [EV-CAR-228] and CBO's finding that demand would outrun supply [EV-CAR-219]; judgment |
| Nursing home staffing, if the 2024 standards were restored | Memo 16 (part D) | about 16,000 registered nurses, plus nurse aides | CMS's own estimate [EV-CAR-601] |

- **The total.** On the decided options, the care parts need about 2.8 million more paid care recruits at maturity, on top of BLS's current-law growth (3.6 million at two activities). The range, 0.9-8.8 million, multiplies every input's extreme at once, so its ends are unlikely. The central figure is still well over twice the growth BLS projects for aides [EV-CAR-423]: home care's paid workforce would grow by about two-thirds from today's 4.7 million aide jobs [EV-CAR-422]. Child care's projected decline would also reverse.
- **When.** Most of the home care demand starts in 2035, with the benefit. Child care and pre-K hiring peaks in 2031-33 (part C, memo 12).
- **What is most uncertain.** The home care figure, because part B's new-user count is the largest uncertainty in part B's cost and because how many hours relatives would take is unknown. Part B's revised figures (#244) nearly tripled this line: more new users, each priced at Medicaid's own average or above. The three-activity line (step 8) lowers it by about a quarter. A range from about 0.6 million to 7.9 million home care recruits is wide enough that part D's design must work at either end.
- **Health is smaller but harder.** Nurses and physicians take years to train. The single plan's copays above 200% of poverty (HLT-7.06) damp the extra use, and the 2035 endpoint gives time (`3-design.md`, memo 4).

## 6. The 2029 baseline under current law

How the law will stand in January 2029, counting enacted laws with later effective dates and pending agency actions (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`):

| Item | Status in 2029 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The 80% worker-pay rule** for Medicaid homemaker, home health aide and personal care services | In regulation, compliance from July 2030 (six years after the 2024 rule took effect). States may set a separate level for small providers and exempt some providers. CMS has said it will propose to rescind or revise the 2024 rules; no proposal had been published by November 11, 2026. A rescission before 2029 is likely, so the plan cannot count on the rule | [EV-CAR-419] [EV-CAR-636] [EV-CAR-420] |
| The access rule's rate advisory group for direct care workers | CMS has delayed enforcement | [EV-CAR-615] |
| **Nursing home minimum staffing** (3.48 hours per resident day, 24/7 registered nurse) | Gone three ways: vacated by two district courts in 2025, barred by the 2025 law until September 30, 2034, and repealed by CMS's interim final rule from February 2, 2026. The facility assessment rule stays | [EV-CAR-602] [EV-CAR-416] [EV-CAR-603] |
| **Home care workers' minimum wage and overtime** | The Labor Department proposed in July 2025 to restore the 1975 exemptions for third-party employers; no final rule by November 2026. If finalized, agency-employed home care workers could lose federal minimum wage and overtime rights | [EV-CAR-616] |
| **Medicaid financing** | The 2025 law limits states' ability to finance Medicaid through provider taxes from 2028; CBO expects no more states to expand Medicaid as a result. The same limit bears on what states can pay in rates (judgment) | [EV-CAR-024] |
| **Immigration enforcement** | Intensified enforcement may reduce the care workforce. Past enforcement (Secure Communities) cut nursing home direct care hours by about 2% | [EV-CAR-615] [EV-CAR-635] |
| **Child care** | HHS rescinded the 2024 CCDF rule's payment protections from July 13, 2026 | [EV-CAR-510] |
| **State wage standards** (in force, at state expense with Medicaid's federal match) | Minnesota's nursing home board: $22.50 an hour for certified nursing assistants from September 10, 2026, plus $1.50 on January 1, 2027. California: $25 for large health systems from 7/1/26, with home health agencies covered and free-standing nursing homes not yet. Washington: state bargaining for individual providers. Nevada's board recommended a $25 Medicaid rate and a $15 wage floor | [EV-CAR-618] [EV-CAR-619] [EV-CAR-620] [EV-CAR-622] |
| **DC's early educator pay supplement** | Funded for FY2027 ($60 million restored), not after | [EV-CAR-630] |
| **Federal bills, none enacted** | Better Care Better Jobs (S. 100, 118th Congress): a 10-point home care match on conditions including pass-through of rate increases to workers. The HCBS Access Act (2026). The Child Care for Working Families Act's parity rule. The residency bill's 14,000 slots | [EV-CAR-617] [EV-CAR-474] [EV-CAR-581] [EV-CAR-634] |
| **Health's decided plan** | The single plan by January 1, 2035; long-term care stays in Medicaid until part B decides (HLT-7.07) | `4-specification.md` |

So in 2029 the federal government sets **no wage standard and no staffing standard** for any care setting. A few states have built their own, and the most protective federal rule (the 80% pass-through) is likely gone before it takes effect.
