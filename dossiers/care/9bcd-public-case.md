# Care you can count on, parts B-D (long-term care, child care and pre-K, the care workforce): the public case

Role: section 9 of the care dossier for parts B, C and D: a plain-language brief, messages, hard questions and a can-say list, built only from sections 1-8 and never ahead of them. Status: draft (stage D, board row D-9, PR 2), on `DEC-2026-11-11-02` to `-06`. Every number traces to its section and evidence ID in the source table at the end. Sources are named in plain text before each tag. Project estimates say "on our estimates" or "about". Each promise is dated from when its provision starts.

## The brief

**Care you can count on, from the first year of life to the last.**

**The problem.** A person turning 65 today will need about $137,800 of paid long-term care on average, and families pay almost 59% of it themselves; almost two in ten spend $100,000 or more (ASPE) [EV-CAR-401]. Medicaid helps only after people spend down to $2,000 in savings [EV-CAR-409], and almost 650,000 people wait an average of 34 months for its help at home (KFF) [EV-HOU-691]. Child care averaged $13,184 a year in 2025 (Child Care Aware) [EV-CAR-515], and federal help reaches about 16% of the children who qualify for it [EV-CAR-507]. The people who do this work earn a median of about $16.77 an hour in home care [EV-CAR-613] and $16.82 in child care [EV-CAR-516].

**The plan.**
- *Long-term care.* From January 2035, help at home and the care part of nursing-home stays become part of the single public health plan, for anyone of any age who needs help with three daily activities or has a serious cognitive impairment. There is no asset test and no estate recovery. Everyone already getting Medicaid help at home keeps it. Before then, a five-year bridge from 2030 gives states more money for home care, tied to shorter waits.
- *Child care and pre-K.* From the 2030-31 school year, child care is free for families at or below 85% of their state's median income, and pre-K is free for 4-year-olds. From July 2032 no family pays more than 7% of its income for child care, with no income limit. By 2033-34 pre-K is free for every 3-year-old too.
- *Care workers.* From January 2030, home care and nursing home workers paid with public money earn at least $20 an hour in 2026 dollars, rising with wages; child care and pre-K staff get the same floor from the 2030-31 school year. Early educators reach pay parity with elementary teachers by the 2036-37 school year. Nursing homes have a registered nurse on site around the clock from 2031.

**What it costs.** On our estimates, about $3.5 trillion over fiscal years 2030-39 and about $730 billion in 2040, about 1.3% of the economy. About $580 billion of that 2040 figure is new beyond what the plan's budget already counts (section 6). Most of it replaces what families now pay themselves or give in unpaid care. States keep paying a share: half of what they spend on long-term care today, and a fifth of child care's cost after three years. How the rest is paid is decided with the plan's other taxes.

**What it protects.** From 2035, no one spends down their savings or loses their home to get long-term care, at home or in a nursing home. No family pays more than 7% of its income for child care. Relatives can still be paid for care wherever their state allows it today.

## The case in one paragraph

Families carry the cost of care alone until they are poor enough for Medicaid. This plan changes that at both ends of life. From 2035, long-term care is part of the single public health plan, with no asset test, for anyone who needs real help with daily life. From 2030, child care becomes free for families at or below 85% of their state's median income, about half of families with young children on our estimates, and from 2032 it is capped at 7% of income for everyone else, and pre-K is free. The workers who give this care are paid at least $20 an hour. On our estimates it costs about $730 billion a year by 2040, much of it money families already spend. It is paid for with the plan's other taxes, and states keep paying their share.

## Messages

- **No more spending down to get care.** From 2035, long-term care comes with no asset test and no estate recovery.
- **Stay home if you can.** The top tier of help at home is funded at the same level as the care in a nursing home, so home is never the worse-funded choice.
- **Child care at 7% of income, at most.** That cap starts in July 2032. From 2030-31, care is free at or below 85% of state median income.
- **Free pre-K for 3s and 4s.** It is free in schools, Head Start, centers and family child care, all at one rate.
- **Pay the people who care.** A $20 floor for care workers comes first, because pay is what keeps workers in the job.
- **States pay their share.** They pay half of what they spend on long-term care today, and declining the child care program saves a state nothing.

## Hard questions, plain answers

**After Medicare for All, can we afford this too?** It is a large commitment: on our estimates about $730 billion in 2040, about 1.3% of GDP, and about $580 billion of that beyond what the budget already counts. The cost was cut by about a fifth before anything was promised, and every protection was kept. Most of it is not new spending in the economy. It replaces what families now pay out of pocket for nursing homes, aides and day care, and the unpaid care relatives give, worth about as much again as paid care (ASPE) [EV-CAR-436]. Whether the country can carry it is settled for the plan as a whole, with health's taxes, not for this part alone. The tax design is not finished, and we don't claim families come out ahead until it is.

**Will you pay my daughter to take care of me?** Where your state allows it today, yes. Most states allow paying relatives in at least one program (44 do in at least one waiver [EV-CAR-439]), and the plan keeps those rules when the benefit starts in 2035. Relatives are paid at least the $20 floor, through a public payroll agent. A national study, randomized where states agree, reports by 2034 on paying spouses and parents everywhere. The plan's administrator can then extend it to every state.

**What if I need help with only two daily activities?** The benefit starts at three, or at a serious cognitive impairment. Anyone already getting Medicaid help at home on December 31, 2034 keeps it. States may keep serving people below the line in Medicaid.

**Will my parent lose their home to pay for a nursing home?** No. From 2035 the plan pays for the care, with no asset test. Residents pay room and board from their income and keep a protected allowance; if their income falls short, Medicaid pays the rest with no savings test, and a spouse at home keeps the income they need. Before then, from 2030, estate recovery ends and Medicaid's savings limit rises to $10,000 for a single person.

**Why not free child care for everyone?** Free for every family would cost more and would mostly help families who can already pay. This design is free at or below 85% of state median income, about half of families with young children on our estimates. It caps everyone else at 7% of income, so no family faces a cliff.

**Why should parents who don't work get child care?** Most families with young children have every parent working [EV-CAR-518]. A work test mostly catches parents looking for work or in school, and it adds paperwork every time a job changes.

**Won't care workers be hard to find?** Yes, this is the hardest part. Pay comes first because it has the best evidence for keeping workers. New users of help at home are phased in over 2035-36, the heaviest needs first. Free training starts before the hiring peaks.

**What if a state refuses?** Families are covered either way. For long-term care, the plan pays everyone. For child care and pre-K, the federal government runs the program in that state, and the state still owes its share. That rule has not been tested in court.

## Stories (composite illustrations, not real people)

- *Ruth, 82, with dementia,* lives with her son. Today she would have to spend her savings down to $2,000 before Medicaid helped. From 2035 an assessment at home sets her budget for an aide, and her son can be paid if their state allows it now.
- *A family with a toddler,* earning 80% of their state's median income. Today they pay about the national average price for care. From the 2030-31 school year their care is free, because they are below 85% of the median.
- *Maria, a home care aide* paid $16.77 an hour. From January 2030 she earns at least $20, in 2026 dollars, rising with wages.

## Can say / can't say

| Can say | Can't say |
| --- | --- |
| "No asset test for long-term care from 2035" | "Free long-term care": there are capped copays above 200% of poverty, and residents pay room and board from income |
| "Anyone already getting Medicaid help at home keeps it" | "No one loses help": people who would have qualified for Medicaid later at two activities are covered only where their state keeps that option |
| "Child care costs no more than 7% of income from July 2032" | "Free child care for all": it is free at or below 85% of state median income |
| "On our estimates, about $730 billion a year by 2040, about 1.3% of GDP; about $580 billion beyond what the budget already counts" | "It pays for itself" or "no new taxes"; or the cost without "on our estimates" |
| "Most of the cost replaces what families now pay or give in unpaid care" | "Families come out ahead" until the taxes are set |
| "Home care and nursing home workers paid with public money earn at least $20 an hour in 2026 dollars from January 2030; child care and pre-K staff from the 2030-31 school year" | "A $20 minimum wage": the floor covers publicly paid care only |
| "Relatives can be paid wherever their state allows it today; a national decision follows a study" | "Pay any family caregiver, anywhere" |
| "States pay half their current long-term care spending" | "States are off the hook" or "the federal government takes it all" |
| "A registered nurse on site around the clock from 2031" | "Safe staffing in every nursing home from 2030": the full standard starts in 2035 |
