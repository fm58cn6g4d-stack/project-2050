# Memo 6: How care at home is guaranteed

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 6 of the care dossier (part B, care at home for all ages; `../3b-design-home-care.md`). Status: **for Espen, not decided.** Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Costs marked "judgment" are this dossier's working assumptions, not official scores; their basis is in `../3b-design-home-care.md`, "What the options cost".

**This memo decides who is guaranteed help with daily living at home, and through which program.** Memos 7-9 then decide the benefit's scope, nursing homes and family caregivers under whichever option you choose.

## The question

Health's path, which you decided, covers every resident's medical care by January 1, 2035, but leaves long-term care in Medicaid until this part decides (`../4-specification.md`, HLT-7.07). Three ways to guarantee care at home are on the table:
- a stronger Medicaid;
- a federal home and community care benefit in the single plan;
- public insurance against long, catastrophic needs only.

Your October 26 direction asked for the second, compared against the other two (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, line 137). That direction is a working choice, weighed here as an option (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`).

## Why it matters

- **Most people will need help, and no program insures it.** 56% of people turning 65 will need long-term care, and 22% for more than five years. The average paid cost is $137,800, and families pay almost 59% of it (ASPE, 2022) [EV-CAR-400] [EV-CAR-401]. Medicare does not pay for daily personal care (Medicare.gov) [EV-CAR-412].
- **Medicaid helps only after people are poor, and then with waiting lists.** Its asset limit is $2,000 in most states (CRS, 2017) [EV-CAR-409]. Almost 650,000 people wait 34 months on average for home care (KFF, 2026) [EV-HOU-691].
- **About half of the people Medicaid serves at home are under 65** (MACPAC, 2025) [EV-CAR-408], so this is an all-age question.
- **Families carry it.** 63 million family caregivers average 27 hours a week (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025) [EV-CAR-434].

## Options

**A. A stronger Medicaid (the legacy HLT-4 route, made permanent).**
- **What it does.**
  - A permanent 10-point increase in the federal match for Medicaid home care.
  - States must serve everyone who is eligible, which ends waiting lists.
  - Medicaid's resource limits rise to $4,100 and $6,200 and are indexed (S. 1506's levels) [EV-CAR-431].
  - Estate recovery ends.
  - Self-direction and paid family care are available in every state.
  - Medicaid stays the program for long-term care after 2035, with its income and asset tests.
- *For people:* a woman with multiple sclerosis who qualifies for Medicaid gets home care without waiting three years. A retired machinist with dementia and $60,000 in savings still spends it down before he qualifies.
- *Cost:* about $45 billion a year more federal money in 2035 (range $35-70 billion), about $60 billion in FY2040 (judgment). That is against current law. Health's booking already moves Medicaid's home care into the plan from 2033-35, so on that basis A's match increase counts only until then. CBO put a 2021 home-care match increase at about $150 billion over ten years (KFF, 2021) [EV-CAR-447], the same scale.
- *Risk:* states still decide much of the benefit, and they bear the cost of serving everyone. Community First Choice's 6-point bonus drew only 9 states (MACPAC, 2023) [EV-CAR-426]. A requirement to serve everyone eligible is a mandate states may resist or litigate.
- *Burden:* Medicaid's income and asset tests, and documents to prove them, stay.

**B. An all-age home and community care benefit in the single plan, from January 1, 2035 (recommended).**
- **What it does.**
  - Everyone the plan covers, of any age, who meets the need threshold (memo 7) gets home and community care: personal care, homemaker help, adult day services, respite, home changes, assistive technology, habilitation, and tenancy and housing-related supports (point 5). This is S. 1506's home-care list (sec. 201) [EV-CAR-276].
  - There is no income or asset test and no estate recovery. Memo 4 C's capped copays above 200% of poverty apply, as for all plan care.
  - Medicaid's home-care users move into the benefit on that date, with no break in services, and a five-year Medicaid bridge prepares the ground (point 1).
  - States keep paying half of what they now spend on home care (working figure; maintenance of effort, as in HLT-7.17).
- *For people:* the same retired machinist gets an assessment at home and a set monthly budget for an aide and adult day care, without spending down. His daughter can be paid as his aide (memo 9). A family with an autistic teenager gets respite and habilitation without a waiting list.
- *Cost:* about $215 billion a year of new federal money in 2035 (range $125-370 billion), about $280 billion in FY2040 (range $165-485 billion) (judgment), measured against current law with long-term care in Medicaid.
  - *Against health's booking it is about $142 billion* (range $94-223 billion). The budget model's booking of health already prices today's Medicaid long-term care into the plan, with half of states' share netted, so B's relief of states (about $73 billion) is already counted. Section 6 settles the basis (`../3b-design-home-care.md`, "How they relate to health's booking"). About $165 billion of federal Medicaid home-care money moves in as a transfer and is not counted. The largest piece is people who now rely on family care alone and would use paid care. Nearly two-thirds of older adults who get help get only unpaid help (Urban Institute, 2020) [EV-CAR-448]. Urban and CBO both expect a single-payer long-term care benefit to add "hundreds of billions of dollars" a year [EV-CAR-448]. RAND's Medicare for All estimate with long-term care raised national spending too (RAND, 2019) [EV-CAR-228]. Most of the cost replaces what families now pay or give unpaid [EV-CAR-401] [EV-CAR-436]. Part D's wage floor would add about $55 billion if it raises pay 20%.
- *Risk:* the workforce. BLS already projects about 760.5 thousand openings a year for aides (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-423]. A universal benefit raises demand further, and part D must size supply. Expansion adds total spending, and the savings from people diverted from nursing homes are real but smaller (Kaye and others, 2009; Grabowski, 2006) [EV-CAR-449] [EV-CAR-451].
- *Burden:* one assessment visit at home (point 8), no financial forms.

**C. Public catastrophic insurance after a waiting period, with Medicaid for the front end.**
- **What it does.** A federal benefit pays for care once someone has needed help for a set period, say two years, and then for as long as the need lasts. Before that, people rely on their own money, families, private insurance or Medicaid (with option A's improvements if you add them).
- *For people:* a person with advanced dementia is protected after two years. Most people, whose needs last less than that, get nothing new: ASPE expects many to need care for less than three years [EV-CAR-400].
- *Cost:* about $100-200 billion a year in 2035 (judgment). Urban's modeling finds a front-end and a catastrophic benefit cost about the same, but the catastrophic one displaces more Medicaid spending: 38 percent against 15 percent (Urban Institute, 2016) [EV-CAR-446]. That is one research team's model.
- *Risk:* it leaves the waiting lists and the spend-down in place for the first years of need, when families are deciding whether care at home can work. A waiting period is a rule people must track and prove.
- *Burden:* records of when need began, then a second determination.

## Recommendation: B

- **It is the option that insures everyone.** A cannot do that: Medicaid helps only after spend-down, and states decide the rest. C leaves most needs uncovered.
- **It fits the plan you chose.** It uses one card, one plan and one date, January 1, 2035. It builds on S. 1506's split: home care federal, institutional care decided separately (memo 8) [EV-CAR-278].
- **It meets each test your October 26 direction set.** It protects against long intensive needs, gives assessed help without spend-down, offers a genuine paid family-caregiver option and keeps an outside-care option.
- **It follows the design lens.** One assessment and no asset test, where today people prove poverty to a state.
- **The cost is large and real.** It is reported, not trimmed (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`). Most of it moves spending families already bear onto the public side, where peer countries put four-fifths of long-term care spending (OECD, 2025) [EV-CAR-444].
- **If you want a smaller first step,** A's permanent Medicaid changes are the fallback. They reach the people waiting today at about a fifth of B's cost against current law, but not the middle-income families B is mainly for.

## If you decide

  - *Under A:* amendments to Medicaid, mostly in reconciliation.
  - *Under C:* a new insurance title.
