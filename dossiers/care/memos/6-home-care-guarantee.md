# Memo 6: How long-term care at home is guaranteed

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 6 of the care dossier (part B, long-term care: support at home and in nursing homes for older and disabled people; `../3b-design-home-care.md`). Status: **for Espen, not decided**; revised after the independent audit (`../review-part-b-evidence.md`). Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Costs marked "judgment" are this dossier's working assumptions, not official scores; their basis is in `../3b-design-home-care.md`, "What the options cost".

**What changed after the audit.** The recommendation is still B. **Its cost is now about $400 billion a year in 2035 (range $250-750 billion), not about $215 billion.** The audit found the old figure well below the one independent model of a similar design (Urban, 2020) [EV-CAR-483]; CBO's December 2020 working paper, now in the register, points the same way [EV-CAR-452]. The rebuilt figure counts more new users, prices them closer to Medicaid's and Urban's averages, and adds richer budgets for today's users. A now costs about a ninth of B, not a fifth. Two corrections: tenancy supports are part B's addition, not S. 1506's list; and the claim that outside estimates were "consistent" is replaced by the benchmark table below.

**This memo decides who is guaranteed help with daily living at home, and through which program.** Memos 7-9 then decide the benefit's scope, nursing homes and family caregivers under whichever option you choose.

## The question

Health's path, which you decided, covers every resident's medical care by January 1, 2035, but leaves long-term care in Medicaid until this part decides (`../4-specification.md`, HLT-7.07). Three ways to guarantee care at home are on the table:
- a stronger Medicaid;
- a federal home and community care benefit in the single plan;
- public insurance against long, catastrophic needs only.

Your October 26 direction asked for the second, compared against the other two (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, line 137). That direction is a working choice, weighed here as an option (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`).

## Why it matters

- **Most people will need help, and no program insures it.** 56% of people turning 65 will need long-term care, and 22% for more than five years. The average paid cost is $137,800, and families pay almost 59% of it (ASPE, 2022) [EV-CAR-400] [EV-CAR-401]. Medicare does not pay for daily personal care (Medicare.gov) [EV-CAR-412].
- **Medicaid helps only after people are poor, and then with waiting lists.** Its asset limit is $2,000 in most states (CRS, 2017) [EV-CAR-409]. Almost 650,000 people wait 34 months on average for home care (KFF, 2026) [EV-HOU-691].
- **About half of the people Medicaid serves at home are under 65** (MACPAC, 2025) [EV-CAR-408], so this is a question for every age.
- **Families carry it.** 63 million family caregivers average 27 hours a week (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025) [EV-CAR-434].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Universal public long-term care exists and lasts abroad** (international practice): Germany since 1995, Japan since 2000, broad public systems in the Netherlands and the Nordic countries, where public money pays about four-fifths of long-term care (OECD, 2025) [EV-CAR-440] [EV-CAR-442] [EV-CAR-444].
2. **Voluntary insurance fails** (program history): CLASS found no sustainable design and was repealed (HHS, 2011; CRS, 2013) [EV-CAR-476] [EV-CAR-424]. Every option here is universal or mandatory.
3. **A universal benefit costs hundreds of billions a year** (modeling): Urban's model of a design close to B adds $355 billion of federal spending in 2020 [EV-CAR-483]; CBO's broader option adds $599 billion in 2030, all of the new demand for care at home [EV-CAR-452].
4. **Home care saves money only for people diverted from institutions** (observational studies, split): state comparisons find slower spending growth with mature home-care programs (Kaye and others, 2009) [EV-CAR-449]; a review finds new home-care models raise total cost while improving welfare (Grabowski, 2006) [EV-CAR-451].
5. **Medicaid match increases work at the margin** (program evaluations): ARPA's one-year 10 points went mostly to workers [EV-CAR-425]; Community First Choice's 6 points drew 9 states [EV-CAR-426]; the Balancing Incentive Program raised home care's share by about 5 points [EV-CAR-480].

## Options

**A. A stronger Medicaid (the legacy HLT-4 route, made permanent).**
- **What it does.**
  - A permanent 10-point increase in the federal match for Medicaid home care.
  - States must serve everyone who is eligible, which ends waiting lists.
  - Medicaid's resource limits rise and are indexed (point 4).
  - Estate recovery ends.
  - Self-direction and paid family care are available in every state.
  - Medicaid stays the program for long-term care after 2035, with its income and asset tests.
- *Where tried:* ARPA sec. 9817, Community First Choice, the Balancing Incentive Program [EV-CAR-425] [EV-CAR-426] [EV-CAR-479]. The HCBS Access Act (2026) is its strongest current form [EV-CAR-474].
- *For people:* a woman with multiple sclerosis who qualifies for Medicaid gets home care without waiting three years. A retired machinist with dementia and $60,000 in savings still spends it down before he qualifies.
- *Cost:* about $45 billion a year more federal money in 2035 (range $35-70 billion), about $60 billion in FY2040 (judgment).
  - *Benchmark:* CBO put Build Back Better's home-care match increase with conditions at about $150 billion over ten years (KFF, 2021) [EV-CAR-447]. Scaled to 10 points and 2035, that is about $35 billion a year, in line with A's match piece of about $31 billion (project arithmetic).
- *Evidence strength:* program evaluations; modest, partly temporary effects.
- *Political risk:* low among Democrats, who backed Better Care Better Jobs [EV-CAR-473]. A requirement to serve everyone eligible is a mandate states may resist or litigate.
- *Burden:* Medicaid's income and asset tests, and documents to prove them, stay.

**B. A home and community care benefit in the single plan, for every age, from January 1, 2035 (recommended).**
- **What it does.**
  - Everyone the plan covers, of any age, who meets the need threshold (memo 7) gets home and community care: personal care, homemaker help, adult day services, respite, home changes, assistive technology and habilitation, the Medicaid state-plan and waiver services S. 1506 lists (sec. 201) [EV-CAR-276]. Part B adds tenancy and housing-related supports (point 5); they are not on S. 1506's list.
  - There is no income or asset test and no estate recovery. Memo 4 C's capped copays above 200% of poverty apply, as for all plan care.
  - Medicaid's home-care users move into the benefit on that date, with no break in services (memo 7's hold-harmless), and a five-year Medicaid bridge prepares the ground (point 1).
  - States keep paying half of what they now spend on home care (working figure; maintenance of effort, as in HLT-7.17).
- *Where tried:* Germany, Japan and the Netherlands nationally; WA Cares as a small state program since July 2026 [EV-CAR-445]. No US federal program.
- *For people:* the same retired machinist gets an assessment at home and a set monthly budget for an aide and adult day care, without spending down. A family with an autistic teenager gets respite and habilitation without a waiting list.
- *Cost:* **about $400 billion a year of new federal money in 2035 (range $250-750 billion); about $520 billion in FY2040 (range $325-980 billion)**; about $2.2 trillion over 2035-39 (judgment), against current law, which health's booking matches for long-term care (board row D-4c).
  - *Pieces:* relief of states' home-care share net of their payment, about $73 billion; waiting lists served, about $22 billion; richer budgets for today's users, about $30 billion; new users, about $275 billion (6.5 million people at about $42,000 each in 2035 dollars, range 5-8 million at $28,000-66,000). About $165 billion of federal Medicaid home-care money moves in as a transfer and is not counted.
  - *Benchmarks (table below):* Urban's model scaled to this design gives about $500-740 billion; CBO's broader option, adjusted to the two-activity threshold and with institutions removed, gives about $350-400 billion.
  - *Who pays now:* most of it replaces what families pay out of pocket or give unpaid [EV-CAR-401] [EV-CAR-436]. Part D's wage floor would sit on top.
- *Evidence strength:* international practice for the direction (strong for feasibility); two models for the cost, which differ by about a factor of two; no US trial.
- *Political risk:*
  - *Scale:* the largest new spending in part B, carried by Medicare for All's financing.
  - *States:* they lose a program they shape, though they gain relief.
  - *Workforce:* BLS projects about 760.5 thousand aide openings a year already (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-423]; part D must supply more.
  - *CLASS:* opponents cite it against any federal long-term care plan, but its lesson, that voluntary enrollment fails, supports a universal design [EV-CAR-476] [EV-CAR-477].
- *Burden:* one assessment visit at home (point 8), no financial forms.

**C. Public catastrophic insurance after a waiting period, with Medicaid for the front end (the WISH Act's route).**
- **What it does.** A federal benefit pays for care once someone has needed help for a set period, say two years, and then for as long as the need lasts. Before that, people rely on their own money, families, private insurance or Medicaid (with option A's improvements if you add them).
- *Where tried:* not in the US; the WISH Act (H.R. 2082, bipartisan) proposes a 65-and-over cash version through Social Security [EV-CAR-472].
- *For people:* a person with advanced dementia is protected after two years. Most people, whose needs last less than that, get nothing new: ASPE expects many to need care for less than three years [EV-CAR-400].
- *Cost:* about $150-250 billion a year in 2035, about $195-325 billion in FY2040 (judgment).
  - *Benchmark:* two actuarial teams put the WISH Act at a 1.7-2.2% payroll tax over 75 years, with no credit for Medicaid savings (Milliman for ASPE, 2025; Oliver Wyman, GCG and ARC, 2026, funded by two insurers) [EV-CAR-454] [EV-CAR-455], reported as about $200 billion at 1.7% [EV-CAR-456]. Both are well above the 0.6% the bill names. C covers all ages, which raises it.
  - Urban's modeling finds a front-end and a catastrophic benefit cost about the same, but the catastrophic one displaces more Medicaid spending: 38 percent against 15 percent (Urban Institute, 2016) [EV-CAR-446].
- *Evidence strength:* actuarial modeling only.
- *Political risk:* the only bipartisan social-insurance route. It leaves the waiting lists and the spend-down in place for the first years of need, when families are deciding whether care at home can work.
- *Burden:* records of when need began, then a second determination.

**Fiscal rule** (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`): this choice is mainly about money and whom it reaches, so it comes to you with its cost. The cost is reported to the financing settlement; nothing is trimmed here.

## Recommendation: B (unchanged, with its cost about doubled)

- **It is the option that insures everyone.** A cannot do that: Medicaid helps only after spend-down, and states decide the rest. C leaves most needs uncovered.
- **It fits the plan you chose.** One card, one plan and one date, January 1, 2035. It builds on S. 1506's split: home care federal, institutional care decided separately (memo 8) [EV-CAR-278].
- **It meets each test your October 26 direction set.** It protects against long intensive needs, gives assessed help without spend-down, keeps a paid family-caregiver option (memo 9) and an outside-care option.
- **It follows the design lens.** One assessment and no asset test, where today people prove poverty to a state.
- **Its evidence is international practice, not a US trial.** Germany and Japan show a universal system can be run and sustained; the US models agree it costs hundreds of billions a year.
- **The cost is large, uncertain and real.** It is about $400 billion in 2035 on the project's rebuilt estimate and could be $500-750 billion if Urban's model is right. Most of it moves spending families already bear onto the public side, where peer countries put four-fifths of long-term care spending [EV-CAR-444].
- **Why not change the recommendation on cost.** The audit's finding changes how much B costs, not which option reaches the most people, and the fiscal rule reports costs rather than trimming designs. If the financing settlement cannot carry B, A's permanent Medicaid changes are the fallback. They reach the people waiting today at about a ninth of B's cost, but not the middle-income families B is mainly for.

**What would change this:** a financing settlement that cannot carry B even at its central cost (toward A, with B later); a US evaluation showing take-up far above the models (toward tighter tiers in memo 7); a bipartisan path for WISH that B would block (toward C as a first step).

## If you decide

- **Under B:** section 4 writes the benefit as a new title of the Medicare for All Act of 2029, effective January 1, 2035, with the bridge (point 1) in the 2029 reconciliation act. HLT-7.07 changes so that home and community care moves to the plan in 2035. The financing settlement plans on about $400 billion in 2035 and $520 billion in FY2040, with the top of the range visible. Part D's wage floor is priced on the larger base.
- **Under A:** amendments to Medicaid, mostly in reconciliation. Points 1-4 become permanent. About $45 billion a year.
- **Under C:** a new insurance title, priced on the WISH actuarial work, with A's Medicaid changes for the front end if you add them.
- **The agenda.** HLT-9 (`option`) carries B; HLT-4 keeps the bridge and Medicaid's remaining role.
