# Memo 11: Pre-K for 3- and 4-year-olds

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 11 of the care dossier (part C; `../3c-design-child-care.md`). Status: **for Espen, not decided.** Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions; their basis is in `../3c-design-child-care.md`, "What the options cost".

**This memo decides whether the plan offers free pre-K, to whom, and how it relates to child care.** It assumes memo 10 decides child care for children under 5. Which dossier holds pre-K is a seam proposed in section 3 (care holds it; education holds K-12 and the transition).

## The question

State pre-K reaches 37% of 4-year-olds and 9% of 3-year-olds, at about $8,124 a child of state money (NIEER, 2026) [EV-CAR-519]. Head Start serves about 716,000 more children and pregnant women [EV-CAR-512]. The legacy design is universal voluntary pre-K for 3s and 4s from 2030-31 (FAM-2A), with the Bonamici standards as an alternative (FAM-2D). The standards are 1,020 hours, K-12 pay parity, a lead teacher's degree within seven years, and a federal share stepping from 100% to 60% (H.R. 10421, 2026) [EV-CAR-582]. Both are working choices weighed here.

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Good school-based pre-K changes lives.** Boston's lotteries show public preschool raised college-going and graduation and cut juvenile incarceration, though test-score effects faded (Gray-Lobe, Pathak and Walters, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2023; lottery) [EV-CAR-557]. Tulsa raised entry scores by up to 0.79 standard deviations (Gormley and others, 2005; regression discontinuity) [EV-CAR-555].
2. **Not every program does.** Tennessee's state pre-K left children behind by sixth grade (Durkin and others, Developmental Psychology, 2022; randomized trial) [EV-CAR-559].
3. **Head Start's test gains fade** (Head Start Impact Study, 2012; randomized) [EV-CAR-560]. Its long-run effects are positive (Deming, 2009; Bailey, Sun and Timpe, 2021) [EV-CAR-562] [EV-CAR-563].
4. **Universal programs help lower-income children most.** Higher-income families mostly switch from private to public preschool (Cascio and Schanzenbach, 2013) [EV-CAR-568].
5. **Pre-K alone can shrink infant care.** New York City's pre-K cut center slots for children under 2 in poor neighborhoods (Brown, 2018; one working paper) [EV-CAR-569]. Public pre-K pays about $53,200 against $29,570 in child care [EV-CAR-521].

## The options

**A. Free school-day pre-K for every 3- and 4-year-old, through states, in any setting at one rate (recommended).**
- *What it is:*
  - At least 1,020 hours a year (about six hours a day, the school year), free.
  - It is delivered in public schools, Head Start, child care centers and family child care homes that meet the standards, all paid the same per-child rate.
  - Lead teachers are paid on the K-12 scale, the standard every design weighed here uses.
  - Classrooms use a proven curriculum with coaching.
  - Wraparound hours are covered by memo 10's guarantee.
  - The federal share is 90% for three years, then 75% (judgment). That is above the CCWFA's 60% [EV-CAR-581], so fewer states stay out.
  - It starts with 4-year-olds in 2030-31, and 3-year-olds where ready, all by 2033-34 (memo 12).
- *Where tried:* Boston, Tulsa, Georgia and Oklahoma [EV-CAR-557] [EV-CAR-555] [EV-CAR-568]; New York City's 3-K and Pre-K for All; Colorado's 15 free hours [EV-CAR-593]; the CCWFA's and BBB's pre-K titles [EV-CAR-581] [EV-CAR-587].
- *For people:* every 3- and 4-year-old has a free six-hour place at the local school, Head Start or their own child care center, which keeps the child and gets paid for the pre-K day.
- *Cost:* about $60 billion a year of new federal money at maturity, range $45-80 billion (judgment), about $80 billion in FY2040. It rests on NIEER's cost of high-quality full-day pre-K with parity, about $12,500 a child in 2020 [EV-CAR-566], grown to about $17,500. It is consistent with CBO's pre-K score for BBB once scaled (via CRFB) [EV-CAR-587].
- *Evidence strength:* lottery and regression-discontinuity evidence for strong programs (Boston, Tulsa); a randomized trial showing a weak program can harm (Tennessee). Quality, not the label, decides.
- *Political risk:*
  - Pre-K is the most popular piece, and red states such as Georgia and Oklahoma run universal programs.
  - The risks are a Tennessee-style result used against the whole program, and school-based pre-K pulling 4-year-olds out of child care centers.
  - One rate in every setting is the answer to the second.
- *Burden:* none on families. Providers meet one set of standards.

**B. No separate pre-K: 3s and 4s inside memo 10's guarantee, as one birth-to-five system.**
- *What it is:*
  - Classrooms for 3s and 4s in any setting meet a pre-K standard and get a higher rate.
  - Families pay the guarantee's copays, which are zero at or below 85% of state median income, for the whole day.
  - Schools take part as providers.
- *Where tried:* the Nordic systems [EV-CAR-543]; New Mexico's universal care [EV-CAR-547].
- *For people:* one program, one application and one rate structure from birth to kindergarten. Families above 85% of state median income pay something for pre-K hours.
- *Cost:* about $45 billion a year (judgment): A less the copays above 85%.
- *Evidence strength:* international practice. U.S. evidence on gains is from school-based programs.
- *Political risk:* "free pre-K for every child" is a simpler promise than "pre-K inside a child care subsidy". B gives it up.
- *Burden:* the yearly income statement for families above 85%.

**C. 4-year-olds only.**
- *What it is:* A's design for 4-year-olds. 3-year-olds rely on memo 10.
- *Where tried:* most state programs [EV-CAR-519].
- *For people:* one free year before kindergarten.
- *Cost:* about $35 billion a year (judgment).
- *Evidence strength:* most of the evidence is for 4-year-olds.
- *Political risk:* the lowest.
- *Burden:* as A.

## Recommendation: A

- **The strongest U.S. evidence is for free, school-day, well-taught pre-K** (Boston, Tulsa) [EV-CAR-557] [EV-CAR-555], and the universal form helps lower-income children most [EV-CAR-568].
- **One rate in every setting answers the under-3 critique.** Child care centers and homes that keep their 4-year-olds are paid for the pre-K day, so infant rooms keep their cross-subsidy. Memo 10 B funds infant care at its full cost in the same law [EV-CAR-569].
- **Quality is written in as practice, not as a checklist.** Point 6 supports credentials rather than mandating them, and point 10 makes evaluation standard. Tennessee met the checklists [EV-CAR-559].
- **Evidence strength:** strong for the best programs, mixed at scale. The design bets on curriculum, coaching and pay.
- **If you prefer one system,** B costs less and is simpler to run, at the price of a less legible promise.

**What would change this:**
- a pre-registered early evaluation (point 10) showing Tennessee-like results;
- states declining en masse at a 75% share, in which case memo 13 A's fallback runs it;
- Espen preferring pre-K in the education dossier, in which case this design moves there unchanged.
