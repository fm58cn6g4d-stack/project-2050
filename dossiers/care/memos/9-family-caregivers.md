# Memo 9: Family caregivers

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 9 of the care dossier (part B; `../3b-design-home-care.md`). Status: **for Espen, not decided.** It applies under each of memo 6's options: through the plan's benefit under B and C, and through Medicaid under A. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions.

**This memo decides how the benefit treats the family members who give most of the care today.** Wages and training for paid caregivers in general are part D's (the care workforce). Social Security credits for caregiving are the secure floor's (flagship 1).

## Why it matters

- **Families give most of the care.** 63 million family caregivers average 27 hours a week, and nearly half report harm to their finances (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025) [EV-CAR-434].
- **That care is worth as much as all paid care.** ASPE puts its lifetime value for an older adult with severe disabilities at about $111,000, about equal to the cost of paid care (ASPE, 2022) [EV-CAR-436].
- **Self-direction works.** The Cash and Counseling randomized trial found that letting people direct their own budgets and hire their own workers, often relatives, increased paid help and reduced unmet needs with no harm to safety (ASPE, 2005; Carlson and others, Health Services Research, 2007) [EV-CAR-428] [EV-CAR-450]. Both reports are by the evaluators, so there is no independent check in the register.
- **The rules on paying relatives are patchy.** Federal state-plan rules bar paying spouses and parents of minor children, but 44 states allow it through waivers (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-439]. The VA pays family caregivers of eligible veterans a stipend pegged to a federal pay grade (VA) [EV-CAR-430].
- **The legacy test.** The October 26 legacy direction asked for "a genuine paid-family-caregiver option" with "recipient choice and an available outside-care option" (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, line 137).

## Options

**A. Pay family caregivers through self-direction, at the benefit's rates, with respite and training (recommended).**
- **What it does.**
  - Anyone receiving the benefit may direct their own budget (memo 7 B) and hire any qualified adult, including a spouse, a parent of a minor child, or another relative. Pay is at the same wage the benefit pays agency aides, which part D sets, with the same labor protections.
  - A public fiscal agent in each state handles payroll and taxes.
  - Respite (paid time off for the family caregiver) and training are covered services.
  - Agency care stays available at any time, so no one is forced to rely on a relative.
- *For people:* a daughter who cut her hours to care for her father is paid as his aide for the hours his tier covers, gets two weeks of respite a year, and can switch him to an agency if she has to go back to full-time work.
- *Cost:* inside memo 6 B's estimate. Paid family care is one way the benefit's budget is spent, and a large share of new users would choose it (judgment). The cost risk is that care families now give unpaid becomes paid hours. The tier budget caps that.
- *Burden:* a timesheet for the paid hours, and the fiscal agent's paperwork.

**B. A plus a cash option.**
- **What it does.** People may instead take a cash allowance worth less than their tier's service budget, to spend as they wish. Germany pays 990 euros in cash at its top grade against up to 2,299 euros in services (German Federal Ministry of Health, 2026) [EV-CAR-440].
- *For people:* the most freedom. Families can use the money however suits them.
- *Cost:* cheaper per person than services, but more people may claim it. Roughly cost-neutral to $30 billion a year more (judgment).
- *Risk:* no one checks that care is given, and families, mostly women, may take on full-time care for less than a wage. The register does not hold German evidence on take-up or quality under the cash option.
- *Burden:* the lightest.

**C. Services only, with respite (Japan's design).**
- *For people:* care comes from paid workers, not relatives. Japan's insurance raised formal care use, with mixed effects on carers' wellbeing (Tamiya and others, Lancet, 2011) [EV-CAR-443].
- *Cost:* perhaps $10-30 billion a year less than A in 2035 (judgment), and more demand on the agency workforce part D must supply.
- *Risk:* fails the legacy test of a genuine family-caregiver option, and removes a choice 44 states already allow [EV-CAR-439].

**D. A federal caregiver tax credit.**
- *For people:* a few thousand dollars a year to caregivers who file taxes.
- *Cost:* perhaps $5-15 billion a year (judgment).
- *Risk:* a nonrefundable credit helps families with tax liability most. It pays for status, not care, and sits outside the benefit.

## Recommendation: A

- **Proven model.** It uses self-direction, the model the randomized trial supports [EV-CAR-428].
- **Choice.** It gives people the choice your October 26 direction asked for, with agency care always available.
- **Fair pay.** Family caregivers earn the same pay and protections as other aides, which also makes the benefit's budget the only limit on paid family hours.
- **Against B,** the risk is unpaid full-time care at less than a wage, without evidence in hand about quality. If you value flexibility over that risk, B is a reasonable choice, and it could follow after an evaluation of A.
- **Against C and D,** each pays less of what families give.

## If you decide

- **Section 4** writes self-direction, the fiscal agent and the respite benefit.
- **Part D** sets the wage and labor standards the benefit pays.
- **The secure floor** decides any Social Security caregiver credit separately.
