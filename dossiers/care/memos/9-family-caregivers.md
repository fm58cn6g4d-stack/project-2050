# Memo 9: Family caregivers

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 9 of the care dossier (part B, long-term care: support at home and in nursing homes for older and disabled people; `../3b-design-home-care.md`). Status: **decided by Espen, November 11, 2026: E, as recommended** (`DEC-2026-11-11-02`); revised after the independent audit (`../review-part-b-evidence.md`, section 4). It applies under each of memo 6's options: through the plan's benefit under B and C, and through Medicaid under A. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions.

**What changed after the audit: the recommendation, from A to E.**
- *New option E* is the audit's lighter option: keep today's state options for paying relatives, add respite and training in 2030-34, evaluate paying spouses and parents nationally, then decide on going national, with the decision point in the 2029 law.
- *Why it changed.* The audit found self-direction, the way relatives get paid, well supported by a randomized trial. It found the national-scale cost of new paid family hours thin, and the evidence on whether paid care replaces unpaid care mixed. It placed the political risk in A's new national right for middle-income families, not in the principle. You raised that risk: paying family caregivers seems politically hard right after Medicare for All. E keeps paid family care wherever states allow it now and lets the evidence decide the national rule.
- *A stays a real option*, with its political case below.
- *A correction.* "44 states pay spouses or parents" becomes "44 states allow it in at least one waiver"; over two-thirds of states cannot count how many recipients get paid family care [EV-CAR-439] [EV-CAR-493].

**This memo decides how the benefit treats the family members who give most of the care today.** Wages and training for paid caregivers in general are part D's (the care workforce). Social Security credits for caregiving are the secure floor's (flagship 1).

## Why it matters

- **Families give most of the care.** 63 million family caregivers average 27 hours a week, and nearly half report harm to their finances (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025) [EV-CAR-434].
- **That care is worth as much as all paid care.** ASPE puts its lifetime value for an older adult with severe disabilities at about $111,000, about equal to the cost of paid care (ASPE, 2022) [EV-CAR-436].
- **Paying relatives is already mainstream.** All responding states pay some family caregivers through at least one Medicaid home-care program; 11 run structured family caregiving (KFF, 2025) [EV-CAR-493]. 44 states allow paying spouses or parents of minor children in at least one waiver, though federal state-plan rules bar it [EV-CAR-439]. In California's IHSS about 70% of recipients hire a relative [EV-CAR-494]. VA pays about 70,000 caregivers a stipend pegged to a federal pay grade, with $2.6 billion obligated in FY2025 [EV-CAR-492] [EV-CAR-430].
- **No one knows the national count.** Over two-thirds of states cannot say how many waiver recipients get paid family care [EV-CAR-493].
- **The legacy test.** The October 26 legacy direction asked for "a genuine paid-family-caregiver option" with "recipient choice and an available outside-care option" (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, line 137).

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Self-direction works for the people served** (randomized trial): Cash and Counseling raised paid help and cut unmet needs, with no harm to safety (ASPE, 2005; Carlson and others, 2007) [EV-CAR-428] [EV-CAR-450]. Most hires were relatives [EV-CAR-488]. Both reports are by the evaluators; there is no independent check in the register.
2. **It eases caregivers' strain, but paid relatives feel more emotional strain than agency workers** (the same trial) [EV-CAR-487] [EV-CAR-488].
3. **Whether paid care replaces unpaid care is contested.** In the trial, paid hours rose but total hours did not, because unpaid hours fell [EV-CAR-486]; an unpublished study finds Medicaid home care mainly replaces family care [EV-CAR-491]. A peer-reviewed study finds state home-care expansions raised adult children's informal care [EV-CAR-489], and a study of private insurance found no fall in informal care [EV-CAR-490]. Inside capped budgets this changes who is paid more than how much is spent; the cost risk is new users who now get only unpaid care.
4. **Respite's evidence is weak:** a review of four randomized trials found no significant effect on caregivers, on very low-quality evidence (Cochrane, 2014) [EV-CAR-496]. That is a gap, not proof it fails.
5. **Fraud is a known risk in personal care:** personal care cases were 12% of Medicaid fraud units' investigations in FY2015, and 38% of indictments in FY2012-15 (HHS inspector general, 2017) [EV-CAR-463].

## Options

**E. Keep today's state options, add respite and training in 2030-34, evaluate, then decide on going national (recommended; the audit's lighter option).**
- **What it does.**
  - *2030-34, under the bridge (point 1):* today's state options for paying relatives continue. Respite and caregiver training are covered home-care services states may claim at the bridge's higher match. States report how many people get paid family care, which two-thirds cannot do today [EV-CAR-493].
  - *An evaluation:* a federal evaluation of paying spouses and parents of minor children, randomized where states agree, reporting by 2034.
  - *From 2035, in the benefit:* anyone may direct their own budget (memo 7 B) and hire a relative on the terms their state allowed on December 31, 2034, at the benefit's wage, through a public fiscal agent that handles payroll. Respite and training stay covered. Agency care is always available.
  - *The decision point, in the 2029 law:* once the evaluation reports, the plan's administrator may extend the national rule (option A) by regulation, and must report to Congress either way.
- *For people:* a daughter in a state that pays relatives today keeps being paid as her father's aide in 2035, gets respite and training, and can switch him to an agency. A wife in a state that bars paying spouses gets respite and training, and paid hours from an agency or another relative, until the national rule follows the evaluation.
- *Where tried:* every responding state, under different rules [EV-CAR-493]; the Cash and Counseling trial [EV-CAR-428].
- *Cost:* inside memo 6 B's estimate, perhaps $10-30 billion a year less than A in 2035, because fewer new paid family hours start before the evaluation (judgment; not taken off memo 6 B's figure). Respite and training under the bridge, a few billion dollars a year, inside point 1's match.
- *Evidence strength:* the mechanism is well supported; E is built to answer the part that is not (study in implementation, `DEC-2026-11-10-07`).
- *Political case:*
  - *What the bill says.* "Paid family care continues as states allow it today," plus respite, training and an evaluation. That is a smaller target in the same bill as Medicare for All than a new national right to be paid for care families now give.
  - *It builds on red-state and VA practice,* which Republicans already fund [EV-CAR-493] [EV-CAR-492].
  - *It answers the fraud attack with records:* counts, payroll through a fiscal agent, and an evaluation [EV-CAR-463].
  - *The cost:* families in states that bar paying spouses or parents wait longer for pay, and advocates will call it timid. A national rule of fifty states' terms is more complex to run than one rule.
- *Burden:* a timesheet and the fiscal agent's paperwork for paid hours; states report one count.

**A. A national right to paid family care through self-direction, at the benefit's rates, from 2035.**
- **What it does.**
  - Anyone receiving the benefit may direct their own budget and hire any qualified adult, including a spouse, a parent of a minor child, or another relative, in every state. Pay is at the same wage the benefit pays agency aides, which part D sets, with the same labor protections.
  - A public fiscal agent in each state handles payroll and taxes. Respite and training are covered services. Agency care stays available.
- *For people:* the daughter and the wife above are both paid as aides for the hours their relative's tier covers, from 2035, in every state.
- *Cost:* inside memo 6 B's estimate. Paid family care is one way the benefit's budget is spent; the cost risk is that care families now give unpaid becomes paid hours for new users. The tier budget caps it per person, not in total.
- *Evidence strength:* the randomized trial for the mechanism; no national test of paying spouses and parents at scale; thin on its cost.
- *Political case:*
  - *One national rule* is simpler for families and agencies than fifty states' terms.
  - *Paying relatives is not new:* all responding states, red and blue, the VA and a bipartisan tax bill already support caregivers [EV-CAR-493] [EV-CAR-492] [EV-CAR-475].
  - *It is a reason for middle-income families to back the benefit,* and one advocate-sponsored poll found 64% saying government should pay all family caregivers, not only low-income ones (New America, 2026) [EV-CAR-495]. That is a single poll.
  - *The risk:* a new national entitlement to be paid for family care, for middle-income families, in the same bill as Medicare for All, priced without a national test. "Paying people to care for their own parents" and "fraud" are the expected attacks.
- *Burden:* as E.

**B. A plus a cash option.**
- *What it does:* people may instead take a cash allowance worth less than their tier's service budget, to spend as they wish. Germany pays 990 euros in cash at its top grade against up to 2,299 euros in services [EV-CAR-440]; about 3.1 million of 5.7 million Germans in need of care take only the cash benefit [EV-CAR-485].
- *For people:* the most freedom.
- *Cost:* roughly cost-neutral to $30 billion a year more (judgment).
- *Risk:* no one checks that care is given, and families, mostly women, may take on full-time care for less than a wage. The register holds no German evidence on quality under the cash option.
- *Burden:* the lightest.

**C. Services only, with respite (Japan's design).**
- *For people:* care comes from paid workers, not relatives. Japan's insurance raised formal care use, with mixed effects on carers' wellbeing (Tamiya and others, Lancet, 2011) [EV-CAR-443].
- *Cost:* perhaps $10-30 billion a year less than A in 2035 (judgment), and more demand on the agency workforce part D must supply.
- *Risk:* fails the legacy test, and removes a choice all responding states already offer in some form [EV-CAR-493].

**D. A federal caregiver tax credit (the Credit for Caring Act).**
- *What it does:* a nonrefundable credit of 30% of care costs above $2,000, up to $5,000, for working caregivers with earned income above $7,500 [EV-CAR-475].
- *Cost:* perhaps $5-15 billion a year (judgment; no CBO score in the register).
- *Risk:* it helps families with tax liability and misses the poorest caregivers; it pays for status, not care. It is bipartisan and could sit alongside E.

## Recommendation: E (changed from A)

- **It keeps what is proven.** Self-direction, with relatives hired where states allow it, is the model the randomized trial supports [EV-CAR-428], and E carries every state's current option into the benefit.
- **It tests what is not.** The national cost of paying spouses and parents, and how much paid care replaces unpaid care, are unresolved [EV-CAR-486] [EV-CAR-489]. E gets the count and the evaluation before the national rule.
- **It answers your concern on timing.** The new national right waits for evidence instead of riding in the same bill as Medicare for All. The audit's reading is that the evidence supports caution on scale and timing, not on the principle.
- **It still passes the legacy test,** with agency care always available and paid family care kept wherever it exists today.
- **Against A,** A is the stronger promise and simpler to run, and it is a reasonable choice if you weigh middle-income families' support for the benefit above the exposure. E can reach A soon after 2035 if the evaluation supports it.
- **Against B, C and D,** each pays less of what families give or pays without care.

## If you decide

- **Under E:** section 4 writes the state options carried into the benefit, respite and training under the bridge, the count, the evaluation and the decision point; part D sets the wage.
- **Under A:** section 4 writes the national rule, the fiscal agent and the respite benefit from 2035.
- **Either way:** the secure floor decides any Social Security caregiver credit separately.
