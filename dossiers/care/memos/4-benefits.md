# Memo 4: What the coverage covers, and what people pay at the point of care

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 4 of the health design (`../3-design.md`). Status: decided by Espen, November 10, 2026 (`DEC-2026-11-10-09`): **C, comprehensive benefits with small capped copays above 200% of poverty; long-term care decided in part B**; with abortion and reproductive care covered and no Hyde-type limit on the fund (`DEC-2026-11-10-11`, item 4). Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`); "the coverage model" is this project's exploratory analysis (`models/tools/health-coverage/results.md`).

**This memo decides the benefit the new system guarantees and its cost sharing.** It applies to the single plan under memo 1's B or C, to each stage of B, and to Medicare under every option. Long-term care at home is decided in part B of this dossier (care at home for all ages), so it is a switch here, not a choice.

## Why it matters

- Traditional Medicare has no yearly cap on what people pay for hospital and doctor care (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-137], and leaves out routine dental, vision and hearing care (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, HLT-2).
- Half of working-age adults delayed or skipped care because of cost in 2026 (Commonwealth Fund, 2026) [EV-CAR-110]. Deductibles are common: the average for single employer coverage was $1,955 (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-113].
- Cost sharing drives the single-payer range more than almost anything but payment rates: Urban's "lite" design with income-based cost sharing cut national spending 6%, while its no-cost-sharing design raised it about 13% (Urban Institute, 2019) [EV-CAR-224] [EV-CAR-223], partly because people use more care when it is free and partly because the enhanced design adds long-term care.

## The options

**A. Today's Medicare benefits plus the legacy upgrades (HLT-2).** A $5,000 yearly out-of-pocket cap in traditional Medicare, and dental, vision and hearing (hearing and vision from 2031, dental phased from 2033), with ordinary Medicare cost sharing.
- *For people:* closes Medicare's worst gaps; keeps deductibles and 20% coinsurance.
- *Cost:* legacy priced it at $0.90-1.24 trillion over 2030-39 (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, HLT-2), with about a quarter covered by Part B premiums.
- *Fits:* memo 1, A (public option), where Medicare stays separate.

**B. Comprehensive benefits with no cost sharing.** Hospital, doctor, prescription drugs, mental health and addiction care, maternity, dental, vision and hearing; no premiums, deductibles or copays.
- *For people:* the simplest possible system to use: no bills, no networks, nothing to check. Matches the design lens fully.
- *Cost:* the most use of care. In the coverage model, cost sharing of 1% to 6% of spending moves the single plan's federal cost by about $300 billion a year in 2032 (from about $3.6 trillion to $3.25 trillion), so no cost sharing sits at the top of that range.
- *Risk:* demand outruns supply at first (CBO, 2020) [EV-CAR-219], so waits grow unless the workforce and rates keep up (memo 5).

**C. Comprehensive benefits with small, income-related cost sharing (recommended).** The benefits in B; no cost sharing for anyone below 200% of the poverty line, and for primary care, prevention, maternity, mental health and chronic-condition drugs for everyone; small fixed copays for other services above that line, with a low yearly cap (a working figure of $1,000 per person).
- *For people:* nearly everyone's routine care is free; nobody faces a large bill.
- *Cost:* between the CBO-like and project-central single-payer in the coverage model; the copays mainly damp use of specialist and hospital outpatient care, where supply is tightest.
- *Burden:* the income line uses tax records the plan already holds; no one applies.

**D. B or C plus long-term care** (home and institutional care for people who need help with daily activities).
- *Cost:* the coverage model's working assumption is $200 billion a year (range $100-400 billion); RAND's Medicare for All estimate with long-term care raised national spending 1.8%, or 9.8% if supply were unconstrained (RAND, 2019) [EV-CAR-228].
- *Why a switch:* Medicaid pays for more than two-thirds of home and community care today, with almost 650,000 people on waiting lists (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-138]. The October 26 legacy direction asks for an all-age home-support benefit weighed against enhanced Medicaid and catastrophic insurance (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`). That is part B's decision.

## Recommendation

**C, with long-term care decided in part B.** It keeps nearly all care free at the point of use, which removes the cost barrier the evidence shows matters most, and uses a small, capped copay where it does the most to protect supply. If you prefer the cleanest system, B costs modestly more. Under memo 1's A, apply A's Medicare upgrades on the legacy dates.

**What would change this:** early-stage data (memo 2's wave 1) showing waits rising for specialist care would argue for C's copays; data showing no rise would argue for B.
