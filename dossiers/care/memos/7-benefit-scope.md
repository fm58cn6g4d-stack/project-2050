# Memo 7: Who qualifies and how much the benefit covers

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 7 of the care dossier (part B, long-term care: support at home and in nursing homes for older and disabled people; `../3b-design-home-care.md`). Status: **for Espen, not decided**; revised after the independent audit (`../review-part-b-evidence.md`). It applies to memo 6's option B (and to C after its waiting period). Under memo 6 A, Medicaid's rules decide both questions state by state. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions; their basis is in `../3b-design-home-care.md`.

**What changed after the audit.** The recommendations stay: part 1 A, part 2 B. Three corrections:
- *Part 1 adds a hold-harmless clause.* The earlier text said today's Medicaid users "keep their eligibility" at two daily activities. Nothing supported that: Medicaid's level of care is set state by state, and the 2025 law adds a waiver for people below it [EV-CAR-414]. The clause makes the promise true.
- *The threshold's source is the tax code's test*, used by private policies, the WISH Act and the Credit for Caring Act [EV-CAR-499], not the Urban brief cited before.
- *Option B states the House bill's line in full:* one daily activity, or a similar need for help with household tasks [EV-CAR-470].

Costs are restated on memo 6's rebuilt basis. The tier amounts in part 2 are working assumptions, not proven figures.

**This memo decides two things, each on its own:** the need threshold that makes someone eligible (part 1), and how much help the benefit pays for once they are (part 2).

## Why it matters

- **Breadth, depth and cost.** These two choices set most of the benefit's cost and whom it reaches. CBO's broad option, from one limitation and with no limits, adds $599 billion of federal spending in 2030 [EV-CAR-452] [EV-CAR-453]; memo 6 B, at two activities with set budgets, about $380 billion in 2035.
- **The threshold.** Private policies and two bipartisan bills use the tax code's test: help with two daily activities for 90 days, or supervision for severe cognitive impairment [EV-CAR-499]. ASPE counted 5.7 million people 65 and over meeting it in 2011 [EV-CAR-482]. The House bill starts at one [EV-CAR-470]; WA Cares requires three [EV-CAR-497]. Medicaid uses each state's nursing-home level of care.
- **Depth.** Germany pays a set budget by care grade: up to 2,299 euros a month in services at grade 5. Its ministry calls it a "partial-benefit system" (German Federal Ministry of Health, 2026) [EV-CAR-440]. Its benefits lost value until they were indexed (Nadash and others, 2018) [EV-CAR-441], and German nursing-home residents still pay about 3,108 euros a month themselves in their first year [EV-CAR-484]. S. 1506 sets no limit [EV-CAR-276].
- **The legacy test.** The October 26 legacy direction asked for "protection against prolonged intensive-care costs" and warned against calling a modest allowance complete care insurance (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, line 137).

## Part 1: the need threshold

**A. Help with two or more daily activities, or the equivalent, with a hold-harmless for today's users (recommended).**
- *Who qualifies:* someone who needs help with two of bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring and continence, or who needs supervision because of a cognitive impairment.
- *For children and people with intellectual or developmental disabilities,* an age-appropriate standard for the same level of need, with supervision and habilitation needs counted.
- *Hold-harmless:* everyone receiving Medicaid home care on December 31, 2034 is eligible for the benefit from January 1, 2035, at least at their current level of service, whether or not they meet the test. They are reassessed only when their needs change (point 8), never to move them off.
- *Where tried:* private long-term care insurance; the WISH Act and Credit for Caring use it [EV-CAR-499]; Urban modeled it [EV-CAR-483].
- *Cost:* as in memo 6 B's central estimate. The hold-harmless costs little new, because these people are already in Medicaid's spending.
- *Evidence strength:* practice and survey data. The audit rates the line well supported.
- *Burden:* one assessment; none for people already served.

**B. One daily activity, or a similar need for help with household tasks (the House bill's line).**
- *For people:* it reaches people earlier, when light help can prevent a fall or a move.
- *Cost:* perhaps 50-80% more users and about $140-220 billion a year more in 2035 (judgment, on memo 6's rebuilt new-user line). The supply strain grows with it. CBO's option 5, at about this line, shows the scale [EV-CAR-452].

**C. Three daily activities (WA Cares' line).**
- *Cost:* perhaps 30-40% fewer new users, saving about $80-130 billion a year in 2035 (judgment).
- *For people:* without A's hold-harmless, many people now on Medicaid waivers would not qualify. With it, they keep their help, but new users with moderate needs are left out.

**Recommendation for part 1: A.** It uses an existing standard that private policies and two bipartisan bills share, reaches serious need without the cost and supply strain of B, and, with the hold-harmless, takes nothing from anyone Medicaid serves today. The CAPABLE demonstration (point 6) and the plan's medical benefit cover earlier, lighter needs.

## Part 2: how much the benefit pays

**A. Full assessed need, no cap (S. 1506's design).**
- *How it works:* the care plan sets the services, with no budget limit.
- *For people:* complete protection, including 24-hour care at home where that is safe.
- *Cost:* the highest, perhaps $50-100 billion a year more than B in 2035 (judgment). The plan carries all the risk of rising use, and assessors face pressure both ways.
- *Burden:* individual care plans negotiated case by case.

**B. Set budgets by need tier, high enough for intensive need at home (recommended).**
- **How it works.**
  - Five need tiers, set by the one assessment (point 8). Each tier carries a monthly budget for services, indexed each year to home-care wages.
  - The top tier is set at about the plan's cost of a nursing-home stay's care part, so that staying home is never the worse-funded choice.
  - People choose agency care, self-directed care or both (memo 9).
  - A clinical exceptions process covers needs above the top tier, for example ventilator users or people needing two-person transfers.
- *For people:* a person in the top tier can get about 40 hours a week of agency care, or more hours if self-directed at lower wages. That is the care part of a nursing-home year (about 65% of $114,975) at $35 an hour (CareScout, 2025) [EV-CAR-437] (project arithmetic and judgment). Family fills the rest, or the exceptions process covers more.
- *Where tried:* Germany's and Japan's graded benefits [EV-CAR-440] [EV-CAR-442]; Medicaid's self-directed budgets, tested in the Cash and Counseling randomized trial [EV-CAR-428].
- *Cost:* memo 6 B's central estimate, about $380 billion a year of new federal money in 2035 (judgment).
- *Why it is not a partial benefit:* the amounts are meant to meet each tier's typical need, not to share the cost, and they are indexed. Germany's are neither. Whether the amounts in fact meet typical need is a working assumption until a national assessment study sets them.
- *Evidence strength:* international practice and one trial for the structure; the amounts are judgment. The audit rates it plausible but thin.
- *Burden:* none beyond the assessment. The budget follows the tier.

**C. A basic allowance.**
- *How it works:* a flat monthly payment, say $500-1,000, to everyone who meets the threshold, with Medicaid on top for low incomes.
- *For people:* useful help for many families, but it is not care insurance. WA Cares' entire lifetime benefit of $36,500 [EV-CAR-445] buys about a year of part-time care at $35 an hour (CareScout, 2025) [EV-CAR-437] (project arithmetic).
- *Cost:* perhaps $60-120 billion a year in 2035 (judgment), with Medicaid's spend-down and waiting lists left in place.
- *Burden:* light.

**Recommendation for part 2: B.**
- **It meets the legacy test** of protection against prolonged intensive costs, through a top tier funded at the level of nursing-home care.
- **It keeps costs predictable.** The plan and the financing settlement can see each tier's budget and how many people are in it. Urban's and CBO's higher figures come partly from designs with no such limit [EV-CAR-483] [EV-CAR-453].
- **It follows the design lens.** One assessment sets the budget, not a negotiation.
- **Against A,** B gives up a little protection at the extremes for a cost the plan can manage. The exceptions process is the backstop.
- **C is not care insurance,** and it should not be called that.

## Against the bills

| Bill | Threshold | Depth | Match or diverge with the recommendation, and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **S. 1506** [EV-CAR-276] | None in statute | Full need, no limit | Diverge on both: a threshold and set budgets make cost predictable |
| **H.R. 3069** [EV-CAR-470] | One daily activity, or a similar need with household tasks | Full need, no cost sharing | Diverge: two activities and tiers; option B and part 2 A are its design |
| **WISH Act** [EV-CAR-472] | The tax code's test, for a year or more | A set amount: 6 hours a day at median cost | Match on the threshold and on a set amount |
| **Credit for Caring Act** [EV-CAR-475] | Two daily activities, or similar | A tax credit | Match on the threshold |
| **WA Cares** [EV-CAR-497] | Three daily activities, 90 days | $36,500 lifetime | Diverge: option C's line and an allowance |
| **Medicaid today** [EV-CAR-414] | Each state's level of care; from 2028 a waiver below it | Each state's services | The hold-harmless carries today's users in whatever the state's line |
