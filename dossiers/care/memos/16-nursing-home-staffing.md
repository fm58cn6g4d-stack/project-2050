# Memo 16: Nursing home staffing

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 16 of the care dossier (part D, the care workforce; `../3d-design-workforce.md`). It answers part B's point 10, which left the 2025 law's staffing moratorium open for part D (`../3b-design-home-care.md`). Status: **for Espen, not decided.** Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to the evidence register (`../evidence.json`). Costs marked "judgment" are this dossier's working assumptions.

**This memo decides whether federal law sets a minimum level of nurse staffing in nursing homes, and how.** Pay in nursing homes is memo 14. Who pays for nursing home care from 2035 is part B's memo 8.

## The question

- **The 2024 rule.** CMS required at least 3.48 hours of nurse staffing per resident a day, including 0.55 registered nurse hours and 2.45 nurse aide hours, and a registered nurse on site around the clock (CMS final rule, 2024) [EV-CAR-600].
- **What happened to it.**
  - *The courts:* two federal courts vacated the core standards in 2025, the Texas court on the ground that CMS lacked authority (as described in CMS's 2025 repeal) [EV-CAR-602].
  - *Congress:* the 2025 reconciliation law barred them until September 30, 2034 [EV-CAR-416].
  - *CMS:* it repealed them from February 2, 2026, keeping only the facility assessment [EV-CAR-603].
- **Where homes stand.**
  - *Each standard:* in May 2024, 59% of homes met the 3.48 total, 50% the registered nurse minimum and 30% the aide minimum (HHS's planning office, ASPE, 2024) [EV-CAR-604].
  - *All three:* fewer than one in five (19%) met them all (KFF, 2024) [EV-CAR-605].
- **The question.** Leave staffing to current law, restore the 2024 standards, set a new standard in statute, or pay for staffing instead of requiring it?

## What the evidence says, in four lines

1. **Quality rises with staffing, with no plateau.** Registered nurse staffing matters most (Abt Associates' study for CMS, 2023) [EV-CAR-606].
2. **State minimums worked, with a side effect.** They raised nursing hours about 5% and cut severe citations, but homes swapped licensed nurses for aides, because the rules counted all hours alike (Chen and Grabowski, Health Economics, 2015; quasi-experimental) [EV-CAR-607].
3. **Registered nurse hours drive quality; aide hours show no significant effect** (Lin, Journal of Health Economics, 2014; quasi-experimental) [EV-CAR-608].
4. **The 2024 rule failed on authority and money, not on evidence.** It cost $43.0 billion over ten years and added no federal payment (CMS, 2024) [EV-CAR-601]; courts held CMS lacked authority [EV-CAR-602].

## The options

**A. Current law until the moratorium ends in 2034.**
- *What it is:* no federal minimum beyond the old law (a registered nurse eight hours a day) and the facility assessment [EV-CAR-603]. Revisit in 2034.
- *For residents:* nothing changes. Four in five homes stay below at least one of the 2024 standards [EV-CAR-605].
- *Cost:* none. The second Trump administration counts the repeal as saving $55.1 billion over ten years [EV-CAR-603], its own estimate.
- *Political risk:* none new. Advocates for residents object.
- *Burden:* none new.

**B. The 2024 standards in statute from 2030.**
- *What it is:* the rule's numbers [EV-CAR-600] written into the Social Security Act, with its phase-in and its exemptions for shortage areas, from 2030.
- *Where tried:* the rule never took effect. New York has a 3.5-hour statutory standard (not in register).
- *For residents:* more nurse and aide time in about 80% of homes [EV-CAR-601].
- *Cost:* about $4-6 billion a year gross; federal about $3-5 billion, since Medicaid pays for 63% of residents (KFF) [EV-CAR-438]. About $0.04 trillion over FY2030-39 (judgment, from CMS's $4.30 billion a year [EV-CAR-601]).
- *Evidence strength:* quasi-experimental for minimums [EV-CAR-607] [EV-CAR-608]. The 2.45 aide figure has the weakest support [EV-CAR-608].
- *Political risk:*
  - The nursing home industry sued and won once [EV-CAR-602].
  - Rural homes argue they cannot hire. CMS estimated about 16,000 registered nurses were needed [EV-CAR-601], about 0.5% of the 3.47 million registered nurse jobs (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-610] (project arithmetic).
  - A statute answers the courts but not the hiring argument.
- *Burden:* homes report staffing through the payroll system they already use [EV-CAR-604].

**C. A statutory standard that puts registered nurses first, phased with the workforce plan and funded (recommended).**
- *What it is:*
  - *From 2031:* a registered nurse on site around the clock and at least 0.55 registered nurse hours per resident day, the parts with the strongest evidence [EV-CAR-608].
  - *From 2035:* the 3.48 total, when the plan pays the care part of nursing home stays under part B's memo 8 C. Licensed practical nurse hours count toward it, so homes are not pushed to swap nurses for aides [EV-CAR-607].
  - *Exemptions:* published, time-limited exemptions where the local nursing workforce is at least 20 percent below the national average, as in the 2024 rule (CMS, 2024) [EV-CAR-640].
  - *Funding:* Medicaid and the plan's rates must fund the standard, with the federal government paying the added cost in Medicaid until 2035.
  - *The 2025 moratorium:* the 2029 law repeals it only as it applies to this new standard.
- *For residents:* round-the-clock registered nurses everywhere from 2031; more total care from 2035.
- *Cost:* about $1-2 billion a year in 2031-34, about $5-7 billion gross from 2035 (federal about $4-6 billion), about $0.03 trillion over FY2030-39 and about $6 billion in FY2040 (judgment).
- *Evidence strength:* quasi-experimental for the registered nurse emphasis [EV-CAR-608] and for counting licensed nurses [EV-CAR-607]; observational for the total [EV-CAR-606]. The phasing is judgment.
- *Political risk:*
  - The industry opposes any minimum, but the funding and phasing remove its main arguments.
  - Unions and resident advocates may call 2035 too slow for the total.
- *Burden:* as B. The phasing gives homes four years to hire.

**D. Published staffing and higher pay for homes that staff more, with no minimum.**
- *What it is:* staffing levels published (they already are) and a payment bonus for homes above set levels.
- *For residents:* better staffing where homes take the bonus; nothing in homes that do not.
- *Cost:* about $1-2 billion a year (judgment).
- *Evidence strength:* none in the register for staffing bonuses.
- *Political risk:* the least opposition.
- *Burden:* none new.

## Recommendation: C

- **It keeps the parts of the 2024 rule with the strongest evidence first:** registered nurse time [EV-CAR-608].
- **It counts licensed nurses toward the total,** which avoids the skill-mix loss of state minimums [EV-CAR-607].
- **It fixes the 2024 rule's two failures.**
  - *Authority:* it is in statute, against the courts' holding [EV-CAR-602].
  - *Money:* the payer funds it [EV-CAR-601].
- **It is timed to the plan.** The full standard starts when the plan pays the care part of nursing home stays (part B, memo 8 C).
- **Evidence strength: moderate to strong** for the registered nurse parts; observational for the full total.

**What would change this:**
- the Fifth Circuit appeal or a final repeal rule changes the legal picture (not decisive: C is statute);
- memo 8 is A (nursing homes stay in Medicaid), in which case the federal government pays the standard's added Medicaid cost from 2031 on;
- the hiring estimate proves far larger than CMS's 16,000 registered nurses [EV-CAR-601] (a slower phase).
