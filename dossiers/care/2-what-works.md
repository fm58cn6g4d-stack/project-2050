# Care you can count on, part A (health): what works

Role: section 2 of the care dossier, health part: research, programs at home and abroad, and failures to avoid, behind the design choices in section 3. Status: reviewed at L3 (health, `review-milestone.md`; see the README). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`). Single-payer Medicare for All, a public option and a staged path are weighed with the same care (`DEC-2026-11-09-07`); legacy designs (HLT-1 to HLT-6) are working choices, not settled (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`).

## In brief

- **Coverage improves lives and finances.** Medicaid expansion cut deaths among near-elderly adults by 9.4% [EV-CAR-200]; Medicare at 65 cuts short-run deaths among the severely ill [EV-CAR-268]; in Oregon's lottery Medicaid nearly eliminated catastrophic medical spending and cut depression, with no measured change in physical health over two years [EV-CAR-201].
- **Paperwork and steps lose eligible people.** In Massachusetts one added step cut enrollment by a third, mostly among the young, healthy and poor [EV-CAR-206]. In the 2023-24 Medicaid unwinding, 69% of people disenrolled lost coverage for procedural reasons [EV-CAR-210]. Arkansas's work requirement cut coverage and raised no employment [EV-CAR-212] [EV-CAR-213].
- **Single-payer's cost turns on a few assumptions**: what it pays providers, how much more care people use, and what it covers. Estimates of the change in national spending run from a 13% saving [EV-CAR-230] to a rise of $7 trillion over ten years on $52 trillion (project arithmetic: about 13%) [EV-CAR-223]. The federal cost is large in every estimate, because it replaces what employers, households and states pay now: $34.0 trillion over ten years in Urban's estimate, offset by $27 trillion less private and state spending [EV-CAR-222] [EV-CAR-223].
- **State public options have had modest effects.** Washington's has hardly lowered premiums [EV-CAR-235]; Colorado's lowered them with rate targets and reached 47% of its exchange [EV-CAR-239] [EV-CAR-241]; Nevada's enrolled under a third of projections in its first year [EV-CAR-242]. What made a difference was a firm cap on provider payment.
- **Abroad, universal coverage comes in more than one form**: single-payer (Taiwan, Canada), regulated competing insurers (the Netherlands, Switzerland, Germany) and a public plan with optional private cover (Australia) [EV-CAR-250] [EV-CAR-255] [EV-CAR-256] [EV-CAR-257]. The three highest-ranked systems in Commonwealth Fund's comparison use different models [EV-CAR-258].

## 1. What coverage does

**Health.**
- A peer-reviewed study of linked survey and death records found the ACA's Medicaid expansions cut annual mortality among near-elderly adults by 0.132 percentage points, 9.4% [EV-CAR-200].
- Reaching Medicare at 65 lowers seven-day mortality among severely ill emergency admissions by nearly 1 percentage point, about 20%, and the gap lasts at least 9 months [EV-CAR-268].
- Oregon's randomized lottery found no statistically significant change in measured physical health in two years, but a 9.15-point drop in depression and near-elimination of catastrophic spending [EV-CAR-201]. The study is the strongest design but was small and short; it does not contradict the mortality findings, which needed larger samples and longer follow-up.

**Money.** Medicaid cut borrowing or skipped bills for medical costs by 14.2 points from a base of 24.4, and catastrophic out-of-pocket spending by 4.5 points from a base of 5.5 [EV-CAR-202].

## 2. What gets people covered, and what drops them

**Subsidies.** Enhanced credits drove record marketplace enrollment: over 24 million plan selections for 2025, and subsidized enrollment up 137% between 2020 and 2025 [EV-CAR-203] [EV-CAR-204]. Their expiry is now reversing it: sign-ups fell to 23.1 million for 2026, the sharpest drop since the marketplaces began, and paid enrollment could fall to about 17.5 million [EV-CAR-205]. Urban projected 4.8 million more uninsured in 2026 without the credits [EV-CAR-203]; CBO, 2.2 million in 2026 and more later [EV-CAR-204] [EV-CAR-028].

**Defaults.** Removing automatic enrollment in Massachusetts's subsidized exchange, a single extra step, cut enrollment by 33% and pushed out the young, healthy and poor [EV-CAR-206] (Shepard and Wagner). This is the strongest evidence for automatic enrollment in any design, and against designs that make people re-verify.

**Churn.** Of more than 25 million people disenrolled in the Medicaid unwinding, 69% lost coverage for paperwork or procedural reasons [EV-CAR-210]. Where states renewed automatically from records they held (61% of renewals), people kept coverage [EV-CAR-211].

**Work requirements.** In Arkansas, coverage among adults 30 to 49 fell 13.2 points more than other ages and the uninsured rate rose 7.1 points, with no significant change in employment; more than 95% of those targeted already worked or should have been exempt [EV-CAR-212] [EV-CAR-213]. About 70% of those who had to report did neither, and over 18,000 people lost coverage [EV-CAR-214]. Georgia's Pathways, applied at application, enrolled 13,369 people in two years at about $110 million, with health care only 31% of the cost [EV-CAR-215]. The 2025 law takes the Arkansas approach national from 2027 (section 1, 8.2).

## 3. Single-payer: what the estimates say

The question is not whether single-payer can cover everyone (every design does) but what it costs, who pays, and whether care is there when people need it.

**CBO.** CBO's 2019 report sets out the design choices and trade-offs without costing any bill [EV-CAR-216]. In December 2020 it costed five illustrative options for 2030 [EV-CAR-218]:
- federal subsidies up $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion in that one year;
- national health spending between $0.7 trillion lower and $0.3 trillion higher;
- demand outrunning supply, so more unmet need, delays and forgone care, especially with low cost sharing and low payment rates [EV-CAR-219].

CBO's lower-rate scenario (hospitals 13% lower, physicians 7% lower, drugs 23% lower) cut national spending 9% against its higher-rate scenario, but cut supply 2% [EV-CAR-220]. If paid for by a payroll or income tax, CBO's staff put GDP about 1% to 10% lower by 2030, and called all such estimates inherently uncertain [EV-CAR-221].

**Urban Institute** [EV-CAR-222] to [EV-CAR-225] (center-left, nonpartisan):
- an "enhanced" single-payer with no cost sharing and long-term care: federal spending up $34.0 trillion over ten years, offset by $27 trillion less spending by households, employers and states, so national spending up $7 trillion (from $52 trillion to $59 trillion);
- a "lite" single-payer with income-based cost sharing and fewer new benefits: national spending down $209.5 billion (6%) in 2020, federal cost $17.6 trillion over ten years;
- a hybrid (better subsidies, automatic enrollment backup, a public option and capped provider rates): covers 25.6 million otherwise uninsured, trims national spending slightly (0.6%), federal cost $1.5 trillion over ten years.

**Mercatus Center** (Blahous; libertarian, critical of single-payer): $32.6 trillion more federal spending over 2022-31, assuming provider payments more than 40% below private rates [EV-CAR-226]. Blahous notes that CBO's comparable option costs $2.20 trillion less than his lower bound, about a quarter of it from a different baseline [EV-CAR-227].

**RAND** (nonpartisan): with long-term care and no cost sharing, total spending up 1.8% in 2019, or 9.8% if supply could meet all new demand [EV-CAR-228]; federal spending up 221%, from $1,090.0 billion to $3,498.7 billion, while administrative spending falls 27.3% [EV-CAR-229].

**Supportive estimates.** Galvani and colleagues in The Lancet (Yale researchers, peer-reviewed; the authors favor single-payer): national spending down 13%, more than $450 billion a year, and more than 68,000 lives saved a year [EV-CAR-230]. PERI (Pollin and colleagues, University of Massachusetts; the authors favor the reform): spending down nearly 10% [EV-CAR-231]. A systematic review of 22 analyses found 19 predicting first-year savings, a median 3.46% [EV-CAR-234], though it left out two widely publicized studies that found net costs.

**Reading the range.**
- Every estimate agrees the federal budget would carry far more: $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion more in 2030 in CBO's options [EV-CAR-218], $2.8 trillion in 2020 in Urban's [EV-CAR-222], because it takes over what employers, households and states now pay. The coverage model (step 3m) sets these to a common year.
- They disagree on national spending, from a 13% saving to a rise of about 13%, for three reasons: the payment rate set for hospitals and doctors (near Medicare's rates saves money where commercial prices are 254% of Medicare [EV-CAR-131]; it also squeezes supply [EV-CAR-219] [EV-CAR-220]); how much more care people use once cost sharing goes; and whether long-term care is in.
- Administrative savings are real but smaller than the boldest claims: CBO's figures for administering traditional Medicare (1.4%) against about 12% for private insurers [EV-CAR-217], against the 34.2% total-system estimate [EV-CAR-132], which counts provider billing too. RAND's 27.3% fall in administrative spending is the middle reading [EV-CAR-229].
- So single-payer is mainly a choice about who pays. Whether the country also pays more depends on the design: the estimates run from 13% less to about 13% more. The high end comes from Urban's enhanced design, with long-term care and no cost sharing [EV-CAR-223]; the low end from designs that assume large administrative and price savings [EV-CAR-230] [EV-CAR-231]. Its household case rests on replacing premiums and out-of-pocket costs with taxes that, by design, fall less on low- and middle-income households (OUT-11, D3). Section 3's memo and the coverage model show this by income group.

**The transition.**
- **Jobs.** About 601,800 people worked for health insurers in 2025 [EV-CAR-233]. PERI's estimate of 1.8 million redundant insurance and billing jobs is gross, not net; spread over a four-year phase-in it would raise national layoffs by about 2% [EV-CAR-232] (EPI, labor-aligned). Transition help for these workers belongs in the design.
- **Supply.** CBO's demand-outruns-supply finding [EV-CAR-219] and RAND's supply-constrained estimate [EV-CAR-228] point the same way: a single-payer launch needs workforce growth and payment rates set to keep providers in, or waits grow. Canada's waits illustrate the risk (section 6).

## 4. Single-payer in the states: failures to learn from

- **Vermont.** In December 2014 Governor Shumlin's financing plan called for an 11.5% employer payroll tax plus an income-based public premium up to 9.5% of income [EV-CAR-244]. The governor dropped it when modeling put the cost at $2.6 billion, not the $1.6 to $2.2 billion first believed [EV-CAR-245]; the state's board judged the predicted administrative savings "not practical to achieve," and expected federal waiver money fell to less than half of the $267 million hoped for [EV-CAR-246].
- **California.** Senate staff put SB 562 (2017) at about $400 billion a year, half of it new taxes, roughly a 15% payroll tax if raised that way [EV-CAR-247]; PERI put it at $331 billion with 18% savings [EV-CAR-248]. AB 1400 died in January 2022 without a floor vote and without saying how it would be paid for [EV-CAR-249].
- **The lesson.** States failed on financing and federal money, not on the coverage design. A state cannot fold in Medicare or federal tax subsidies without federal permission, and must raise the whole tax itself. A federal program avoids both problems but faces the same visible tax at a larger scale. The financing interface is therefore a design fork of its own (section 3).

## 5. Public options: what the states have learned

- **Washington (Cascade Select, 2021).** The law caps aggregate payments at 160% of Medicare and requires every hospital to contract with at least one plan [EV-CAR-236]. Enrollment reached 94,000, up 50% from 2024 [EV-CAR-237]. But the state's own agency found its silver plans were the cheapest plan in 26 of 39 counties in 2025, and meaningfully cheaper than other plans in none [EV-CAR-235]. The agency now proposes a hospital-specific cap at 135% of Medicare and tying state subsidies to the public option [EV-CAR-238]. Washington's experience is that a public option without a firm rate cap does little to prices.
- **Colorado (Colorado Option, 2023).** The law set premium-reduction targets of 5%, 10% and 15% over 2023-25 and a 20% reduction target for provider rates, negotiated in public hearings [EV-CAR-240]. Enrollment reached 132,791, 47% of exchange selections, in 2025 [EV-CAR-239]. A Health Affairs study found it cut the average marketplace premium by $101 a month against 2020; hospitals say it costs them $125 million a year [EV-CAR-241] (reported by the insurers' trade group).
- **Nevada (Battle Born State Plans, 2026).** 10,762 enrolled in the first year, under a third of the roughly 35,000 expected [EV-CAR-242]. An advocacy group reports premiums about 4.7% lower, and other plans about 4.6% lower, than they would have been [EV-CAR-243].
- **The lesson.** Each is confined to the individual market. The federal public option in the legacy design (HLT-3) is larger: Medicare-based rates, on and off the exchanges, with automatic enrollment. Its savings depend on the rate it pays and on how many people it reaches, the same two levers as single-payer's.

## 6. Abroad

- **Taiwan (single-payer, 1995).** Coverage jumped overnight from about 57% to virtually everyone [EV-CAR-250]; by the end of 1995, 90% of 21 million residents were enrolled [EV-CAR-252]. Administrative cost was 1.5% of spending in the early years [EV-CAR-250] and 0.87% in 2018 [EV-CAR-252]. Critics note it is underfunded, by 1-2% of GDP, and patients still pay more out of pocket than in most of Europe [EV-CAR-251]. It is the clearest case of a fast national single-payer launch, from a starting point with more uninsured than the U.S. has today.
- **Canada (provincial single-payer).** Waits are the system's known weakness. The Fraser Institute (market-oriented) reports a median wait of 30.0 weeks from GP referral to treatment in 2024, up from 27.7 [EV-CAR-253]; its survey has a 17.0% response rate. Canada's health information institute reports 68% of hip and 61% of knee replacements within the recommended six months, below 2019 [EV-CAR-254]. The two disagree on size, not direction.
- **The Netherlands, Switzerland and Germany (regulated multiple insurers).** Switzerland since 1996 and the Netherlands since 2006 require everyone to buy basic private insurance, with about 40% of residents getting premium help and risk adjustment among insurers; uninsured rates are about 1.5% and below 1% [EV-CAR-255]. Germany covers about 86% in competing nonprofit sickness funds paid by a risk-adjusted formula, and lets higher earners opt out [EV-CAR-256]. These reach universal coverage without a single payer, but rely on a mandate and on regulating prices.
- **Australia (public Medicare plus optional private cover).** Medicare covers everyone; about half also buy private insurance, encouraged by a rebate and a tax penalty on higher earners who don't [EV-CAR-257].
- **Ranking.** Commonwealth Fund's 2024 comparison ranks Australia, the Netherlands and the U.K. top of 10 and the U.S. last, though second on care process [EV-CAR-258] [EV-CAR-130]. The top three are a public plan with private options, regulated competing insurers and a national health service: no one model wins.

## 7. Paying providers

- **Hospital price caps work.** Oregon's state employee plan capped hospital payments at 200% of Medicare in October 2019 and saved $107.5 million, 4% of spending, in two years [EV-CAR-262] (peer-reviewed). All 24 network hospitals stayed in, with no rise in other commercial prices [EV-CAR-263] (commentary). A simulation of 200% caps in every state employee plan found savings of $7.1 billion nationally in 2022, with hospital commercial margins falling only from 42.7% to 41.7% [EV-CAR-264]. Montana's plan saved $47.8 million over two years by paying 220-225% of Medicare for inpatient and 230-250% for outpatient care, with no hospital closures [EV-CAR-265].
- **All-payer rate setting works.** Maryland's all-payer model cut total Medicare spending 2.8% and hospital spending 4.1% over 2014-18, and slowed commercial hospital spending growth by 6.1% [EV-CAR-259]. Its successor cut Medicare fee-for-service spending 2.1% and admissions 16.2% in 2019-22 [EV-CAR-260], though rising non-hospital spending trimmed savings in 2021 [EV-CAR-261]. These are CMS's own evaluations (first Trump and Biden administrations), carried out by independent contractors.
- **Medicare Advantage.** MedPAC finds Medicare paid 20% more for Advantage enrollees than for similar people in traditional Medicare in 2025, about $84 billion, split between coding ($40 billion) and favorable selection ($44 billion) [EV-CAR-266], and 14% for 2026 under a new method [EV-CAR-135]. The insurers' trade group disputes the coding part [EV-CAR-267]. Both readings are presented; this dossier relies on MedPAC, which is independent and has no stake in the result, and tests the low end.

## 8. A staged path: lowering the Medicare age

- CBO projected that lowering the age to 60 without automatic enrollment would bring only 53% of the newly eligible into Parts A and B, against about 82% of people 65 to 69 [EV-CAR-207]. Among the 1.3 million uninsured newly eligible, 70% would join [EV-CAR-208].
- Urban estimated most people 60 to 64 with employer coverage, 9.2 million of 12.0 million, would keep it with Medicare as a second payer; 2.8 million would drop it [EV-CAR-209].
- So a lower age mainly moves people from employer and marketplace coverage into Medicare, covering few uninsured unless enrollment is automatic. As a step toward single-payer, its value is in building Medicare's capacity and automatic enrollment, cohort by cohort, while the larger financing is decided.

## Failures to avoid

1. **Steps that drop eligible people**: re-verification, documents and work reporting [EV-CAR-206] [EV-CAR-210] [EV-CAR-213]. The design lens (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`) points every option to automatic enrollment from records the government holds [EV-CAR-211].
2. **A public option without a rate cap** [EV-CAR-235].
3. **Financing left vague**, the reason Vermont and California stopped [EV-CAR-245] [EV-CAR-249].
4. **A launch without the supply to meet new demand** [EV-CAR-219].
5. **Counting a contested saving as certain**: the Medicare Advantage overpayment [EV-CAR-266] [EV-CAR-267] and single-payer's administrative savings [EV-CAR-217] [EV-CAR-246].
