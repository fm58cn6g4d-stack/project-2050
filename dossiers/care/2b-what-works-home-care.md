# Care you can count on, part B (care at home for all ages): what works

Role: section 2 of the care dossier, part B. It covers the evidence on paying for and delivering long-term care at home, in the United States and abroad, the designs on the table, and the failures to avoid. Status: draft (stage D, step 5). Figures cite `evidence.json`. Grades and the data-or-analysis labels are there; where only one research team supports a finding, the text says so.

## In brief

- **Home care can cost less per person, but expanding it raises total spending at first.** States with large, well-established home-care programs had slower long-term care spending growth (Kaye and others, Health Affairs, 2009) [EV-CAR-449]. A review found that new home-care models raise total costs while improving welfare (Grabowski, 2006) [EV-CAR-451]. Both can be true: substitution saves, and new users add cost. A universal benefit should be priced as new spending, not as a saving.
- **Letting people direct their own care works.** In a randomized trial, Cash and Counseling raised the amount and quality of paid help and reduced unmet needs, with no harm to safety (ASPE, 2005; Carlson and others, 2007) [EV-CAR-428] [EV-CAR-450]. Today 44 states pay spouses or parents through waivers (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-439].
- **Universal social insurance for long-term care exists and lasts.** Germany has run it since 1995 [EV-CAR-440] [EV-CAR-441], Japan since 2000 [EV-CAR-442] [EV-CAR-443], and Washington State began paying benefits in July 2026 [EV-CAR-445]. The design choices that differ are the depth of the benefit, cash versus services, and who pays.
- **Voluntary insurance fails.** The federal CLASS Act was dropped and then repealed before it began, because a voluntary program could not be made financially sustainable (CRS, 2013) [EV-CAR-424], and private policy sales have collapsed [EV-CAR-411].
- **Medicaid's tools for rebalancing work at the margin, not at scale.** Money Follows the Person moved people out of institutions and improved their lives [EV-CAR-429] [EV-CAR-421]. Temporary match increases bought workforce gains [EV-CAR-425]. None ended waiting lists, because home care stays optional.

## 1. Home care against institutional care: cost and substitution

**The two views.** The evidence divides on cost, and both views are given here.
- **Expansion saves over time.** Kaye, LaPlante and Harrington found that Medicaid long-term care spending grew faster in states with limited home-care programs than in states with large, established ones. Expansion raised spending at first, then lowered institutional spending (Health Affairs, 2009) [EV-CAR-449]. The study is observational, comparing states over 1995 to 2005.
- **New services add total cost.** Grabowski's review found that new noninstitutional care models were associated with higher total costs and greater welfare for clients and caregivers. He also noted that most evaluations used potentially confounded designs (Medical Care Research and Review, 2006) [EV-CAR-451].
- **Leaving an institution.** People who moved out of institutions under Money Follows the Person improved on all seven quality-of-life measures, and the gains largely lasted two years. Their monthly Medicaid and Medicare spending fell by $1,840 (23 percent) among older adults leaving nursing homes (CMS report to Congress, 2017) [EV-CAR-429]. Participants chose to move, so this is not a randomized result.

**Reading them together.** Moving someone who would otherwise be in a nursing home to care at home usually costs less. A benefit open to everyone with a need also reaches people who would never have entered a nursing home and now use paid care instead of family care. That second effect dominates a universal design, which is why Urban and CBO both expect single-payer long-term care to add hundreds of billions of dollars a year in federal cost (Urban Institute, 2020) [EV-CAR-448], and why RAND's Medicare for All estimate with long-term care raises national spending (RAND, 2019) [EV-CAR-228]. The added cost buys care that families now give unpaid or go without [EV-CAR-436]. **For section 3:** price the benefit as new spending, count institutional savings only for people who would otherwise enter an institution, and don't call it self-financing.

## 2. United States programs

| Program | What it does | Evidence | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Medicaid HCBS waivers (1915(c))** | Home care for people who would otherwise need an institution; states may cap enrollment | 7.8 million users, $129.4 billion in 2022 [EV-CAR-407]; almost 650,000 waiting, 34 months on average [EV-HOU-691] | An optional, capped benefit produces waiting lists however well run |
| **Community First Choice (1915(k))** | Attendant care as a state-plan entitlement, with a 6-point match increase | Only 9 states use it (MACPAC, 2023) [EV-CAR-426] | A match bonus alone does not make most states take on an open-ended benefit |
| **American Rescue Plan sec. 9817** | A 10-point match increase on home care for one year, to be reinvested | $37.1 billion planned across more than 1,400 activities. Most of what states kept supported the direct care workforce (MACPAC, 2026) [EV-CAR-425] | Temporary money buys one-time gains. Recurring pay needs recurring money |
| **Money Follows the Person** | Grants to move people out of institutions | 127,184 moved since 2008 [EV-CAR-421]; quality-of-life gains [EV-CAR-429]; expires after FY2027 [EV-HOU-697] | It works and is cheap. It should be permanent until a universal benefit replaces it |
| **Cash and Counseling, self-direction** | Participants manage a budget and hire their own workers, including relatives | A randomized trial found more paid care and fewer unmet needs, with no harm to safety [EV-CAR-428] [EV-CAR-450] | Self-direction should be a standard option, not a pilot |
| **Paying family caregivers** | Waivers can pay "legally responsible relatives" for extraordinary care | 44 states allow it through waivers. State plans generally bar paying spouses and parents of minor children (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-439] | A federal benefit can lift the bar |
| **VA family caregiver program** | A monthly stipend to family caregivers of eligible veterans, pegged to a federal pay grade | Stipend at the GS-4, step 1 rate [EV-CAR-430] | A federal precedent for paying family caregivers at a public wage benchmark |
| **PACE** | All-inclusive medical and long-term care for older adults who qualify for a nursing home | About 89,659 enrolled in 2023 (MACPAC, 2026) [EV-CAR-427] | It works for a small group. It is a delivery option, not a financing answer |
| **Medicare home health** | Skilled care for homebound people | 2.7 million users, $16.0 billion [EV-CAR-413]; no personal care alone [EV-CAR-412] | Medicare already has a home benefit. It is the wrong shape for daily help |
| **CLASS Act (2010)** | Voluntary federal long-term care insurance | HHS found no viable path to sustainability in 2011, and Congress repealed it [EV-CAR-424] | Voluntary enrollment draws people who expect to need care. A program must be universal or not attempted |
| **WA Cares Fund (Washington)** | Mandatory payroll premium of 0.58%; up to $36,500 lifetime for people needing help with three or more daily activities | Benefits began July 2026 [EV-CAR-445]. Voters kept it mandatory in 2024 | A small, universal, front-end benefit is feasible and politically durable, but it is not complete coverage |
| **Private long-term care insurance** | Individual policies | Sales fell from 754,000 in 2002 to 129,000 in 2014 [EV-CAR-411] | Not a base to build on |

**Federal proposals weighed.**
- **The Senate Medicare for All bill (S. 1506).** It puts home and community-based long-term care in the federal benefit (sec. 201) [EV-CAR-276] and keeps institutional care in Medicaid with a state floor based on FY2024 spending (sec. 204) [EV-CAR-278]. It bars estate recovery for correctly paid Medicaid and raises SSI's resource limits to $4,100 and $6,200 (secs. 205 and 1101) [EV-CAR-431].
- **The House bill.** It covers all long-term care, institutional and at home, with no cost sharing [EV-CAR-291].
- **Build Back Better (2021).** It would have raised the home-care match for states meeting conditions, for about $150 billion over ten years on CBO's estimate (KFF, 2021) [EV-CAR-447]. It did not become law.
- **Catastrophic public insurance.** This benefit starts after a waiting period. Urban's modeling finds a front-end benefit and a catastrophic benefit cost about the same, but 38 percent of the catastrophic option's spending displaces Medicaid, against 15 percent for the front-end option (Urban Institute, 2016) [EV-CAR-446]. That is one team's modeling.

## 3. Abroad

| Country | Design | Evidence | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Germany (1995)** | Universal social insurance: a payroll contribution of 3.6% (4.2% without children); five care grades; a choice of cash or services; capped benefits. At grade 5, 990 euros a month in cash or up to 2,299 euros in services (German Federal Ministry of Health, 2026) [EV-CAR-440] | It gives basic support to nearly everyone and shows a social insurance model can last. The benefit's value fell when it was not indexed (Nadash and others, 2018) [EV-CAR-441] | Need tiers with set budgets are workable. A partial benefit leaves heavy users paying. Index the amounts |
| **Japan (2000)** | Social insurance for everyone 40 and over, financed half by premiums and half by taxes (IPSS) [EV-CAR-442]. Services only, no cash | More use of formal care at lower cost to households, with mixed effects on carers and fiscal strain (Tamiya and others, Lancet, 2011) [EV-CAR-443] | Services-only shifts care from families to paid workers and strains the budget |
| **Netherlands, Nordic countries** | Broad public long-term care; the Netherlands spends 4.1% of GDP, Sweden 3.7%, Finland and Denmark 3.2%, against an OECD average of 1.8% (OECD, 2025) [EV-CAR-444] | Mostly public money: four-fifths of OECD long-term care spending | Broad public coverage is a choice to spend about 2-4% of GDP |

On CRS's 2023 figures, the United States spends about $460 billion a year on long-term care excluding Medicare post-acute care. That is roughly 1.6-1.7% of GDP, close to the OECD's 1.8%, but less than two-thirds of it is public: Medicaid and other public payers are about $290 billion: 69.4% of the total less Medicare [EV-CAR-404] [EV-CAR-405] (project arithmetic; GDP from health spending's share in `1-problem.md` [EV-CAR-120], a 2024 figure, so the share is approximate). The difference from peers is less the total than who pays: here families pay the rest out of pocket or give the care themselves. A universal benefit would raise the public share toward the OECD's four-fifths [EV-CAR-444].

## 4. Failures to avoid

- **A voluntary program.** CLASS showed that voluntary enrollment cannot be made sustainable [EV-CAR-424].
- **An optional benefit with a cap on enrollment.** It produces waiting lists however well funded [EV-CAR-417] [EV-HOU-691].
- **Temporary money for recurring costs.** ARPA's one-year match bought mainly one-time gains [EV-CAR-425].
- **Unindexed benefit amounts.** Germany's benefits lost value [EV-CAR-441].
- **An allowance called insurance.** A modest flat payment helps, but it is not care insurance. The October 26 legacy direction says not to describe it as complete (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, line 137). WA Cares' $36,500 is a lifetime total that covers about a year of part-time home care at CareScout's $35 an hour [EV-CAR-445] [EV-CAR-437] (project arithmetic: about 20 hours a week).
- **Counting institutional savings for a universal benefit.** Savings are real for people diverted from institutions [EV-CAR-429], but a universal benefit mostly serves people who would have stayed home anyway [EV-CAR-448] [EV-CAR-451].
- **Promising care the workforce cannot deliver.** Japan's fiscal strain [EV-CAR-443] and the BLS projections of openings [EV-CAR-423] are warnings. Part D sizes the workforce.

## 5. Forks for section 3

1. Who is guaranteed care at home, and through which program: a stronger Medicaid, a federal benefit in the single plan, or catastrophic insurance.
2. How much the benefit covers and who qualifies: the need threshold, and full assessed need, set budgets by need tier, or a basic allowance.
3. Whether nursing-home and other institutional care stay in Medicaid, as S. 1506 and health's working position have it, or join the plan, in whole or for the care part.
4. How the benefit treats family caregivers: paid through self-direction, a cash option, services only, or a tax credit.

The bridge from 2029 to the endpoint, Money Follows the Person, estate recovery and the seams with housing and the workforce are narrower points for section 3's yes-or-no list.

## Evidence still needed

- Randomized or quasi-experimental evidence on the cost of universal home-care benefits; the register has observational state comparisons and one team's modeling. CBO's December 2020 working paper (its option 5 includes long-term care) would help; CBO blocks automated downloads.
- The share of German beneficiaries choosing cash rather than services, and German evidence on care quality under the cash option.
- Japan's coinsurance rates and benefit trims since 2015 from a primary source.
- Independent corroboration of the BLS workforce projection, and of Urban's catastrophic-insurance modeling.
- Early WA Cares claims data (benefits began July 2026).
