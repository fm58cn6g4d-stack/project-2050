# Care you can count on, part A (health): the problem

Role: section 1 of the care dossier, health part: what is wrong, for whom and how large, and how the law will stand in January 2029. Status: draft (stage D, step 2). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`); claims still missing evidence are listed at the end and are not made here. Home care, child care and the workforce get their own problem sections later (`README.md`).

Outcomes this section frames:
- OUT-03, care people can count on, for every age;
- OUT-02, planning beyond the next paycheck (medical bills and debt);
- OUT-01, child poverty (children's coverage).

## In brief

- About 27 million people had no coverage all of 2025 [EV-CAR-100], and the number is now rising. The enhanced premium credits expired at the end of 2025 and were not renewed [EV-CAR-031] [EV-CAR-040]. The 2025 reconciliation law's Medicaid and marketplace cuts take effect from late 2026 through 2028 [EV-CAR-001] [EV-CAR-008] [EV-CAR-010].
- CBO projects 36.6 million uninsured in 2029, 10.4% of the population, the year the plan starts [EV-CAR-020].
- Having coverage is not the same as being protected. Commonwealth Fund's 2026 survey counts 46% of working-age adults as inadequately insured, uninsured or with a coverage gap or with costs too high for their income [EV-CAR-107]. Half of working-age adults delayed or skipped care because of cost [EV-CAR-110].
- The country spends far more than its peers and gets worse results. Health spending was $5.3 trillion in 2024, 18.0% of GDP [EV-CAR-120], against an OECD average of 9.3% [EV-CAR-125]. Life expectancy is 2.7 years below the OECD average [EV-CAR-126]. The gap is mostly prices, not use of care [EV-CAR-129] [EV-CAR-131].

## 1. Who has no coverage

**The count.**
- Census: 26.7 million people (7.9%) were uninsured for all of 2025, not statistically different from 2024 [EV-CAR-100].
- The National Health Interview Survey counts 28.0 million (8.3%) uninsured at the time of interview in 2025, and 11.6% of adults 18 to 64 [EV-CAR-104].
- The two surveys measure different things (all year against a point in time), so this dossier uses Census for the headline and the interview survey for groups.

**Who.**
- **Working-age adults.** 11.0% of adults 19 to 64 were uninsured, against 5.8% of children [EV-CAR-102]. Young adults 19 to 25 had the highest rate, 14.5% [EV-CAR-101].
- **Hispanic and Black adults.** 21.9% of Hispanic adults 18 to 64 were uninsured, and 11.3% of Black adults, against 8.5% of White and 4.9% of Asian adults [EV-CAR-105].
- **Noncitizens.** 28.9% of noncitizen working-age adults were uninsured, against 9.4% of naturalized citizens [EV-CAR-103].
- **States that did not expand Medicaid.** Adults there were almost twice as likely to be uninsured: 18.1% against 9.0% [EV-CAR-106]. CBO assumes no more states expand, mainly because the 2025 law limits provider taxes from 2028 [EV-CAR-024].

**The 2026 turn.** Census's 2025 figures predate the credit expiry. Commonwealth Fund's 2026 survey finds the working-age uninsured rate up from 8.9% in 2024 to 11.2% in 2026, with marketplace and individual coverage down from 8.5% to 6.4% [EV-CAR-108]. That is one survey's reading, not yet confirmed by Census, whose 2026 figures arrive in September 2027.

## 2. Coverage that does not protect

- **Underinsurance.** 30% of working-age adults insured all year were underinsured in 2026: 29% of people with employer coverage and half of people in marketplace plans [EV-CAR-109].
- **Skipped care.** The share of working-age adults who delayed or avoided care because of cost rose from 46% in 2022 to 50% in 2026 [EV-CAR-110]. In KFF's polling, 28% of all adults and 62% of uninsured adults skipped or postponed needed care in the past year because of cost [EV-CAR-115].
- **Employer coverage.** It is still the largest source, and its price keeps climbing. A family premium reached $28,303 in 2026, 5% higher than in 2025, with workers paying $7,471 of it [EV-CAR-111] [EV-CAR-112]. The average single deductible was $1,955 [EV-CAR-113]. KFF reports forecasts of employer premium increases approaching double digits for 2027 [EV-CAR-114].
- **Medicare's gap.** Traditional Medicare has no annual limit on out-of-pocket costs for Part A and Part B services, while Medicare Advantage plans must cap in-network costs at $9,250 in 2026 [EV-CAR-137].

## 3. Medical debt

- $88 billion of medical debt sat on consumer credit records in June 2021 [EV-CAR-116]. Medical bills were 58% of all third-party collection items, the most common kind [EV-CAR-117].
- A Census analysis found 17% of households owed medical debt in 2019 [EV-CAR-119]. KFF polling puts the share of adults with health care debt at about 41%, which KFF Health News and NPR reported as more than 100 million people [EV-CAR-118]. The polling counts debts owed to family, cards and payment plans, so it runs higher than the credit-record count; the two are not added.
- Coverage reduces it. In the randomized Oregon experiment, Medicaid cut the chance of borrowing or skipping other bills for medical costs by 14.2 percentage points from a base of 24.4 [EV-CAR-202].

## 4. Spending and prices

- **The total.** Health spending grew 7.2% to $5.3 trillion in 2024, $15,474 per person and 18.0% of GDP [EV-CAR-120]. Private insurance paid $1,644.6 billion of it, Medicare $1,118.0 billion, Medicaid $931.7 billion and people $556.6 billion out of pocket [EV-CAR-121].
- **The path.** CMS's actuaries project spending growing 5.4% a year over 2025-34 against 4.1% for GDP, reaching 20.6% of GDP in 2034 [EV-CAR-122], with the insured share falling from 91.8% to 90.5% [EV-CAR-123]. This is a projection by CMS's actuaries under the second Trump administration. The actuaries' office has a long nonpartisan record, but no independent projection is in the register yet, so it is presented as CMS's view.
- **Abroad.** The OECD counts U.S. spending at $14,885 per person against an OECD average of $5,967 [EV-CAR-124], 17.2% of GDP against 9.3% [EV-CAR-125]. Against wealthy peers alone, the U.S. spends nearly twice as much per person, and Peterson-KFF finds use of care generally lower, so prices explain the gap [EV-CAR-129]. Peers average 11.2% of GDP [EV-CAR-128].
- **Hospital prices.** Employers and private insurers paid on average 254% of what Medicare would have paid for the same hospital services in 2022 [EV-CAR-131] (RAND). This number drives every design in section 3: a public plan or single-payer that pays near Medicare rates saves money where these prices are high, and hospitals lose revenue there.
- **Administration.** One peer-reviewed estimate puts U.S. administrative spending at 34.2% of health spending in 2017, $2,497 per person, against $551 in Canada [EV-CAR-132]. Its authors are longtime single-payer advocates, and CBO's figures (about 1.4% for administering traditional Medicare and about 12% for private insurers [EV-CAR-217]) measure something narrower. Section 2 weighs how much a single payer could actually save.

## 5. Medicare Advantage overpayment

- 55% of eligible Medicare beneficiaries were in Medicare Advantage in 2025, up from 37% in 2018 [EV-CAR-133]; in 2026, 35.2 million of 64.2 million [EV-CAR-134].
- MedPAC estimates Medicare will spend 14% more for Medicare Advantage enrollees in 2026 than if they were in traditional Medicare, about $76 billion [EV-CAR-135]. Its 2025 figure was 20% [EV-CAR-136], about $84 billion, roughly half from coding and half from favorable selection [EV-CAR-266]. MedPAC changed its method between the two years, so they are not strictly comparable. The insurers' trade association disputes MedPAC's coding estimate [EV-CAR-267].
- Why it matters here: the legacy package counted on capturing all of this overpayment to pay for much of the plan (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, HLT-6). The size of that saving is the single largest uncertainty in the legacy financing (section 3).

## 6. Long-term care

- Medicaid pays for more than two-thirds of long-term care delivered at home and in the community. At least half a million people have been on state waiting or interest lists every year since 2016, almost 650,000 in 2026 [EV-CAR-138].
- This is part B's problem (care at home for all ages) and is not sized here, but it bears on health's choice: a single-payer design can include long-term care or leave it out, and that one switch changes its cost a great deal (section 2).

## 7. Health outcomes

- Life expectancy at birth was 79.0 years in 2024, up 0.6 year from 2023 [EV-CAR-139]; the OECD's comparison year puts the U.S. 2.7 years below the OECD average [EV-CAR-126].
- Preventable deaths were 217 per 100,000 against an OECD average of 145; deaths treatable with timely care were 95 against 77 [EV-CAR-127].
- Maternal mortality was 17.9 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2024 [EV-CAR-140]; for Black women it was 44.8, against 14.2 for White women [EV-CAR-141].
- Coverage saves lives. The ACA's Medicaid expansions cut yearly mortality among near-elderly adults by 9.4% [EV-CAR-200], and reaching Medicare at 65 cuts short-run deaths among severely ill emergency patients [EV-CAR-268] (section 2).

## 8. The baseline in January 2029

The plan starts against current law as it will stand in January 2029 (`charter/STANDARDS.md`, "Time frame"). Three changes define it.

### 8.1 The enhanced premium credits expired

- The enhanced credits from the 2021 and 2022 laws ended after December 31, 2025 [EV-CAR-031]. The House passed a three-year extension 230 to 196 on January 8, 2026, but a Senate bill fell short of 60 votes [EV-CAR-039]. As of November 2026 Congress had not extended them; at least 11 states fund extra premium help on their own [EV-CAR-040].
- Effects so far:
  - plan selections fell to 23.1 million for 2026, about 1.2 million fewer than 2025 [EV-CAR-030];
  - KFF reports paid enrollment falling from 21.8 million to 19.2 million, and perhaps to 17.5 million [EV-CAR-034];
  - net premium payments rose 58% on average and deductibles 37%, as people moved to thinner plans [EV-CAR-035].
- CBO projected 3.8 million more uninsured a year on average over 2026-34 without an extension [EV-CAR-028]. A permanent extension would cost about $350 billion and cover 3.8 million more people by 2035 [EV-CAR-029].
- A 2027-28 deal could restore the credits for some years before the plan starts (`dossiers/scenario/`, question 0a). Section 3 states the cost both ways.

- Two summaries put the work requirement's start at "December 31, 2026" (CRS) [EV-CAR-027] or "January 1, 2027" (KFF) [EV-CAR-044]. The statute's "first quarter beginning after December 31, 2026" means January 1, 2027 [EV-CAR-001]; the legacy note that six-month checks begin "after December 31, 2026" is corrected the same way [EV-CAR-006].
- **Where implementation stands.** CMS's interim final rule took effect July 31, 2026, limiting first exemptions to six months [EV-CAR-041]. CMS expects about 10 states to ask and about 2 to be approved [EV-CAR-042]. CMS itself projects 2.3 million fewer Medicaid enrollees in FY2027 and $350.3 billion less federal spending over ten years [EV-CAR-043] (second Trump administration estimate). On October 1, 2026 five governors asked for a delay [EV-CAR-045]; California asked for an exemption through June 30, 2027, warning that 4.8 million members were at risk of wrongly losing coverage [EV-CAR-046].
- **CBO's estimate.** The law's Medicaid provisions cut federal outlays by $989.7 billion over FY2025-34 and add 7.5 million uninsured in 2034 [EV-CAR-025]; the work requirement alone saves $325.6 billion [EV-CAR-026]. CBO shows work requirements and six-month checks starting in 2027 and fully phased in by 2030 and 2029 [EV-CAR-023].

### 8.3 The 2025 law: the marketplaces

- From 2026 there is no credit for plans bought through an income-based special enrollment period, and no cap on repaying excess advance credits (71304, 71305) [EV-CAR-018].
- From 2027 the credit is limited to citizens, permanent residents, Cuban and Haitian entrants and Compact residents (71301) [EV-CAR-016].
- From 2028 no credit is paid before the exchange verifies income, status, coverage and residence (71303) [EV-CAR-017]. CBO reads this as ending automatic re-enrollment with advance credits; repealing that section alone would cover 900,000 more people [EV-CAR-050].
- Repealing all the law's marketplace sections would cover 2.8 million more people in 2030 at $271.9 billion over 2026-35 [EV-CAR-049].
- CMS's 2025 marketplace rule is partly stayed by a federal court; CBO assumes a two-year delay for the stayed parts [EV-CAR-047], and KFF notes further rules on hold in litigation [EV-CAR-048].

### 8.4 The combined baseline

- **Uninsured.** CBO projects 26.6 million in 2025 rising to 36.6 million in 2029 (10.4%) [EV-CAR-020]. Read from CBO's tables, the 2025 law alone adds 8.6 million uninsured in 2029 and 10 million by 2034 [EV-CAR-038]; with the credit expiry the total increase tops 14 million [EV-CAR-037].
- **Medicaid.** Expansion adults fall from 15.5 million to 11.1 million by 2029, and Medicaid and CHIP from 80.5 million to 74.3 million [EV-CAR-021].
- **Marketplaces.** Enrollment falls from 22.4 million in 2025 to 12.8 million in 2029, with subsidized enrollment down from 20.9 million to 10.0 million [EV-CAR-022].
- The legacy files expected uninsurance "toward 40 million"; CBO's 36.6 million for 2029 is the baseline used here.

### 8.5 Other law in force in 2029

- The February 3, 2026 appropriations law extended Medicare telehealth through December 31, 2027 [EV-CAR-032] and funded community health centers only through December 31, 2026 [EV-CAR-033]. Both are likely to be extended again before 2029; this dossier assumes the health centers' funding continues at its current level (a working assumption), and section 3 does not depend on it.
- Medicare physician payments got a one-year 2.5% increase for 2026 only [EV-CAR-019].

## What this means for the design

1. **Restoration is the floor, not the plan.** Reversing the 2025 law and the credit expiry would bring back roughly 14 million people [EV-CAR-037], but it would still leave about 27 million uninsured, the 2025 level [EV-CAR-100], and half of working-age adults avoiding care for cost [EV-CAR-110].
2. **Prices are the main cost lever.** Commercial hospital prices at 254% of Medicare [EV-CAR-131] are where a public option or single-payer saves money and where hospitals will fight hardest.
3. **Paperwork is a coverage killer.** The 2025 law's six-month checks, verification and work rules work mainly by removing people who are eligible (section 2: Arkansas and the unwinding). Any design under the design lens (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`) should make coverage automatic.
4. **Immigrant coverage diverges.** The 2025 law drops many lawfully present immigrants from Medicaid and the credits [EV-CAR-008] [EV-CAR-016]; noncitizens are already the least covered group [EV-CAR-103]. Who a new system covers is a shared rule (`dossiers/shared-rules.md`, rule 3) for section 3.
