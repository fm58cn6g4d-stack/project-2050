# Care you can count on, part D (the care workforce): what works

Role: section 2 of the care dossier, part D: the evidence on each lever for paying, staffing and supplying the care workforce, its strength, and where each has been tried. Status: reviewed at L3 (parts B-D, `review-milestone-bcd.md`; see the README). Bracketed tags point to `evidence.json`.

How strength is stated, strongest first:
- **randomized trial**;
- **quasi-experimental study** (a natural experiment: a law that changed in some places and not others);
- **program evaluation** (before and after, or against a comparison group that chose differently);
- **international practice**;
- **expert judgment** (including simulations).

"Not in register" marks a fact the project has not yet sourced.

## In brief

| Lever | Strength | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher pay raises retention and quality | Quasi-experimental; program evaluations | Consistent: higher minimum wages cut nursing home separations and deaths [EV-CAR-624]; home care retention rose from 39 to 74 percent after raises [EV-CAR-625]; DC's child care fund cut turnover [EV-CAR-550] |
| Money tied to pay through Medicaid rates | Program evaluation | Works while the money lasts. Most of ARPA's raises were not sustained [EV-CAR-628], unless a legislature made them permanent (Colorado) |
| A required share of payments for wages (pass-through) | Untested nationally | The 80% rule never took effect [EV-CAR-419] [EV-CAR-420]. Better Care Better Jobs asked only for a proportionate pass-through of increases [EV-CAR-617] |
| Wage boards and state bargaining | Program experience; no rigorous evaluation in the register | Minnesota and Washington set public wage floors funded through Medicaid rates [EV-CAR-618] [EV-CAR-620] [EV-CAR-621]; Nevada's board only recommended [EV-CAR-622] |
| Nursing home staffing minimums | Quasi-experimental | State minimums raised hours about 5% and cut severe citations, but lowered the skill mix [EV-CAR-607]; registered nurse hours drive quality, aide hours less clearly [EV-CAR-608] |
| Training programs | Randomized trials | Health training moved people into health jobs but not into higher earnings [EV-CAR-631]; sector programs that place people in better-paid jobs raised earnings 11-40 percent [EV-CAR-632] |
| Immigration | Quasi-experimental | Enforcement reduced nursing home direct care hours by about 2% [EV-CAR-635]; immigrants are 30% of direct care workers [EV-CAR-614] |

## 1. Pay

### Does pay change who stays and the care people get?

**Nursing homes, quasi-experimental (strong).**
- Ruffini uses state minimum wage changes as natural experiments.
- A ten percent higher minimum wage raised low-paid nursing home workers' earnings one to two percent, cut separations, and would prevent at least 15,000 deaths. It lowered inspection violations by one to two percent, with no significant change in profitability [EV-CAR-624].
- This is the strongest US evidence that pay is a quality lever, not only a fairness one.

**Home care, program evaluation (moderate).**
- In San Francisco's In-Home Supportive Services, after bargaining raised wages and added benefits, the annual retention rate of new providers rose from 39 percent to 74 percent.
- Howes estimates a $1 raise from $8 an hour adds 17 points of retention [EV-CAR-625].
- One county, and a before-and-after design, so selection and timing could explain part of it.

**Child care, program evaluation (moderate).**
- DC's pay equity fund raised educators to salaries of about $51,000-75,000, against an average of about $37,300 before.
- Participating centers had 37 percent turnover, against 51 percent at comparable centers without the raises [EV-CAR-550]. Centers chose to join, so selection may explain part of the gap.
- The military's child care reform tied pay rises to training, with large quality gains (RAND) [EV-CAR-551].

**Simulation (expert judgment).**
- LeadingAge's LTSS Center estimated that raising every direct care worker to a living wage in 2022 would give 75.3% of them a raise averaging 15.5%, at about $9.4 billion [EV-CAR-626].
- Its headline says lower turnover and higher productivity would offset "most, if not all" of the cost. Its own numbers put turnover savings at a little over 10% of the cost, so the offset rests on a productivity estimate. The project uses its cost and not its offset.

**What the evidence does not say.** It does not show at what level a floor stops buying retention, or how much of a raise employers pass on when the floor is set through rates rather than wages.

### Ways to get money to wages

**Raising rates with conditions.** This is the ARPA and Better Care Better Jobs route.
- *Tried:* ARPA's 10-point match increase for one year, 2021-22. States planned $37.1 billion of reinvestment, mostly for the workforce [EV-CAR-425].
- *Result:* temporary money gave temporary raises. About one-third of workforce activities were sustained [EV-CAR-628], and five states planned to end their increases [EV-CAR-627].
- *The exception:* Colorado legislated its base wage, from $12.41 to nearly $18 an hour [EV-CAR-628].
- *Strength:* program evaluation.

**A required share of payments for compensation (the 80% rule).**
- *Design:* the 2024 access rule requires 80% of Medicaid payments for three home care services to go to direct care workers' compensation from July 2030 [EV-CAR-419]. States may set a separate level for small providers and exempt some providers [EV-CAR-636].
- *Status:* it was never tried, and CMS plans to rescind or revise it [EV-CAR-420].
- *Strength:* none yet.
- *Risk:* a share rule controls how a payment is split, not how large the payment is. If rates stay low, 80% of a low rate is still a low wage. Small agencies with high fixed costs are the ones the exceptions exist for.

**A wage floor written into the rate.** This is the Minnesota and Washington route.
- *Minnesota:* the Nursing Home Workforce Standards Board, with workers, employers and the public, set minimum wages by job. Certified nursing assistants get $22.50 an hour from September 10, 2026 and $1.50 more on January 1, 2027, raising pay for about 76% of them. The standards took effect only once CMS approved the Medicaid rate change that funds them [EV-CAR-618].
- *California:* a statutory health care minimum wage reaches $25 for large systems. It leaves free-standing nursing homes out until a minimum spending requirement exists [EV-CAR-619], a pass-through condition in another form.
- *Nevada:* its home care board recommended a $25 Medicaid rate and a $15 floor [EV-CAR-622].
- *Strength:* program experience only. None is evaluated in the register.
- *The common feature:* the payer funds the floor in the rate at the same time it sets it.

**Bargaining with the state as employer of record.**
- *Washington:* the governor is the public employer of individual providers for bargaining, in one statewide unit [EV-CAR-620]. The 2025-27 agreement starts at $22.52 an hour, rising to $23.68 by January 2027 [EV-CAR-621]. California's IHSS uses county public authorities (not in register).
- *Strength:* program experience; Howes's San Francisco study is the main evidence [EV-CAR-625].
- *Legal limit:* bargaining is lawful, but workers cannot be required to pay agency fees [EV-CAR-623], so units depend on voluntary membership.
- *Sectoral bargaining across private employers* (the legacy LAB-3B): no US statutory precedent outside the old NIRA codes (`legacy/domains/domain-labor.md`). Its home is international practice: industry-wide awards and agreements in Australia and much of Europe (not in register).

### Child care pay parity

- *The gap:* about $13 an hour for early educators against about $32 for elementary teachers [EV-CAR-629].
- *Tried:* DC's fund is the nearest US test of parity-level pay outside schools [EV-CAR-550]. It has been cut and is funded only through FY2027 [EV-CAR-630].
- *Designs:* the Child Care for Working Families Act requires rates that pay a living wage and parity with elementary educators [EV-CAR-581]. Public pre-K already pays more where it sits in schools [EV-CAR-521].
- *Strength:* program evaluation (DC), plus the military system [EV-CAR-551].

## 2. Staffing standards in nursing homes

**What minimums do: quasi-experimental (strong).**
- Chen and Grabowski find state minimums raised total nursing hours about 5% and reduced severe deficiency citations. But because they counted all direct care staff alike, homes substituted aides for licensed nurses [EV-CAR-607].
- Lin, using eight states' 2000-2001 laws, finds registered nurse hours have a large effect on quality and nurse aide hours no significant one [EV-CAR-608].
- The design lesson is that a minimum should name registered nurse time, not only total hours.

**What CMS's study found: observational and simulation.** Quality rises with staffing with no plateau or cliff; registered nurse staffing matters most. Moving from a 3.30 to a 3.88 minimum adds 6 points to the share of homes predicted above median quality [EV-CAR-606]. That is a modest gain for a large increase, and it argues for phasing.

**How far homes are from the 2024 standards:** see section 1, part 4 [EV-CAR-604] [EV-CAR-605].

**Why the 2024 rule failed.**
- *Legal:* two federal courts vacated the hours and round-the-clock nurse requirements, the Texas court holding on the major questions doctrine that CMS exceeded its authority [EV-CAR-602]. The Iowa court's reading, that the round-the-clock rule conflicts with the statute's eight-hour floor, is from its opinion, which the register does not hold. Congress then barred it until 2034 [EV-CAR-416], and CMS repealed it [EV-CAR-603].
- *Money:* the rule's $43.0 billion over ten years came with no new federal payment [EV-CAR-601], so it read as an unfunded mandate.
- *Lesson:* a standard needs a statute and the money to meet it.

**States.** New York requires 3.5 hours per resident day by statute (not in register); enforcement has been weak (KFF Health News, 2024; not in register).

## 3. Training and supply

**Training programs: randomized trials (strong).**
- *HPOG:* HHS's grants trained low-income adults for health jobs. Six years on, access to the program raised health care employment from 32 to 37 percent and credentials from 70 to 78 percent, but did not detectably raise earnings [EV-CAR-631]. Most training was short, for jobs like nursing assistant.
- *Sector programs:* those that screen applicants, train for specific better-paid jobs and place people raised earnings 11 to 40 percent [EV-CAR-632].
- *Lesson:* training moves people into care jobs, and pay decides whether those jobs pay. Training without a wage floor fills the entry level and keeps losing workers from it.

**Apprenticeship: data only.** 41,311 registered apprentices served in health and human services in FY2025, up 47 percent in five years [EV-CAR-633]. No count for direct care or early educators, and no evaluation, is in the register.

**Credentials in child care.** Part C chose support for credentials over a hard degree deadline (`3c-design-child-care.md`, point 6), because Tennessee's certified teachers still produced harm [EV-CAR-559] while Boston's gains came with coaching [EV-CAR-557].

**Physicians.** Medicare funds most residency slots. The bipartisan Resident Physician Shortage Reduction Act would add 2,000 slots a year for FY2027-2033, 14,000 in all [EV-CAR-634]. It is the source of legacy HLT-4's figure.

## 4. Immigration

- **The share.** 30% of direct care workers are immigrants, 33% in home care [EV-CAR-614].
- **Enforcement.** Secure Communities reduced nursing homes' direct care hours per resident day by 0.073, about 2% (quasi-experimental) [EV-CAR-635].
- **Current policy.** Intensified enforcement and restrictive policies could reduce immigration and worsen shortages [EV-CAR-615].
- **Proposals.** The legacy plan included a care-worker visa tied to child care and long-term care (`legacy/domains/domain-immigration.md`, IMM-2). That is the immigration flagship's to decide (shared rule 8).

## 5. Failures to avoid

1. **Temporary money for a permanent job.** ARPA's raises faded with the money [EV-CAR-628].
2. **A mandate without the payment.** The 2024 staffing rule [EV-CAR-601] [EV-CAR-603].
3. **A standard that counts all hours alike.** Homes substitute cheaper staff [EV-CAR-607].
4. **A share rule without a rate floor.** 80% of a low rate (judgment, from section 1's rates [EV-CAR-627]).
5. **Training without pay.** It does not raise earnings [EV-CAR-631].
6. **Demand before supply.** Free care at home or in child care with no plan for workers turns into waiting lists. Part C cites Canada's waitlists [EV-CAR-539]; health cites CBO's demand-outruns-supply finding [EV-CAR-219].
