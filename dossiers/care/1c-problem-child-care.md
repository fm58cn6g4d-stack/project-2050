# Care you can count on, part C (child care and pre-K): the problem

Role: section 1 of the care dossier, part C. It covers what is wrong with child care for children from birth to school age and with pre-K, for whom, how large the problem is, and how the law will stand in January 2029. Status: reviewed at L3 (parts B-D, `review-milestone-bcd.md`; see the README). Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`, EV-CAR-500 to 599 for part C).

**What this part covers.**
- *Child care:* paid care and early education for children from birth until they start kindergarten. It may be given in centers, family child care homes or Head Start, while parents work, study or look for work.
- *Pre-K:* public preschool for 3- and 4-year-olds in the year or two before kindergarten.
- *Not covered here:*
  - school-age care before and after school;
  - kindergarten and K-12 (education);
  - the Child Tax Credit and paid leave (the secure floor, flagship 1);
  - health care for children (health's decided plan covers every child from 2031, HLT-7.03);
  - workforce wages, training and supply, which are part D (shown here as separate lines only).

Outcomes this section frames:
- OUT-08, time and practical freedom (parents' work and time);
- OUT-01, child poverty (child care costs and parents' earnings);
- OUT-03, care people can count on (for the youngest).

## In brief

- **Care costs as much as rent, and help reaches few.**
  - *Prices.* Full-day care for one child cost families 8.9% to 16.0% of median family income in 2022, at $6,552 to $15,600 a year (Department of Labor, Women's Bureau, 2024) [EV-CAR-514].
  - *Subsidies.* Federal law makes 11.8 million children eligible for help, and 1.8 million, 16 percent, get it (ASPE, 2026) [EV-CAR-507].
- **Federal money doubled, and the number of children served did not move.**
  - *Funding.* The block grant rose from $2.435 billion in FY2015 to $8.746 billion in FY2024.
  - *Children served.* The monthly average stayed near 1.3 to 1.4 million (CRS, 2024) [EV-CAR-508].
  - *Why.* The money went into higher payment rates and the 2014 law's health, safety and eligibility rules, not into more children.
- **Pre-K is half-built.** State pre-K enrolls 37% of 4-year-olds and 9% of 3-year-olds (NIEER, 2026) [EV-CAR-519]. Head Start was funded for 715,873 children and pregnant women in FY2024 (Office of Head Start) [EV-CAR-512].
- **The workers are paid poverty-level wages.**
  - *Child care workers.* The median wage is $16.82 an hour (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-516].
  - *Child care against school-based pre-K.* Child care educators earn about $29,570 a year against $53,200 in school-based pre-K (Markowitz and Bassok) [EV-CAR-521].
  - *Effect.* Programs cannot hire, and any pre-K expansion pulls staff upward.
- **The 2029 baseline is weaker than today's.**
  - The 2024 rule's 7% copayment cap and pay-by-enrollment are rescinded from July 2026 (HHS, 2026) [EV-CAR-510].
  - The pandemic money ended in 2023-24 [EV-CAR-509].
  - The 2025 law's changes are tax benefits for employers and for families who owe income tax [EV-CAR-500] [EV-CAR-501] [EV-CAR-502] [EV-CAR-503].

## 1. Who needs care, and how much

- **Children.** About 18.5 million children are under 5: 5.4% of 341.8 million people (Census Bureau, 2025) [EV-CAR-520]. That is roughly 3.7 million in each year of age (project arithmetic).
- **Parents at work.** 68.0 percent of women with children under 6 were in the labor force in 2025, against 95.3 percent of men (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-518]. Most young children have every parent at work or looking for it, so the question is not whether they are cared for by someone other than a parent, but by whom, at what price and at what quality.
- **What section 3 needs from this.** How many families would use paid care at a low price is the largest uncertainty in the cost. Section 3 uses it as a working assumption with a range, not a measured count ("Evidence still needed").

## 2. What families pay

- **Prices.** In 2022, families paid 8.9% to 16.0% of the county median family income for full-day care for one child, $6,552 to $15,600 a year, depending on age, setting and county size (National Database of Childcare Prices, via the Women's Bureau, 2024) [EV-CAR-514]. Child Care Aware of America, an advocacy group, puts the 2025 national average at $13,184 [EV-CAR-515].
- **Infants cost most.** Infant rooms need the most staff per child, so the price is highest when parents' earnings are lowest and many are on unpaid leave or just back at work. Paid leave (FAM-3, the secure floor) covers the first weeks. Nothing covers the years from then to school.
- **The tax code helps families who owe tax.** The child and dependent care credit is nonrefundable, so families who owe little income tax get little or nothing (Tax Policy Center, 2025) [EV-CAR-503]. The 2025 law raised its rate but did not make it refundable [EV-CAR-502].

## 3. Who gets help today

- **Subsidies reach one eligible child in six.**
  - *Coverage.* In 2022, 11.8 million children were eligible under federal rules and 1.8 million received subsidies, 16 percent (20 percent under states' stricter rules) (ASPE, second Trump administration, 2026) [EV-CAR-507].
  - *The counts.* The 1.8 million is an administrative count. The 11.8 million is a microsimulation, and earlier editions under other administrations gave similar shares.
  - *HHS's own count.* About 1.6 million children from 994,000 families were served each month in FY2023 [EV-CAR-511].
- **More money, the same number of children.**
  - *The money.* Block-grant appropriations rose from $2.435 billion (FY2015) to $8.746 billion (FY2024).
  - *Children served.* 1.394 million (FY2015), 1.429 million (FY2019) and 1.314 million (FY2021) (CRS, 2024) [EV-CAR-508].
  - *The 2014 law.* It set 12-month eligibility and new health, safety and inspection rules [EV-CAR-585]. Without matching money, those raised the cost per child. CLASP, an advocacy group, counts 373,100 fewer children served in 2015 than in 2006 [EV-CAR-586], a decline that began before the law.
  - *Lesson for section 3.* Better rules without money serve fewer children.
- **Head Start.**
  - *Size and money.* It is funded for 715,873 children and pregnant women (FY2024) [EV-CAR-512], with $12.4 billion in FY2026 [EV-CAR-504].
  - *How it works.* It runs federal-to-local, outside states' systems, with health, family and nutrition services.
- **State pre-K.**
  - *Reach and spending.* In 2024-25, state pre-K reached 37% of 4-year-olds and 9% of 3-year-olds, at about $8,124 a child of state money and $14.4 billion in all (NIEER, 2026) [EV-CAR-519].
  - *Unevenness.* Reach and quality vary widely: a few states are near universal, and some have no program.

## 4. Supply and the workforce

- **Pay.** Child care workers' median wage was $16.82 an hour in May 2025, about $34,980 a year at full time, with about 1.0 million jobs (BLS, 2026) [EV-CAR-516]. Preschool teachers' median was $38,140 [EV-CAR-517]. Child care educators earned about $29,570 in 2022, Head Start staff $35,819 and school-based pre-K teachers $53,200 (Markowitz and Bassok) [EV-CAR-521].
- **Why it matters here.** Programs fund staff from what families can pay. Wages stay low, turnover is high and slots for infants, which need the most staff, are scarcest. A program that raises demand without raising pay will find the workers are not there. That is part D's question, and section 3 shows wage lines separately.
- **What the pandemic money showed.** ARPA's $23.975 billion of stabilization grants paid programs' operating costs directly, and had to be spent by September 30, 2023 (CRS, 2023) [EV-CAR-509]. The grants ended, and with them the main federal experiment in paying for supply rather than per child.

## 5. The baseline in January 2029

Current law as it will stand when the plan starts, counting enacted laws with later effective dates and expirations (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`).

**The 2025 reconciliation law (Public Law 119-21): three permanent tax changes, effective 2026.**
- *Employer-provided child care credit (sec. 70401, IRC 45F).* 40% of qualified expenses, 50% for small businesses, up to $500,000 ($600,000 for small businesses), up from 25% and $150,000 [EV-CAR-500]. JCT puts it at about $0.7 billion over ten years [EV-CAR-503].
- *Dependent care assistance exclusion (sec. 70404, IRC 129).* $7,500, up from $5,000, not indexed [EV-CAR-501]. About $6.0 billion over ten years (JCT) [EV-CAR-503].
- *Child and dependent care credit (sec. 70405, IRC 21).* A 50% top rate falling to 35% and then 20% above $75,000 of AGI ($150,000 joint), on the same $3,000 and $6,000 expense limits. It is still nonrefundable and not indexed [EV-CAR-502]. $9.257 billion over FY2025-34 (JCT, via the Tax Policy Center) [EV-CAR-503].
- *What they do.* They help employers and taxpaying families buy care that exists, about $1.6 billion a year in all. They do not build slots or reach families who owe no tax. They came from the bipartisan Britt-Kaine bill, without its refundability [EV-CAR-583] [EV-CAR-503]. They are part of the 2029 baseline and stay unless the plan changes them.

**Block-grant money.**
- *Discretionary CCDBG.* $8.8 billion in FY2026, $85 million more than FY2025 [EV-CAR-504]. The House committee's FY2027 bill gives $8.84 billion [EV-CAR-506].
- *The mandatory entitlement.* The Child Care Entitlement to States is permanent at $3.550 billion a year [EV-CAR-505]. All CCDF funding in FY2026 is $12.381 billion [EV-CAR-511].
- *Working assumption for 2029.* CCDF stays near $12.5-13 billion, roughly flat in real terms. The range runs from a cut of a few hundred million dollars to a modest increase, set by appropriations in 2027-28.

**The 2024 rule is gone.**
- *What HHS rescinded.* HHS's May 2026 rule, effective July 13, 2026, removes the federal requirements to cap copayments at 7 percent of income, to pay providers by enrollment and in advance, and to fund some care through grants or contracts. States may keep those policies [EV-CAR-510].
- *For 2029.* The federal floor reverts to the rules in force before 2024. Copayments, payment practices and grants are state choices.

**Head Start.**
- *Money.* $12.4 billion in FY2026 [EV-CAR-504] and $12.37 billion in the House FY2027 bill [EV-CAR-506].
- *The immigrant-eligibility directive.* In July 2025 HHS reinterpreted the 1996 welfare law to make Head Start a "federal public benefit", which would bar many immigrant children. A federal court enjoined the directive, and the government dropped its appeal in December 2025 [EV-CAR-513]. The litigation continues.
- *Working assumption for 2029.* Head Start is funded near today's level in real terms, with its eligibility rules as before July 2025. The risk of a cut or a new eligibility rule before 2029 is real but not measured.

**The pandemic money is spent.** ARPA's stabilization grants ended in 2023 and its supplemental block-grant money in 2024 [EV-CAR-509].

**Health and the secure floor in the plan.**
- *Health.* Health's decided path enrolls every child in the single plan from 2031 (HLT-7.03). Child care is outside it.
- *The secure floor.* The Child Tax Credit (FAM-1) and paid leave (FAM-3) are the secure floor's. Paid leave shortens the months families need infant care. The Child Tax Credit adds money, but not slots.

## 6. What this means for the design

- **Money without supply raises prices; supply without money is unaffordable.** Every option in section 3 has to pay for slots and pay families' share at once. The block grant's record of more money and the same children served [EV-CAR-508] is the warning.
- **Pre-K and infant care are one market.** A program that pays well for 4-year-olds draws staff and revenue away from infant rooms (section 2, part 5). Section 3 decides them together.
- **The baseline asks more of the plan.** With the 2024 rule rescinded [EV-CAR-510], the protections families had in 2025 are not in 2029's law. The plan has to put them back in statute if it wants them.
- **Wages decide quality and supply.** Part D owns them. Each option here is priced without a new wage floor, with the wage line beside it.

## Evidence still needed

- **How many families would use paid care at a low price.** Today's arrangements by age and family income would anchor section 3's take-up assumption, the largest cost uncertainty: the Census Bureau's SIPP child care tables and the National Survey of Early Care and Education (NSECE).
- **Supply.** Licensed slots by age and county ("child care deserts"), for example from the NSECE or the Center for American Progress's maps, to size the facilities need.
- **Head Start's FY2025-26 funded enrollment,** split between Head Start and Early Head Start.
- **Census's count of children under 5** by single year of age, from the Census file rather than QuickFacts (blocked to automated download).
- **The Federal Register's text of the May 2026 rule,** saved from an upload (the site blocks automated downloads).

Claims this section leaves out for lack of evidence: the number of providers that closed after the stabilization grants ended, and how many states have kept the 2024 rule's policies since July 2026 (one secondary source says 31 states and DC cap copayments at 7% or less; not verified).
