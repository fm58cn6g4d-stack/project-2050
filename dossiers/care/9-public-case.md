# Care you can count on, part A (health): the public case

Role: section 9 of the care dossier, health part: a plain-language brief, messages, hard questions and a can-say list, built only from sections 1-8 and never ahead of them. Status: draft (stage D, step 4, PR 2), on `DEC-2026-11-10-09`, `-11`, `-12` and `-13`; revised after the milestone review (`review-milestone.md`, step 3). Every number traces to its section and evidence ID in the source table at the end. Sources are named in plain text before each tag. Project estimates say "on our estimates" or "about".

## The brief

**Health care you can count on, for everyone, by 2035.**

In 2029, about 36.6 million Americans will have no health insurance (Congressional Budget Office, 2026) [EV-CAR-020]. Many more have insurance that doesn't protect them: half of working-age adults delayed or skipped care because of cost in 2026 (Commonwealth Fund, 2026) [EV-CAR-110], and 29% of people with job-based coverage were underinsured (Commonwealth Fund, 2026) [EV-CAR-109]. A family plan at work cost $28,303 in 2026 (KFF, 2026) [EV-CAR-111]. Medical bills were 58% of debts in collections on credit records, the most common kind on credit records in 2021 (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2022) [EV-CAR-117].

We pay more and get less. The country spent $5.3 trillion on health care in 2024, 18.0% of the economy (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2026) [EV-CAR-120]. On the OECD's comparable measure the U.S. spends 17.2% against an average of 9.3% across its member countries, and Americans live 2.7 fewer years than the OECD average (OECD, 2025) [EV-CAR-125] [EV-CAR-126]. The gap is mostly prices, not how much care Americans use (Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, 2025) [EV-CAR-129]: employer plans paid hospitals 254% of what Medicare pays for the same care (RAND, 2024) [EV-CAR-131].

**The plan.** One public plan for everyone who lives here, built in steps the law sets in advance:
- **2029:** undo the 2025 cuts to Medicaid and marketplace coverage, so people who lost coverage get it back.
- **January 1, 2031**, unless Congress votes to delay it: everyone uninsured, everyone who buys their own insurance, and every child is enrolled automatically. People 60 to 64 can join Medicare. People already on Medicare get a $5,000 yearly cap on what they pay and hearing and vision coverage, with dental from 2033.
- **2033 to 2035:** people on Medicaid and people with job-based coverage move in, about a third each year.
- **January 1, 2035:** everyone is in.

**What you get.** Hospital, doctor, prescriptions, mental health and addiction care, maternity, reproductive care including abortion, dental, vision and hearing. No premiums, no deductibles, no networks. No charge at all for primary care, prevention, maternity, mental health or chronic-condition drugs, and none for anyone under twice the poverty line. Above that, small copays for other care, capped at about $1,000 a year on our working figure.

**How it's paid for.** On our estimates, at full scale, households would stop paying about $1.5 trillion a year in premiums and out-of-pocket costs and employers about $1.5 trillion in premiums, while the country spends about $200 billion less on health care in all. The money already in the system keeps flowing: Medicare's taxes, federal Medicaid money, a share from states, and a contribution from employers until a new payroll contribution replaces it. New taxes fill the rest, set in the plan's financing settlement, which is designed so that households below the top come out ahead. The new taxes would be large: on our estimates about $2.4 trillion a year at full scale, in 2032 terms (about $3.6 trillion in FY2040, in that year's dollars). That is on top of the employers' contribution, which runs until the settlement's payroll contribution replaces it, and the income and payroll tax on the former premiums that become wages.

**What it protects.** No one loses coverage before the new plan covers them. Workers in insurance and billing get pay, training and first claim on the plan's jobs. Rural and safety-net hospitals get fixed yearly budgets, and a capped transition fund helps hospitals that lose revenue from 2033 to 2038, rural, safety-net and sole-community hospitals first. Veterans keep VA care, military families keep TRICARE, and Native Americans keep the Indian Health Service, and all of them can also use the plan.

## The case in one paragraph

Americans pay the most in the world for health care and still go without it. By 2035 everyone who lives here would have one public plan, with no premiums or deductibles, enrolled automatically, step by step: the uninsured and every child first in 2031, then Medicaid and job-based coverage, then everyone. Hospitals would be paid a fair rate, a little above Medicare's, instead of the 254% employer plans pay now (RAND, 2024) [EV-CAR-131]. On our estimates the country would spend less in total, about $200 billion a year at full scale, and the money households and employers spend on premiums would be replaced by taxes designed to leave most families ahead.

## Messages

- **Everyone in, nobody out.** One plan, automatic, no forms, no premiums.
- **Children first.** Every child is covered from January 1, 2031, unless Congress votes to delay it.
- **Seniors don't wait.** People on Medicare get a cap on costs and hearing and vision from 2031, and dental from 2033.
- **No more surprise bills.** No deductibles or networks; the most you'd pay in copays is capped.
- **Step by step, in law.** Each step happens on its date unless Congress votes to stop it.
- **Fair prices, not blank checks.** Hospitals are paid about 1.15 times Medicare's rates, after five years to adjust.

## Hard questions, plain answers

**Will my taxes go up?** Yes, for most people, but premiums, deductibles and copays go away or mostly away. On our estimates the new taxes come to about $2.4 trillion a year at full scale (2032 terms), on top of the employers' contribution and the tax on former premiums that become wages. Households would stop paying about $1.5 trillion a year in premiums and out-of-pocket costs, and employers about $1.5 trillion in premiums. The financing settlement sets the taxes; its rule is that households below the top come out ahead. That is a design target, not yet a result: it holds only once the settlement's taxes are set.

**Can I keep my plan?** Your job-based plan continues until your employer's group moves, between 2033 and 2035. After January 1, 2035, private insurance can cover extras the plan doesn't, but not the same care.

**Will I wait longer for care?** Waits may grow at first, as the Congressional Budget Office expects for any single-payer plan (Congressional Budget Office, 2020) [EV-CAR-219]. The plan starts with smaller groups to test it, keeps a small copay where care is scarcest, and pays doctors and hospitals above Medicare's rates.

**Will my hospital close?** Rural and safety-net hospitals get fixed yearly budgets. On our estimates, hospitals as a whole end up with about the same revenue, within a few percent: most of what they lose on high-priced private patients they gain from patients who were on Medicaid, on Medicare or uninsured. A capped transition fund, about $100 billion over 2033-38, helps hospitals that do lose, rural, safety-net and sole-community hospitals first, and holds rural and safety-net hospitals' budgets at least at their old revenue for three years.

**What about abortion?** The plan covers reproductive care, including abortion, and ends the Hyde Amendment's limits on federal funding.

**What if a later Congress stops it?** Each step is written into the 2029 law and happens on its date unless Congress votes to delay it, and no one loses coverage before the plan covers them.

## Stories (composite illustrations, not real people)

- A restaurant worker in her 40s, uninsured since the 2025 cuts, is enrolled from her tax return on January 1, 2031, and sees a doctor that month without a bill.
- A retired teacher on Medicare hits the $5,000 cap in 2031 after a hip replacement and pays nothing more that year; in 2035 the cap falls to the plan's.
- A small business owner with 12 workers stops buying insurance in 2033 and pays a contribution with her payroll taxes instead, until the new payroll contribution replaces it.
