# Care you can count on, part B (long-term care: support at home and in nursing homes for older and disabled people): design choices

Role: section 3 of the care dossier, part B: the real forks, the options weighed with their evidence, a recommendation for each, one decision memo per fork in `memos/` (memos 6-9), and a numbered list of narrower yes-or-no points. Status: draft (stage D, step 5, revised in step 5b after the independent audit, `review-part-b-evidence.md`); **decided by Espen, November 11, 2026 (`DEC-2026-11-11-02`): as recommended, except memo 7 part 1, which is C, three daily activities, with the hold-harmless kept**. Costs repriced on that threshold in step 8 (`DEC-2026-11-11-05`), with the states' share set out for the financing settlement; further cost options are in memo 17 (board question 0n). It builds on health's decided path: a staged path to one public plan, with every resident covered by January 1, 2035 (`DEC-2026-11-10-09`, `-11`, `-12`, `-13`, `DEC-2026-11-11-01`). Under that path long-term care stays in Medicaid until this part decides (`4-specification.md`, HLT-7.07). Sources are named in plain text in the points and memos; bracketed tags point to `evidence.json`.

**What part B covers.** Long-term services and supports: help with daily living, at home, in the community and in nursing homes and similar institutions, for older adults and for children and working-age adults with disabilities, and how it is insured. Child care is part C; the care workforce is part D. (The earlier name, "care at home for all ages", read as if it took in child care and left out nursing homes.)

## How to read this section

- **One batched set.** Four memos and ten yes-or-no points, so Espen can answer once. Memo 6 shapes the rest; memos 7-9 say how each answer applies under each of memo 6's options. Each is decided on its own, and none is adopted by silence.
- **Evidence strength is stated** for every recommendation: randomized trial, quasi-experimental study, program evaluation, international practice or expert judgment, with the audit's verdict.
- **Legacy designs are options** (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`).
  - The October 26 legacy direction asked for an all-age home-support benefit (`legacy/domains/domain-health.md`, line 137). It specified protection against prolonged intensive-care costs, assessed help without asset spend-down, a genuine paid-family-caregiver option, and preservation of stronger Medicaid services, compared with enhanced Medicaid and catastrophic-only insurance. It appears here as memo 6 B, weighed against both.
  - The legacy HLT-4 "HCBS down payment" (enhanced Medicaid matching plus workforce support) is memo 6 A and the bridge in point 1.
- **Health's working position on institutional care is an option, not a given.** S. 1506 keeps institutional long-term care in Medicaid (sec. 204) [EV-CAR-278], and so does health's HLT-7.07 for now. Memo 8 weighs keeping it there against moving all or part of it into the plan.
- **Report, don't gate** (`DEC-2026-11-10-05`). Each option's cost is reported for the financing settlement as a working assumption with its basis and range (`DEC-2026-11-10-07`), benchmarked against outside estimates; nothing is trimmed to fit. Memo 6 is mainly about money and reaching people, so it comes to Espen with its cost. The budget model books part B only after sections 4-6.
- **Design lens** (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`). Each option says what it asks of people: forms, tests, waiting.

## The baseline in 2029

Section 1, part 6, gives the full baseline:
- **Medicaid.** Medicaid is the only broad payer, after spend-down to SSI's $2,000 asset limit [EV-CAR-409]. Home care is mostly an optional waiver service with enrollment caps, so almost 650,000 people wait 34 months on average [EV-HOU-691].
- **The 2025 law.** It cuts retroactive coverage from 2027 [EV-CAR-418], caps home equity at $1,000,000 from 2028 [EV-CAR-415], adds a lower-need waiver option from July 2028 [EV-CAR-414], and suspends nursing-home staffing standards to September 2034 [EV-CAR-416].
- **Expirations.** Money Follows the Person and the home-care spousal protections expire after FY2027 [EV-HOU-697].
- **The 2024 access rule.** Its 80% worker-pay rule (from July 2030) and waiting-list reporting (from 2027) may be rescinded [EV-CAR-419] [EV-CAR-420].
- **Health's plan.** Medicaid enrollees join the single plan in 2033-35, but their long-term care stays in Medicaid (HLT-7.07).

## What the options cost

These are working assumptions with their basis and range (`DEC-2026-11-10-07`), not a model run. They are measured **against current law, with HLT-7.07 honored**: today's Medicaid long-term care stays in Medicaid, and states keep paying their share of it. Health's booking keeps Medicaid's long-term care in Medicaid at current-law cost (board row D-4c; `6-cost.md`, part 1), and the coverage model's own long-term care line is zero, so these figures are also the increment over the booking. The withdrawn readings against the earlier booking (about $142 billion for memo 6 B, $25-45 billion for memo 8 C) are not used.

**The basis (project arithmetic; judgment where marked).**
- **Medicaid spending.** Medicaid long-term care was $257.0 billion in 2023 (CRS, 2025) [EV-CAR-404], 63.8% of it for home care (CMS, 2025) [EV-CAR-406]: about $164 billion for home care and $93 billion for institutions. CMS's T-MSIS count gives $129.4 billion for home care in 2022 [EV-CAR-407]; the gap between the two totals (about 27%) is in the range.
- **Growth (working assumption).** Spending grows 5.5% a year in nominal terms, range 4.5-6.5%, from wage growth and a population 85 and older that nearly doubles by 2040 [EV-CAR-403]. Medicaid home care reaches about $312 billion in 2035. Cost per user grows about 4% a year, with wages (judgment).
- **States' share and maintenance of effort.** States pay about 47 percent [EV-CAR-448]. They keep paying half of what they now spend on whatever moves to the plan, as health's working figure for the rest of Medicaid (`4-specification.md`, HLT-7.17; range 0-80%, set by the financing settlement).
- **New users (judgment, repriced at three activities in step 8).** About 3.9 million people (range 2.8-5.6 million) of every age in 2035, not now served by Medicaid, who meet memo 7's decided threshold of three daily activities or the equivalent and use paid care. They include the almost 650,000 people on waiting lists [EV-HOU-691], who are not now served, so the waiting lists have no separate line under B.
  - *At two activities (the earlier recommendation):* 5-8 million (central 6.5 million). ASPE counts 7.2 million older adults with significant disabilities in 2020 [EV-CAR-402], against about 3.2 million Medicaid home-care users 65 and over (40.5% of 7.8 million) [EV-CAR-408] [EV-CAR-407], so about 4 million older adults are not served. Younger adults and children who meet the test but are not served add 1-2 million (judgment). The population 85 and over nearly doubles by 2040 [EV-CAR-403]. Urban's model of a similar design serves 10.0 million people in 2020 [EV-CAR-483].
  - *How many qualify at two and at three:*

    | Measure | Two activities or the equivalent | Three activities or the equivalent |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Older adults meeting the test, 2011 | 5.7 million, all settings (ASPE, NHATS) [EV-CAR-482] | Nearly 3 million outside nursing homes got help with three or more self-care or mobility activities (Freedman and Spillman, NHATS) [EV-CAR-641], plus most nursing-home residents: 84% have three or more limitations (CBO, HRS) [EV-CAR-647]. So about two-thirds of the two-activity group, as a rough bracket: the surveys and activity lists differ |
    | Who already gets help | About 45% of older people with one or two limitations get any help | About 85% [EV-CAR-642]; 24% are on Medicaid, about five times the rate of people without limitations [EV-CAR-643] |
    | Today's Medicaid home-care users at the line | All, under each state's level of care | From about a third in a California sample [EV-CAR-645] to about 95% under Washington's definitions [EV-CAR-646]; the hold-harmless keeps everyone served on December 31, 2034 |
    | WA Cares' experience | — | The line since benefits began in July 2026 [EV-CAR-497]; too new for claims data |
    | **New users in 2035 (judgment)** | **6.5 million (5-8 million)** | **3.9 million (2.8-5.6 million)**: 55-70% of the two-activity count, below the population's two-thirds because people at three activities are likelier to be served already |

  - *Cost each:* $31,000-83,000 in 2035 dollars (central about $50,000), about a fifth above the two-activity figure ($28,000-66,000, central $42,000), because the lightest-need tier drops out: people with three or more limitations get about 9 hours of help a day, paid and unpaid [EV-CAR-644] (judgment, a lift of 12-25%). The two-activity figure's low end is Medicaid's own 2022 average of about $16,600 per home-care user [EV-CAR-407], grown with wages. Its high end is Urban's $40,900 average for 2020 [EV-CAR-483], grown with wages and reduced about a tenth for memo 7 B's tier budgets and memo 4 C's capped copays. People who now manage on family care alone are often those with the heaviest needs [EV-CAR-448], and Cash and Counseling found paid hours rose when relatives could be paid [EV-CAR-486].
  - *The line:* about $197 billion (range $86-460 billion), against $275 billion at two activities.
- **Below the line after 2034 (judgment).** The hold-harmless covers everyone on Medicaid home care on December 31, 2034. People who would later have entered Medicaid home care below three activities, in states whose level of care is lower or under the 2025 law's lower-need waiver [EV-CAR-414], are not covered. That lowers spending against current law by a share of the Medicaid home-care line that grows to about 6% (3-10%) by FY2040, net of people whose needs grow past the line: about $0.04 trillion over 2035-39 and $24 billion in FY2040, counted in the totals below. Memo 17 asks whether to keep a state option for them (question 0n, 1).
- **Richer budgets for today's users (new piece, judgment).** Today's Medicaid users move to tier budgets indexed to wages, with the hold-harmless clause (memo 7). About 5-15% more spending on them, about $30 billion (range $15-45 billion) in 2035.

- *Opting out.* A state cannot opt its residents out of the plan's benefit, so people are protected either way. The risk is fiscal: a state that challenges or withholds the payment shifts its share to the federal government. HLT-7.17's rule applies: if part B brings new federal money for states' long-term care, the payment may instead become a condition on that money. The bridge's match points are such money in 2030-34, and their entry condition already requires states to keep their own spending (point 1).
- *Recommendation: 50%.* It is health's figure, so states face one rule. A higher share buys no state effort once states run nothing, and it enlarges the payment a court could strike. If the financing settlement raises health's share, part B's should move with it (memo 17, option 7).
- *The bridge's wage floor* (2030-34) is part D's line: the recommended share is each state's bridge rate rather than 90% (`3d-design-workforce.md`).

**Patterns** (the audit's reading). Bipartisan bills either insure catastrophic need through Social Security (WISH) or give tax help (Credit for Caring, SSI limits). Democratic bills either strengthen Medicaid (Better Care Better Jobs, the HCBS Access Act) or federalize all of it (H.R. 3069). The recommended set sits between S. 1506 and H.R. 3069, takes its threshold from the bipartisan bills, and borrows the Medicaid bills' mechanism for the bridge. CLASS's failure (voluntary, premium-funded, repealed in 2013) is why every option here is universal [EV-CAR-476] [EV-CAR-424].

## Recommended without a memo: ten yes-or-no points

Each is decided on its own. None is adopted by silence. Costs are judgment unless an evidence tag gives them.

1. **The bridge, 2030-34: a performance-linked increase of up to 10 points in the federal match for Medicaid home care**, the legacy HLT-4 "down payment." *Changed after the audit: the condition.*
   - *Entry, 6 points from FY2030:* states keep their own spending, pass the increase into rates and workers' pay, and report the access rule's measures.
   - *Progress, 4 more points from FY2032:* paid each year to states that improve on the access rule's measures: time from request to services, home care's share of long-term care spending, and the share of payments reaching workers. Waiting lists are reported, not the test.
   - It ends when the benefit starts in 2035, or, under memo 6 A, it becomes permanent.
   - *Why:* ARPA's one-year 10 points went mostly to the workforce, but temporary money buys one-time gains (MACPAC, 2026) [EV-CAR-425]. The Balancing Incentive Program, a match increase tied to targets, raised home care's share of spending by about 5 points, but with no penalty for missing targets it got partial results [EV-CAR-480] [EV-CAR-479]. Waiting lists are not comparable across states and are partly unscreened [EV-CAR-433], so a list-halving target would reward counting, not care.
   - *Bills:* Better Care Better Jobs' 10 points with maintenance of effort, made temporary [EV-CAR-473]; larger than Build Back Better's [EV-CAR-447]; smaller than the HCBS Access Act's 100% [EV-CAR-474].
   - *Cost:* up to about $24 billion in 2030 and $0.13 trillion over FY2030-34, less where states miss the progress measures. *Burden:* state reporting the access rule already requires [EV-CAR-432]. *Evidence:* program evaluations, modest effects. **Recommended: yes.**
2. **Make Money Follows the Person and the home-care spousal protections permanent from FY2028**, in a 2027-28 extender if one moves (a 2027-28 exception) or in the first 2029 bill. They fold into the benefit in 2035. *Why:* the program has moved 127,184 people since 2008 (CMS, 2025) [EV-CAR-421], with quality-of-life gains and lower spending for those who left nursing homes (CMS report to Congress, 2017) [EV-CAR-429]. Both expire after FY2027 (CMS, 2024) [EV-HOU-697]. *Bills:* Better Care Better Jobs and the HCBS Access Act make both permanent [EV-CAR-473] [EV-CAR-474]. *Cost:* about $0.45 billion a year for the program [EV-HOU-697]. **Recommended: yes.**
3. **No estate recovery for correctly paid Medicaid, from 2030** (S. 1506 sec. 205) [EV-CAR-431]. *Why:* recovery brought in about $733.4 million in FY2019, only 0.55 percent of fee-for-service long-term care spending, and falls on modest estates (MACPAC, 2021) [EV-CAR-410]. *Bills:* S. 1506 and the HCBS Access Act [EV-CAR-474]. *Cost:* the federal share of what states recover, under $0.5 billion a year (judgment from MACPAC's total). *Burden:* it removes a burden from families. **Recommended: yes.**
4. **Raise the SSI-linked resource limits that Medicaid long-term care uses to $10,000 for an individual and $20,000 for a couple, indexed**, the bipartisan SSI Savings Penalty Elimination Act's figures [EV-CAR-478]. Repeal the 2025 law's $1,000,000 home equity cap [EV-CAR-415]. Both apply from 2030 until the benefit replaces the asset test, and afterward for Medicaid's room-and-board help (memo 8). *Changed after the audit:* from S. 1506's $4,100 and $6,200 [EV-CAR-431]. *Why:* the $2,000 limit [EV-CAR-409] has not changed since the 1980s and forces people to impoverish themselves first; a bipartisan bill sets the higher figures. *Owner:* the SSI limit itself belongs to the secure floor (flagship 1). This point asks only that Medicaid's long-term care test use the higher figure, whatever SSI does. *Cost:* about $2-6 billion a year (judgment). **Recommended: yes.**
5. **Services that make housing work (shared rule 7, which care owns): include tenancy-sustaining services, housing navigation and assertive community treatment** in the home benefit from 2035, and as covered home-care services states may claim under the bridge's match. These are part B's addition; they are not on S. 1506's home-care list [EV-CAR-276]. *Why:* housing asked for this (`dossiers/home/4-specification.md`, HOU-4.12), since waivers must now be budget neutral (Public Law 119-21) [EV-HOU-337] and CMS withdrew its housing-services guidance [EV-HOU-339]. Housing's HUD backstop for tenants whose state Medicaid does not cover these services runs through 2035 (`DEC-2026-11-09-10`), the year the benefit starts, so the seam closes. *Evidence:* rests on housing's register; the audit did not check it, so it is plausible but thin. *Cost:* inside memo 6 B's new-user line (judgment). **Recommended: yes.** The proposed update to rule 7's note is below.
6. **CAPABLE's clinical part: a CMS demonstration in 2030-34, then the home benefit covers it from 2035.** CAPABLE pairs nurse and occupational-therapist visits with the home repairs housing's fund pays for (HOU-12.04). *Why:* in a randomized trial it cut disability scores 30% at about $2,825 a person (Szanton and others, JAMA Internal Medicine, 2019) [EV-HOU-686]. The effect faded by 12 months, and its savings estimate is uncertain [EV-HOU-688], so a Medicare benefit now (housing memo 12, part 2 C) would run ahead of the evidence. *Cost:* small, inside the benefit. **Recommended: yes.**
7. **Write the 2024 access rule's waiting-list and timeliness reporting into law.** Leave its 80% worker-pay rule and any wage floor to part D, the workforce. *Why:* the reporting is light, starts in 2027 [EV-CAR-432] [EV-CAR-433], and is the only national count of who waits, and CMS plans to rescind or revise provisions of the 2024 rules [EV-CAR-420]. It also supplies point 1's measures. *Bills:* the HCBS Access Act adds reporting [EV-CAR-474]. *Burden:* state reporting already in place. **Recommended: yes.**
8. **One plain need assessment, run locally.** States, or the plan's regional offices where a state declines, assess need with one national tool, under contract to the plan and conflict-free (the assessor does not provide the services). People are reassessed only when their needs change, not on a calendar. Under memo 6 A it is the Medicaid assessment. *Why:* the design lens; the Balancing Incentive Program required a core standardized assessment and conflict-free case management, and 19 of 20 reporting states completed the latter [EV-CAR-479]; S. 1506 gives each regional office a deputy for home and community care [EV-CAR-280]. *Burden:* one visit, at home. **Recommended: yes.**
9. **PACE and other integrated programs continue** as ways to receive the benefit, for people who choose them. PACE serves about 89,659 people (MACPAC, 2026) [EV-CAR-427]. It is a delivery option, not a saving: an HHS-commissioned review finds no savings and higher Medicaid cost [EV-CAR-481]. **Recommended: yes.**
10. **The 2025 law's nursing-home staffing moratorium** (to September 30, 2034) [EV-CAR-416]: **left to part D**, which now answers it in memo 16 (`3d-design-workforce.md`). *Not adopted by silence:* part B carries no staffing commitment of its own.

## How the choices fit together

- **The recommended set.** It runs in two steps.
  - *2030-34, the bridge.* A five-year Medicaid bridge: a higher match tied to measured progress, Money Follows the Person permanent, no estate recovery, higher resource limits, respite and training for family caregivers, and a national evaluation of paying spouses and parents (memo 9 E).
  - *From January 1, 2035.* One home and community care benefit in the single plan for everyone of any age who needs help with three daily activities or the equivalent (decided), and for everyone already on Medicaid home care. It pays set budgets by need tier, with no asset test and a self-directed option; relatives can be paid as each state allows today, and the national rule follows the evaluation. The plan pays the care part of nursing-home stays, and residents pay room and board from income above a protected allowance.
  - *Where it meets health.* The start is the same date as health's endpoint, so people move once. Medicaid's long-term care role shrinks to room and board for people who cannot pay it.
- **Against the legacy direction.** Memo 6 B meets each test it set.
  - *Prolonged intensive-care costs:* memo 7 B's top tier and memo 8 C.
  - *Assessed help without spend-down:* no asset test.
  - *A genuine paid-family-caregiver option:* memo 9 E keeps paid family care where states allow it now and decides the national rule on evidence; memo 9 A would give it everywhere at once.
  - *Stronger Medicaid services preserved:* memo 7's hold-harmless and exceptions.
  - *An outside-care option:* agency care is always available.
- **If memo 6 is A.** Points 1 to 4 become the program. The match increase is permanent, states must cover everyone eligible, and resource limits rise. Memo 8 stays A or B, and memo 9 applies through Medicaid self-direction.
- **If memo 6 is C.** Medicaid and points 1 to 4 carry the front end. The federal benefit starts after a waiting period. Memo 7's threshold applies, and memo 8 C applies after the waiting period.
- **Tensions to watch.**
  - Free care at home raises demand for workers that part D must supply [EV-CAR-423]. CBO assumed all the added demand for long-term care would be met by more care supplied [EV-CAR-452]; that holds only if part D's supply plan works.
  - The benefit's start lines up with health's busiest years (2033-35), which strains CMS's capacity (`5-delivery.md`). The one assessment (point 8) runs through states to spread that load.
  - States lose control of a program they now shape. The maintenance-of-effort payment keeps them paying.
  - Fraud in personal care is a known risk: personal care cases were 38% of Medicaid fraud units' indictments in FY2012-15 (HHS inspector general, 2017) [EV-CAR-463]. The fiscal agent's payroll records (memo 9) are the check.

## Evidence still needed

- **Independent counts of new users.** Counts of people of every age with long-term care needs who receive no paid help would tighten the new-user line, still the largest uncertainty: the National Health and Aging Trends Study, and MEPS or NHIS for younger people (section 1).
- **Urban's option 1 tables at full detail**, and CBO's current-law long-term care level for 2030, to tighten the benchmark table.
- **The WISH Act actuarial report** (Oliver Wyman, GCG and ARC, 2026) in full, and the year-by-year outgo in Milliman's exhibits for ASPE.
- **Room and board.** Nursing-home spending by payer and the room-and-board share (memo 8).
- **Family caregivers.** The national count of self-directed participants who hire relatives; an independent check of the Cash and Counseling caregiver findings [EV-CAR-487]; Germany's cash take-up and quality (memo 9).
- **The Netherlands' 2015 reform**, from a primary or OECD source.
