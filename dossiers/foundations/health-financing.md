# Paying for the single health plan: pricing and financing, a fresh start

Role: the stage E memo on how the single health plan (HLT-1 to HLT-8) is paid for, in the transition years and at full adoption. It replaces part B of `checkpoint-financing.md` (with the health parts of its "In plain terms", part C's arithmetic and part D's questions F1 to F3 and B4). Status: **outline, November 15, 2026; the full memo follows on this branch.** Every figure here is an early, approximate project estimate on the project's own models, not an official score, and may move in the full memo.

## How the pieces fit (one page)

**1. The country pays less; every dollar has a named payer.**
- National health spending, FY2040: about **$12.7 trillion today against $11.9 trillion under the plan, about $0.75 trillion (6%) less**, with about 34 million more people covered. (Coverage model, decided design; 2032 full scale is $8.28T against $7.79T.)
- The checkpoint memo's "no named payer" term (about $0.86T in FY2040) is resolved line by line: about **$0.26T is a model seam** (the coverage model starts Medicare from CMS's older projection, about $157B above CBO's February 2026 baseline in 2032); about **$0.33T is real spending with real payers** (Medicare's premiums and other receipts the model leaves out, private premiums counted twice, other premiums and out-of-pocket costs); about **$0.27T is care for the uninsured that hospitals, states and charities pay for today**.
- **The seam is fixed** by re-running the coverage model with Medicare at CBO's figure (the project's decided baseline, `DEC-2026-11-08-03`). The booked federal cost falls by about **$0.30T in FY2040** (about $1.3T over FY2030-39): from $4.09T to about **$3.79T** net in FY2040. Rebooking is a later tier-3 change.

**2. The direct answer to Espen's question.** Yes. Once every payer is named, the new public money is less than what everyone else stops paying, by the national saving: in FY2040 households, employers, states and the other payers stop paying about $5.7T, and the plan's new federal spending is about $4.9T (model basis). The first draft found them "about equal" only because it left payers out and carried the seam.

**3. Who pays what under the plan, at full adoption (FY2040, early figures).**

| Payer | Today | Under the plan | Recommended financing shape |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Employers | $2.37T in premiums | A health contribution equal to what they pay now, in total | **100% of former premiums**, as a payroll contribution of about **11.9%** of pay (about **13.4%** with the existing 1.45% HI tax); net yield about $1.63T after the tax lost on pay that no longer rises |
| States | Medicaid's state share for the people who move | A fixed payment indexed to the plan's growth | **100%** of their former spending for the people who move (about $0.35T more than the booked 50%); long-term care stays with them |
| Households | $2.37T in premiums and out-of-pocket costs | Small copays, plus a health contribution | An income-based contribution above $20,000/$40,000, collected with the income tax: about **10%** alone; Espen's 5-6% needs about **$0.8T** from somewhere else (point 4) |
| Federal | Medicare, Medicaid, premium credits, the tax break for job coverage | The plan | The existing federal streams stay, including HI's 2.9% payroll tax, which folds into the plan's fund in 2035 (memo 3 B); HX (income and payroll tax on former premiums, both shares) falls from about $1.04T to about $0.30T when employers keep paying 100%: what is left is the tax on the pay workers used to put into premiums before tax |

**4. Espen's 5-6% household rate: what it costs.**
- **The top end is used up.** With the plan's booked measures, the top federal rate on wages is already about **63%** before health: 39.6% income tax, 12.4% Social Security above $250,000 (SS-1), 5% Medicare (H1b), and the 4 + 2 booked income points. (The handover notes' 50.6% left out SS-1.) A second tier big enough for a 5-6% first tier would push it well past 80%. Not a credible route.
- **Savings** (administration at 3%, drug prices 23% lower) are worth about $0.26T in FY2040, about 1.4 points; shown as a sensitivity, not booked.
- **That leaves a second broad base**: a value-added tax with a per-person rebate (about 9-10% gross), or a larger employer contribution than today's premiums (which workers bear through pay). The full memo prices both and shows who pays.

**5. The transition years (FY2030-39).** Each payer starts paying when its group moves: employers from each move (on their former premiums until the 2035 endpoint, then on payroll), states with each Medicaid third, households' contribution phased in with the waves (wave 1 in 2031, thirds in 2033-35). The full memo shows each year's cost, each source and what is left; the early years carry costs no payer moves with (restoration, Medicare upgrades, Medicare at 60), which the general fund carries.

**6. The packages (two to four, with a recommendation; numbers in the full memo).**
- **A. Premiums become contributions:** employers 100%, states 100%, one household contribution of about 10%.
- **B. Mid-single digits with a rebated VAT:** A's employers and states, a household contribution of about 5.5-6%, and a VAT with a per-person rebate for the rest.
- **C. Mid-single digits through employers:** a household contribution of about 5.5-6%, with employers paying more than their former premiums.
- **D. Mid-single digits with savings booked:** as A, with administration and drug savings booked, the household rate about 8.5%.

For each: the household and employer rates (alone and with HI's 1.45%), which pieces go to trust funds by law and which to the general fund, HX, per-fifth and top-1% net effects, worked examples (a single worker at $45K, families at $90K and $160K with job coverage, a retiree on Medicare, a self-employed person), and who loses and how many.

**7. Beside the Sanders and Jayapal bills.** Neither bill levies a tax. Sanders's options paper paired a 7.5% employer premium and a 4% household premium with about $4.6T of top-end taxes this plan has already spent on its other flagships, which is why this plan's household side has to be larger or broader.
