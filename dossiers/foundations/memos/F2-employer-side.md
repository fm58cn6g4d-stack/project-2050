# Memo F2: What employers pay

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 2 of the financing checkpoint's health batch (`README.md`; `../checkpoint-financing.md`, part B). Status: **superseded, November 15, 2026, by [`../health-financing.md`](../health-financing.md) (its part 8 holds the new questions); kept for the record.** Earlier status: **for decision, November 14, 2026**. Figures are FY2040, from `models/tools/health_financing.py` (project arithmetic, not an official score). Each option's household figures include the income contribution (memo F1, A) sized to close the rest and the states at 80% (memo F3, B).

## The question

Employers stop paying about **$2.37 trillion** a year in premiums in FY2040, about 11.9% of all pay. The design already requires a transitional contribution while their workers move (HLT-7.18; care memo 3 B, `DEC-2026-11-10-11`), "until the financing settlement's payroll contribution takes effect". The questions are its level and what replaces it.

Workers bear part of premium costs: one study finds total compensation falls about $0.52 per dollar of higher premiums, mostly through workers' own premium shares rather than wages (Anand, 2017) [EV-CAR-299]. So an employer contribution is mostly a choice about how surely the savings reach the public fund, and about who pays among workers.

## The options

**A. 65% of former premiums during the move, then the same yield as a payroll contribution from 2036 (recommended).**
- Each employer pays 65% of what it paid toward premiums in 2030, indexed, from the date its workers move (as designed).
- From January 2036, after the 2035 endpoint, all employers pay a payroll contribution of the same national yield, about **7.7%** of pay, with their payroll-tax deposits.
- *Gap:* −$1,060B net (−$1,541B gross, less $482B of tax on pay that no longer rises).
- *Households (D3):* Q1 +$6,712, Q2 +$10,703, Q3 +$11,077, Q4 +$4,921; Q5 −$39,551; top 1% −$278,055 (with F1 A at 15.7%).
- *Administrative burden:* low, on payroll deposits that already exist. A premium base needs records for five years only.
- *Political risk:* employers that never offered coverage begin to pay from 2036, about 7.7% of pay; their workers gain the plan's coverage and their pay may rise less. Firms that paid high premiums save.
- *Legal risk:* low (a federal tax).

**B. 65% of former premiums, never converted** (the financing half of Medicare "Part E" as the board notes it).
- *Gap:* −$1,060B.
- *Households:* Q1 +$5,316, Q2 +$8,047, Q3 +$8,127, Q4 +$3,583; Q5 −$31,211; top 1% −$201,914. It falls on the workers who had coverage, more evenly across incomes than a payroll contribution.
- *Administrative burden:* rising. Each employer's 2030 premium has to be carried and indexed forever; new firms pay nothing; mergers and splits need rules.
- *Political risk:* it looks least like a new tax at first, but it freezes 2030's differences between firms.
- *Legal risk:* low.

**C. 100% of former premiums, then a payroll contribution of that yield** (about 11.9% of pay).
- *Gap:* −$1,631B; the household contribution falls to about 12.5%.
- *Households:* Q1 +$6,437, Q2 +$10,054, Q3 +$10,479, Q4 +$4,631; Q5 −$37,739; top 1% −$278,292.
- *Administrative burden:* as A.
- *Political risk:* employers save nothing, so business support is less likely, and the payroll rate is about Vermont's 11.5% [EV-CAR-244].
- *Legal risk:* low.

**D. No employer contribution** (as S. 1506's text).
- *Gap:* $0 from employers; the household contribution rises to about 21.6%.
- *Households:* Q1 +$7,223, Q2 +$11,908, Q3 +$12,188, Q4 +$5,459; Q5 −$42,917; top 1% −$277,614, but **only if employers pass all of their savings into pay**. At Anand's half, the bottom four fifths lose much of this.
- *Administrative burden:* lowest.
- *Political risk:* a windfall for employers that don't raise pay, and the largest household rate.
- *Legal risk:* low.

## Recommendation

**A.** It keeps the designed transition, collects the premium savings directly while pay adjusts, and then moves to the simplest base, the payroll every employer already reports (`DEC-2026-11-13-08`). It treats firms alike after 2035. D gives the best household numbers on paper, but only on full pass-through. B's base grows more arbitrary every year.

**What would change this:**
- evidence of fast, full pass-through would favor a smaller share (toward D);
- a decision to keep employers' support would favor the 65% level over C.

## Question for you

Which employer side: A (recommended), B, C or D?
