# Democratic renewal: specification

Role: section 4 of the democratic renewal foundation. It writes the decided designs as provisions, part by part (DEM, INT, CRT, CAP, as the README's layout sets): what each does, its parameters, who is covered, the vehicle, the agency, how it changes the January 2029 law and the evidence it rests on. Provision IDs and their fields are in `provisions.json`. Status: **draft** (stage F, step 4), on Espen's decisions `DEC-2026-11-17-40` to `-99` and three standing rules: `DEC-2026-11-09-13` (guide, don't police), `DEC-2026-11-10-05` (report, don't gate) and `DEC-2026-11-10-07` (what needs an answer now). Nothing here is booked or changes a model input; costs and unbooked lines are reported for stage G (part "What this costs, for stage G"). Section 5 (`5-delivery.md`) says who runs each provision, its capacity, timeline and remedies, and adds four provisional fixes (its part 8); section 6 (`6-cost.md`) prices them, section 7 (`7-validation.md`) validates them, and sections 8 and 9 are not yet written; each provision's `cost_source` points to section 6 (`6-cost.md`).

## How to read this section

- **Status of each provision.**
  - *Decided:* Espen decided it, in a memo option, a yes-or-no point or a direction in `-99`, cited.
  - *Working choice:* a parameter this section sets so the draft is complete. It rests on judgment or on a named bill's text, and can change without reopening a decision. Where a working choice copies a bill's number that the register does not hold, it says "to confirm in drafting" and is listed under "Evidence still needed".
  - *Direction:* Espen decided the direction, not the parameters (DEM-3, DEM-4's nationwide step, DEM-9, CAP-10). The provision commits to the decision point and the evidence it will use.
  - *Pointer:* the design belongs to another topic (DEM-14 to SAF-8, DEM-15 to LAB-9, CRT-14 to each repair's home, the effects standard to the rights topic, public defense to SAF-7). The pointer says what is sent and why.
  - *Provisional:* drafted on the recommendation under the overnight relay (`charter/PROGRESS.md`, In motion, item 3), listed under "Open questions for Espen" and revised for Espen's answer. Six elements are provisional: INT-1.01's self-directed retirement accounts, which narrows Espen's express exclusion of retirement accounts, so it waits for Espen; DEM-12's fallback, carrying the federal rule with a state track behind it rather than the state track alone (open question 3, from the milestone review's question 3); and four fixes section 5 found it needed for the provisions to run (`5-delivery.md`, part 8): CAP-7.02's start rule, CRT-5.03's interim appointments, DEM-1.13's enforcement of the floor, and DEM-11.01's and CAP-13.01's payment clock.
  - *Option kept:* not enacted, kept on the table as Espen asked (CRT-12, Court expansion including 13 Justices, the screening division). Options are listed, not specified.
- **Commitments and working assumptions.** This section commits to: the voting floor and who registers voters; the Voting Rights Act titles and their severability; DEM-2's three titles; the match, the regulators' structure and the ethics rules; the immigration court's form, tenure and review; the judgeships, the court integrity rules and the term statute; the capacity allocation, its release rule and its accountability machinery; the civil-service statute, the payment continuation and shared rule 5. Dates after enactment, thresholds copied from bills and every cost are working assumptions.
- **Dates.** The plan begins in January 2029 (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`). Calendar dates are when a rule bites; fiscal years are for money. Preparation in 2027-28 is labeled as such (CAP-18.01).
- **Evidence.** The last column lists the evidence IDs a provision rests on, in this dossier's register or another's (`EV-IMM-` for the immigration court's caseload). "Legacy" cites a legacy design file by line, restated here as evidence to weigh, not as a default (`DEC-2026-11-13-15`); figures a memo relied on are already grade D entries (EV-DEM-328 to 332).
- **IDs.** Each provision sits under the agenda row it carries (`DEM-1.01` under DEM-1). Two rows are added for decided items that had no row: **CAP-19**, shared rule 5 (memo 11), and **CRT-20**, civil legal aid beyond the immigration court (memo 9 part 3).

**Why this split.**
- *One democracy act, not several* (memo 2 B; memo 3 D): the voting floor carries the Voting Rights titles, which have the 2031 deadline and the fewest independent votes (legacy, `domain-democracy.md`:80). DEM-2 rides it because its ban and criteria must also be law before the 2031 maps, and the House state option rides with DEM-2's criteria, as the staged path sets (`DEC-2026-11-17-99`, h). INT-15, the shareholder statute, rides here rather than in the ethics act: it is the item most likely to cost the ethics act its bipartisan fast track (legacy, `domain-integrity.md`:179, :202).
- *Reconciliation carries money, not rules without a budget effect* (the Byrd rule). CAP-7S and CAP-7 are direct appropriations [EV-DEM-328]. The hiring authority is a rule; memo 10 puts it in the first bill, so it is drafted into R1 as a condition on the money, and moves to the civil service act if struck.
- *The court bills in P2 with P4's order* (`DEC-2026-11-17-52`): the judgeships first, aimed at 60 votes, because the 2024 bill passed the Senate by unanimous consent before a veto [EV-DEM-044]; then integrity; then terms, which move when the politics allow.
- *The immigration court rides the immigration act,* so the court and the caseload it decides are scored together, as both dossiers ask (`dossiers/immigration/README.md`:21; legacy, `domain-courts.md`:251).

### The floor's legal footing (milestone fix 2)

The voting floor rests mostly on the Elections Clause (Art. I, sec. 4), which lets Congress "alter" the states' rules for congressional elections and which the Court applied to the federal registration form [EV-DEM-359]. The same Court held that the clause "empowers Congress to regulate how federal elections are held, but not who may vote in them" [EV-DEM-375]. Two consequences follow: DEM-12.01, which sets who may vote, needs another power; and the floor's reach to presidential electors needs its own footing. DEM-1.03's preregistration at 16 and 17 is a manner rule, not a qualification: a preregistrant votes at 18, the Twenty-sixth Amendment's age, so its risk is low.

**DEM-12.01's power, with its weaknesses.**
- **The enforcement clauses, on a findings record (relied on).** *For it:* a state law adopted with a racially discriminatory purpose violates the Fourteenth Amendment even though it disenfranchises for crime, and section 2's exception does not shield it (*Hunter v. Underwood*, 1985) [EV-DEM-372]; Congress may set a nationwide rule over a state voter qualification on a record of racial discrimination, as the unanimous literacy-test ban in *Oregon v. Mitchell* (1970) did for every election [EV-DEM-373], and section 5 let Congress set aside New York's English literacy requirement (*Katzenbach v. Morgan*, 1966) [EV-DEM-378]; the Democracy Restoration Act's findings, the model for DEM-12.01's, state that at least 14 states enacted or expanded felony disenfranchisement laws between 1865 and 1880 [EV-DEM-379]; and the effect today falls on one African American of voting age in 22 [EV-DEM-159]. The Brennan Center reads this as enough (2019) [EV-DEM-380]. The Fifteenth Amendment has no exception like the Fourteenth's section 2, which the same memo stresses.
- *Its weaknesses, stated plainly.* (1) *Richardson v. Ramirez* (1974) held that felony disenfranchisement has "an affirmative sanction" in the Fourteenth Amendment's section 2 [EV-DEM-371]; a law that forbids what the Amendment permits risks being read as changing the right rather than enforcing it, which *City of Boerne* (1997) forbids [EV-DEM-377]. (2) Enforcement legislation needs "congruence and proportionality" to proven violations [EV-DEM-377]: a nationwide rule reaches states whose laws have no proven discriminatory origin. The Eleventh Circuit, en banc, said that without a record of constitutional violations a federal rule on these laws would raise the question whether Congress exceeded its enforcement powers (2005) [EV-DEM-381]; the Brennan Center disagrees [EV-DEM-380]. (3) A racial effect alone is not a violation; after *Callais* turned Section 2 toward intent [EV-DEM-007], the record of effect counts mainly as evidence of purpose. (4) *Shelby County* required a coverage rule to rest on current conditions [EV-DEM-011]; a record built mostly on 1865-1880 origins invites the same objection. (5) *Inter Tribal Council* reads *Oregon v. Mitchell* as five Justices rejecting a general Fourteenth Amendment power over the voting age [EV-DEM-374], so the power must be the race-specific remedial one, not a general one over who votes. *Judgment:* plausible, untested and contested; a court could strike it in whole or for particular states. That is why it is its own section, severable state by state, with the state track behind it.
- **The Elections Clause (not relied on for the restoration).** *Inter Tribal Council* reads five Justices in *Mitchell* as rejecting a congressional power to set qualifications under it, and says that view "underlies our analysis here" [EV-DEM-374]. The Brennan Center calls this dictum [EV-DEM-380]; the plan does not stake the rule on it. The clause does carry DEM-12.02's notice, registration form and pretrial access, which are manner rules.
- **Article II and *Burroughs* (not a power over who votes).** Congress may protect a presidential election from corruption without displacing the state's power to appoint electors (*Burroughs v. United States*, 1934) [EV-DEM-376]; nothing there reaches qualifications. The enforcement clauses reach elections of every kind, so if they carry DEM-12.01 they carry it for presidential electors too.
- **What the act does with this.** (a) Findings, state by state, of each provision's origin and operation and of its racial effect today, built in 2027-28 preparation from hearings and the Justice Department's records (no new cost), with the powers named in the text; (b) DEM-12.01 as its own section, severable from the act and as applied to each state, so a record a court finds thin for one state does not take the rule down for the others; (c) DEM-12.02 and DEM-12.03 behind it. *Timing:* DEM-12.01 is on the 180-day schedule (section 5, part 4, provisional), **if it survives**: a suit before the first covered election could ask a three-judge court (DEM-1.13) to block it, and *Purcell* cautions against late orders either way [EV-DEM-362].

**Which floor rules reach presidential electors, and on what power.** Article II, sec. 1, cl. 2 gives each state's legislature the choice of the manner of appointing electors, and cl. 4 lets Congress set only the time of choosing them and the day they vote (the Constitution's text, which *Inter Tribal Council* reads beside the Elections Clause [EV-DEM-375]). In practice the exposure is small: every state chooses its electors by popular vote on the same ballot, list and machines as its congressional races, so a state that wanted different rules for the presidential line would have to run two sets of procedures for one ballot (the dossier's judgment). Each rule's application to electors is its own severability unit (DEM-1.13).

**The forum rules together (milestone fix 3).** The review found two provisions sending the same suit to two courts (`review-milestone.md`, L-2). CRT-3.02's order rule settles it: a court of appeals that a statute names hears what is channelled to it, and the three-judge courts take only district-court cases. Which court hears what:

| Suit | Court | Provisions |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Review a statute already sends to a court of appeals (the Hobbs Act; the Clean Air Act's sec. 307(b)) | That court of appeals, as today | CRT-3.02's order rule |
| A district-court suit against other federal action (an executive order, guidance, a grant termination, how a program is run) seeking relief beyond the parties | A three-judge district court, direct appeal | CRT-3.01, 3.02, 9.01 |
| The same suit, relief for the plaintiffs alone | One judge, as today | — |

- **The review's narrower rule (A-3), weighed.** A-3 sends to three judges only suits seeking relief against federal action beyond the parties, outside channelled review. Its first half is the order rule, which L-2 requires. The second half, federal action only, is adopted as a working choice, for three reasons. (1) It is what was decided: point 26 went to Espen as random assignment "for cases seeking nationwide relief" (`DEC-2026-11-17-77`), and the legacy's three-judge leg ran through 28 U.S.C. 1391(e), the venue statute for suits against federal officers and agencies (`domain-courts.md`:88). (2) Reaching state action would bring in suits like the 487 a year that challenge state statutes [EV-DEM-383], most of them, on our judgment, asking to stop enforcement statewide: several times the federal panels, each with a direct appeal. (3) That was the rule Congress ended in 1976, when it stopped requiring three judges for injunctions against state statutes [EV-DEM-387]. The cost: a statewide injunction against a state law is still decided by one judge, now drawn at random (CRT-3.01), with an ordinary appeal. If Espen wants state action in, it is a one-line change, at the cost section 5 counts.
- **Why no D.C. venue.** The legacy sent these suits to the D.C. district court *or* a three-judge court, by amending 1391(e) (`domain-courts.md`:88). CRT-3.02 keeps only the panel. One district fixed as the venue for every nationwide suit against the government would make its bench the prize, which is the legacy's own warning about this rule ("it concentrates the stakes in D.D.C.", :90). With CRT-7.01, every rule challenge and every nationwide suit would then end in one circuit. A panel of three judges drawn at random already removes the single chosen judge, the harm point 26 answers. What it leaves: a plaintiff still chooses the circuit by where it files, so circuit-shopping remains, diluted because the panel is drawn from the whole circuit. A national draw of the two added judges is the fix if circuit choice proves the problem (left open, `8-change-our-minds.md`, part 5).
- **The D.C. Circuit's composition as a prize.** The legacy warns that channelling rule review there "makes the D.C. Circuit's composition a political prize" (`domain-courts.md`:132). It does: CRT-7.01 puts most pre-enforcement rule review before the court's 11 judgeships [EV-DEM-389], so each vacancy matters more. The plan keeps the channel because point 29 decided one court, the Clean Air Act already does this for national rules, and the alternative is the present one, where any rule can be challenged before a chosen judge anywhere. What limits the prize: panels are drawn from the whole court, the en banc court and the Supreme Court review them, a statute may name another circuit for its own rules, and the Justices' terms (CRT-15) set the top court's turnover. The fallback, if the prize proves corrosive, is the Hobbs Act's venue: the petitioner's home circuit or the D.C. Circuit, with a random draw among circuits when petitions are filed in several (28 U.S.C. 2112(a), not yet in the register). That changes point 29's "one court", so it would go to Espen (`8-change-our-minds.md`, part 5).

Restoring the vote on release (point 1, `-64`) sets who may vote, which the Elections Clause does not reach; its footing is the enforcement clauses on a findings record, plausible but contested ("The floor's legal footing"). The commitment stays as decided; the question is how to carry it if the record proves weak. It costs nothing either way.
   - *A, recommended:* carry DEM-12.01 as its own federal section, severable from the act and state by state, with the notice and pretrial access (DEM-12.02) and the state track (DEM-12.03) behind it. *Consequences:* the promise reaches every state at once if it survives, and where it falls the state track and the notice carry on; the cost is a likely early suit, a promise that must be stated with its risk ("if it survives"), and a findings record to build in 2027-28.
   - *B:* rely on the state track alone, with DEM-12.02's notice and pretrial access kept federal. *Consequences:* no court risk and no 2030 suit over who may vote, but people released in states that do not restore on release stay disenfranchised in federal elections until their states act, and the plan no longer makes point 1's federal promise, so Espen's yes would become a narrower commitment.
   - *What would change the recommendation:* a ruling before 2029 that narrows the enforcement power over voting further, or a findings record that cannot document a discriminatory purpose in most states that still disenfranchise after release. Either goes back to Espen before drafting (section 8, part 1).
