## The brief

**Registration that doesn't depend on paperwork, maps drawn by fair rules, officials who can't trade on what they know, courts with rules of their own, and a government that can deliver what it promises.**

About one eligible citizen in four was not registered in 2024, counting people who did not answer the question: 73.6% of voting-age citizens were registered and 65.3% voted (Census Bureau, 2025) [EV-DEM-001], and about 28.0 million were not registered (Census Bureau, 2026) [EV-DEM-002], a floor, because many did not answer the question (section 1, A.1).

Meanwhile:
- **The federal voting-rights floor has narrowed to almost nothing.** Preclearance has been inoperable since *Shelby County* in 2013 (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-DEM-011]. On April 29, 2026, *Louisiana v. Callais* limited Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act to cases with a strong inference of intentional discrimination (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-DEM-007].
- **The referees can't act.** The Election Assistance Commission has had no commissioners since July 9, 2026 (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-DEM-020]. The Federal Election Commission had two of its six seats filled in March 2026, and most of its actions need four votes (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-DEM-031].
- **Money moves outside the limits.** Independent expenditures reported to the FEC were $4.4 billion in 2023-24 (Federal Election Commission, 2023-24 cycle summary) [EV-DEM-023]. Dark money was $1.4 billion by OpenSecrets' count and more than $1.9 billion by the Brennan Center's, depending on method (OpenSecrets, 2025; Brennan Center, 2025) [EV-DEM-026] [EV-DEM-025]. In June 2026 the Supreme Court ended the limits on party spending coordinated with candidates (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-DEM-027].
- **The checks on officials were weakened.** A court held the firing of 17 inspectors general unlawful but did not reinstate the eight who sued (U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, 2025) [EV-DEM-036]. The Supreme Court's 2023 code of conduct has no enforcement mechanism of its own (Congressional Research Service, 2023) [EV-DEM-040]. 34% of adults approve of the Supreme Court (Gallup, 2026) [EV-DEM-062]; 89% favor a strict ethics code for the Justices and 79% fixed terms (Marquette Law School Poll, reported by SCOTUSblog, 2026) [EV-DEM-063].
- **Trust is low.** 17% trust the government in Washington to do what is right most of the time (Pew Research Center, 2025) [EV-DEM-065].
- **The agencies every promise runs through lost their people.** The Office of Personnel Management counts a net loss of 258,108 federal employees since January 20, 2025 (OPM's data, second Trump administration, 2026) [EV-DEM-082]. IRS staff fell from 94,095 to 70,829 full-time equivalents between FY2025 and FY2026 (Treasury, 2026) [EV-DEM-088]; Social Security's from 56,645 in FY2024 to 48,530 in FY2026 (Social Security Administration, 2026) [EV-DEM-105].
- **Delivery already fails in ways you can count.** Agencies reported about $186 billion of improper payments in FY2025 (Government Accountability Office, 2026) [EV-DEM-113]. Callers to Social Security waited an average 108.6 minutes for a callback, and about 25 million calls ended without service in FY2025 (Social Security's inspector general, 2025) [EV-DEM-106]. Funding lapsed three times in seven months: a 42-day government-wide gap from October 1, 2025, a three-day gap from January 31, 2026, and a 75-day gap for one department, the Department of Homeland Security (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-DEM-127].
- **The immigration courts are buried and run by the prosecutor's department.** 3,300,793 cases were pending in FY2026 (Justice Department's immigration court office, 2026) [EV-IMM-100], with 753 judges on board (same office, 2026) [EV-IMM-103] and a cap of 800 judges in law from November 1, 2028 (the 2025 reconciliation law) [EV-IMM-105].

**What the plan does.** A democracy act, an ethics act, a civil service and delivery act, three court bills, the agency rebuild in the first reconciliation act, and the immigration court in the immigration act.

1. **A vote that's easy to cast and easy to check** (the democracy act, Division A). **One start date:** every rule of the floor applies from the first regularly scheduled federal general election held at least 180 days after enactment (November 5, 2030 if the act is law by May 9, 2030), and to any federal primary or special election after it (DEM-1F.01; section 5, part 4; confirmed by Espen). One pushback, with evidence: a jurisdiction that still votes on paperless machines at enactment offers a paper ballot on request and audits what it has from that election, and meets the full paper-ballot rule by the 2032 general election at the latest (DEM-1.11; Louisiana voted on paperless machines statewide in 2024, Verified Voting, 2024) [EV-DEM-410]. The count of other such jurisdictions is small and not published: about 4,407 precincts nationwide were projected on paperless machines in 2024 (Verified Voting and the Brennan Center, 2024) [EV-DEM-412].
   - *From that date:* any current government, tribal, student or benefit card as identification, or a signed statement under penalty of perjury, checked afterward; mail voting without an excuse, with a drop box in every county; same-day, online and preregistration at 16 and 17; at least two weeks of early voting, including two weekends; registration done by the state from records it already holds, with a mailed notice and a way to decline; citizenship checked against federal records in a set order (state records, then immigration and passport records, then Social Security last, and no flag on a missing or lagging record), never documents a voter must bring; a notice within two business days and a chance to fix a problem with a mail ballot; no removal from the rolls for not voting; certification of results as a duty a court can enforce; an official notice of voting rights for people leaving prison, and access to vote for people held before trial; and paid time off to vote (DEM-1.01 to 1.12, DEM-12.02, DEM-13.01). Notices come in the languages the Voting Rights Act already requires and in accessible formats, and a voter with no fixed address gives a description of where they live and a mailing point. Federal voting rights restored on release from prison (DEM-12.01) are on the same date **if the rule survives a court challenge**: it rests first on Congress's power to enforce the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments against racial discrimination and, in the alternative, on the federal-elections power *Oregon v. Mitchell* (1970) upheld, which the Court now gives little weight; a court may hold either too thin, so it is written to fall alone, state by state, with state restoration laws behind it (DEM-12.03; see "What a court could strike").
   - A free voter card, mailed from records, optional, never a condition of voting. **Every registrant gets theirs by one deadline**, 45 days before that first election (September 21, 2030 for November 5), which is about 170 million cards in about 135 days, on our estimates about $0.14-0.26 billion once and well under $0.1 billion a year after (DEM-1.07; section 5, part 4; section 6). Later registrants get theirs with their registration notice.
   - Election offices get recurring federal money for the first time: about $2 billion a year, a block grant plus an equipment fund, a working figure (range $1-4 billion) until a county cost study replaces it, with the first grants paid within 60 days of enactment and each year's within 60 days of the fiscal year's start (DEM-11.01, 11.02). Today's federal election grant is $45 million (Congressional Research Service, 2026) [EV-DEM-017].
2. **Voting rights and fair maps** (the democracy act, Divisions B and C, from enactment).
   - Preclearance returns for places with a record of recent violations, recalculated every year, so coverage follows conduct, not 1965's history (DEM-1.16). A study found preclearance raised long-run turnout 4 to 8 points (*American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 2019) [EV-DEM-157].
   - The Voting Rights Act's results test is restored, and a separate title gives intent claims rules of proof they can be won on. Each title stands alone if another is struck (DEM-1.17 to 1.19).
   - From enactment through the 2031 cycle, no state may redraw its congressional map mid-decade; maps must meet neutral criteria and a partisan-fairness test; a three-judge court hears challenges on a fast track; an independent commission is a safe harbor, never a mandate (DEM-2.01 to 2.06). The 2025-26 maps stand until the 2031 redraw.
3. **Money and ethics** (the democracy act, Division D, and the 2029 ethics act).
   - **A 6:1 match on small in-state gifts** up to $200 for House and Senate candidates who opt in: a $25 gift brings the candidate $175. No spending cap and no trigger (INT-5.01). The first matched cycle is 2032 on the working timeline.
   - Disclosure of the donors behind election spending; single-candidate super PACs presumed coordinated with their candidate, on objective tests the PAC can rebut; no political spending by majority foreign-owned companies; shareholder approval for corporate political spending (INT-2.01, 8.01, 15.01, 16.01).
   - A five-member FEC with a quorum of three; if it does not act on a complaint in 120 days, the complainant may sue the violator (INT-7.01). The same five-member structure for the EAC, whose executive director can keep its core work running without a quorum (DEM-1.15). Nominations to both under current law from January 20, 2029.
   - **A stock ban** for members of Congress, senior congressional staff (on the public-filer pay line the Ethics in Government Act already uses), their spouses and dependent children, the President, the Vice President, Senate-confirmed executive officials, the Federal Reserve governors and the Reserve Bank presidents, from the ethics act (2029): no individual stocks. Judges and Justices get disclosure only. Diversified funds, Treasuries, municipal bonds and plan-managed retirement accounts (the TSP, 401(k) funds, pensions) are never covered; a new purchase of an individual stock inside a self-directed account is. **No forced sale:** within 180 days (a working choice), a covered person complies by holding only those, by a qualified blind trust, or by selling, with no immediate tax on a sale made to comply. Penalties are automatic and published, computed from the person's own filings, with notice and an appeal; for anyone other than a member of Congress the figure is only a notice, and the penalty is collected by a Justice Department suit in district court (INT-1.01 to 1.06). Presidential candidates file their tax returns with the FEC; one who refuses owes a civil penalty by suit and the refusal is published, with ballot access untouched (INT-6.01).
   - Cooling-off periods, a two-year bar on senior officials taking paid speeches or later pay from anyone with a matter they handled, a right to sue over emoluments, lobbying and foreign-agent loopholes closed, and contractors with adjudicated fraud barred (INT-3, 4, 10, 11, 13, 14).
4. **Courts with rules.**
   - **71 new judgeships**, the Judicial Conference's 2025 request, in three tranches (2030, 2033 and 2035 on the working timeline), so no one President fills them all (CRT-1.01).
   - **A binding code for the Justices**, adopted by the Court within a year of the court integrity act (a working choice); recusal decided by the Court with published reasons; an inspector general who investigates and reports but cannot sanction; recorded votes and written reasons for emergency orders within 30 days; judges assigned at random for cases that seek to bar or require government action beyond the parties, and three randomly drawn judges, with a direct appeal to the Supreme Court, when such a case challenges federal action in a district court; challenges to federal rules go to one court of appeals within 60 days of publication, a working choice (CRT-2, 3, 4, 7). The 2029-30 court integrity act carries them.
   - **Eighteen-year terms for the Justices**, two years apart, with Article III pay and tenure kept after, for Justices appointed after the terms act; a separate, severable part moves sitting Justices one every two years; a constitutional amendment goes beside the statute. The Court stays at nine active Justices. The terms act moves when the politics allow; the first statutory term ends about 2047 (CRT-15.01 to 15.03).
5. **An immigration court outside the prosecutor's department** (the immigration act).
   - A unit of the federal courts on the bankruptcy-court model: the district courts get original jurisdiction and refer cases to it by standing order, with withdrawal for cause; judges appointed by the courts of appeals for 14-year terms and removable only by judges, with appeals to panels of immigration judges and then the courts of appeals (CRT-5.01 to 5.05).
   - It takes over from the Justice Department on October 1, 2031 (a working date, about 21 months after the act). Until then the Justice Department keeps hiring about 250 judges a year.
   - The 800-judge cap is repealed. On the immigration model, about 1,670 judges at the peak (range about 860 to 2,400) clear the backlog by about FY2035 (CRT-5.06). Children, people with a serious mental illness and everyone detained get a government-appointed lawyer (CRT-5.08, the immigration flagship's IMM-3.12).
   - Civil legal aid grows through the Legal Services Corporation from FY2030, in six steps to about $0.6 billion a year more by FY2035 on our estimates (range $0.3-1.1 billion), each step released only as local legal aid offices hire (CRT-20.01). No new right to a lawyer is created now.
6. **A government that can deliver** (R1 by about mid-2029; the civil service and delivery act, 2029).
   - **The agency rebuild:** the money every other part of the plan runs through, for the IRS, Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid's agency, HUD, FEMA, the Labor Department and others, allocated agency by agency, about 54,000 added staff across the six largest (CAP-7S.01, 7.01; the project's legacy capacity review, 2026) [EV-DEM-331]. Money is released as each agency hires against a published path; a slow agency's next tranche waits and the money stays available (CAP-7.02). An OMB delivery unit from January 20, 2029, and published times, error rates and call waits for every plan program each quarter (CAP-7.03, 7.04).
   - **Proving it once:** programs decide eligibility first from federal records they already hold; a person proves a fact once; a data match is a flag for a person to review, never a denial; recovery of an overpayment is capped at 10% of a monthly payment, and the government's own error is never repaid by the person (CAP-19.01 to 19.05). No federal record shared under the rule may be used for immigration or criminal enforcement, and the rule does not reach state, local or school records (CAP-19.02).
   - **Payments keep flowing in a shutdown:** benefit payments, pay for the staff who make them and payments under grants already awarded continue at the prior year's rate (CAP-4.01), from the civil service and delivery act.
   - **A career civil service in law:** Schedule Policy/Career rescinded by order on January 20, 2029, then barred by statute, with about 50,000 positions returned (OPM's estimate, second Trump administration, 2026) [EV-DEM-087] and a faster performance process (CAP-8.01, CAP-9.01).
   - Congress's own capacity: staff budgets restored to 1979 levels in real terms, a modernized Office of Technology Assessment, and stronger support agencies, costed from FY2030 (CAP-1 to CAP-3).
7. **Long-run representation, in stages.** A state option for multi-member districts elected by ranked choice, with the democracy act (2029-30); the House's size set in the 2031 review, for the 2032 election if the census numbers come in time, otherwise 2034; nationwide districts and the presidential method decided in 2033 with one national popular vote amendment (DEM-3.01, DEM-4.01, 4.02, DEM-8.02, DEM-9.01). D.C. statehood and a binding Puerto Rico status vote in 2029-30, each admission act giving the new state temporary House seats on the latest population estimate until the next census (one for D.C., four for Puerto Rico on 2025 estimates, about $25-50 million a year once admitted; confirmed); territories and tribal representation in the second Congress (DEM-5.01, 6.01, 7.01). Three national citizens' assemblies, whose referring committee or agency must answer each recommendation in writing, within 180 days on the working choice (DEM-18.01).

**What the plan is honest about.**
- **Most of this needs 60 Senate votes or a rules change.** In the project's vote simulation, every regular-order bill here passes in none of the simulated Congresses without a rules change. With the graduated-cloture reform, the democracy act passes in 72% and the court package in 31% (the project's vote model, on the legacy war game's estimates: relative odds, not forecasts) (section 7, part 1.1). The agency rebuild rides the 51-vote reconciliation act.
- **Some of it may be struck in court.** No part of the plan is promised to survive a challenge. Each risky part is written to fall alone (below, "What a court could strike, and what would stand").
- **The rebuild is limited by hiring, not money.** Large agencies hired 65,953 people and lost 134,122 in January to June 2025 (Government Accountability Office, 2026) [EV-DEM-085]. The IRS lost 28% of its staff, net, between January 2025 and January 2026 (Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration, 2026) [EV-DEM-089], though it onboarded nearly 19,000 external hires in FY2024 (same, 2026) [EV-DEM-370]. A miss in agency hiring is a miss in every other flagship's promises.
- **No turnout gain is promised.** Each rule's effect comes from separate studies; rules interact, so no total is claimed (section 7, part 3).

## The case in one paragraph

A quarter of eligible citizens aren't registered, the Supreme Court has hollowed out the Voting Rights Act, the agencies that referee elections and campaign money have no quorum, and the government that is supposed to deliver lost about a tenth of its people. This plan has the states register citizens from records they already hold, checks citizenship against federal records instead of making voters bring papers, and turns no eligible voter away for lack of a card. It brings back preclearance where violations are recent, bans mid-decade gerrymanders and matches small gifts six to one. It bars members of Congress and top officials from owning individual stocks, without forcing a sale. It gives the Justices a binding code and future Justices eighteen-year terms, and moves the immigration courts out of the prosecutor's department. And it rebuilds the agencies every other promise runs through, paying them as they hire, with eligibility proved once and payments that keep flowing in a shutdown.

## Hard questions, plain answers

- **"Isn't this a federal takeover of elections?"** No. The Constitution's Elections Clause lets Congress alter the states' rules for congressional elections, and in 2013 the Supreme Court held it let the federal registration form displace Arizona's documentary-proof rule (*Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council*, 2013) [EV-DEM-359]. The floor governs federal elections only. States keep running them, set their own rules for their own elections and keep their own privacy law. The money comes with the duties: a recurring grant and money for the new systems, with no new state match (section 5, part 6).
- **"If a state ignores the floor, who makes it comply?"** Two people can take it to a federal judge (DEM-1.13). The Attorney General can sue the state at once. A voter who is harmed, say wrongly removed from the rolls, first writes to the state's chief election official; the state then has 90 days to fix it, 20 days if the problem comes within 120 days of a federal election, and in the last 30 days before an election the voter can go straight to court (the same rule as the National Voter Registration Act since 1993) [EV-DEM-351]. If the state fixes it, there is no lawsuit. If not, the judge can order the state to follow the law, for example to put voters back on the rolls before the election; a state that ignores the order is in contempt. A voter who wins gets their lawyer's fees paid [EV-DEM-411]. No damages, no fines and no prosecutions: the state still runs its elections, and the court only makes it meet the floor (section 4, "How the floor is enforced").
- **"Won't registration from records put noncitizens on the rolls?"** Citizenship is checked against records in a set order: the state's own records, then immigration and passport records, and Social Security's last, and a missing or lagging record never flags anyone, because Social Security's data lag for naturalized citizens (the 2025 run of the federal SAVE tool on Social Security data flagged citizens [EV-DEM-413] [EV-DEM-414]). Only an affirmative record that a person is not a citizen can flag them; the voter gets notice and may answer with any evidence, and stays registered until a person decides, and nobody is removed for a missed reply alone. A household record passes on only the person known to be a citizen of voting age (DEM-1.02, 1.08). On the old order Texas's 2025 run flagged about 0.015% of its voters, so we expect about 8,000 to 26,000 flags in a first national run, on our estimates. Audits find noncitizen registration rare: 20 among 8.2 million in Georgia [EV-DEM-163], 27 of 2,069,640 records in Utah [EV-DEM-164].
- **"Why not require photo ID?"** The Supreme Court upheld Indiana's photo ID law in 2008 (*Crawford v. Marion County*, 2008) [EV-DEM-361]; that permits ID, it doesn't show ID is needed. A national study found strict ID laws had no negative effect on registration or turnout, and no effect on fraud (Cantoni and Pons, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2021) [EV-DEM-154]; the Government Accountability Office found turnout fell in Kansas and Tennessee (GAO, 2014) [EV-DEM-155], which the Heritage Foundation disputes (Heritage Foundation, 2015) [EV-DEM-156]. The plan mails every registrant a free card, so ID is easy to have, and keeps the signed statement, checked against records afterward, so no eligible voter is turned away. If audits of statement voting found abuse at scale, the answer is a stronger records check after the vote, never a document at the door (section 8, part 1).
- **"Will the Voting Rights titles survive this Supreme Court?"** Some may not. *Callais* limits Section 2 to a strong inference of intent [EV-DEM-007], and the plan claims no survival for the restored results test. That is why a separate title writes rules of proof for intent claims, which is new and untested, and why preclearance rests on a current record written to meet *Shelby County*'s objection to a frozen formula [EV-DEM-011], with no claim that it survives: *Callais*'s reasoning could also reach the effect standard in preclearance and the burden shift in the intent title, and an intent-based preclearance or the amendment is the fallback. Each title stands alone; if intent claims also narrow, the record supports a constitutional amendment (section 7, part 6.2; section 8, part 2).
- **"Is public financing just money for politicians?"** About $6 billion over 2030-39 on our estimates (range $4-8 billion, up to $12 billion if participation and payment ceilings run high), scaled from the Congressional Budget Office's estimate of a House-only match, about $620 million for the 2028 election and $850 million for 2030 (CBO, 2021) [EV-DEM-355]. For scale, presidential and congressional candidates raised $5.8 billion in the 2023-24 cycle alone [EV-DEM-023]. It goes only to candidates who opt in and raise small in-state gifts, and the FEC publishes the first two matched cycles (2032 and 2034 on the working timeline) to test it.
- **"Doesn't the First Amendment block this?"** It closes some doors and leaves others open. The Court upheld public financing in 1976 while striking mandatory spending limits (*Buckley v. Valeo*, 1976) [EV-DEM-363]; the match has no spending cap and no trigger, which the Court forbade in 2011 (*Arizona Free Enterprise*, 2011) [EV-DEM-208]. Disclosure won 8-1 in *Citizens United* (2010) [EV-DEM-205], and the disclosure title is written to the narrow tailoring *Bonta* requires (2021) [EV-DEM-206]. The coordination presumption and shareholder approval are the parts most at risk, and each is written to fall alone. The presumption rests on *Buckley*'s "prearranged or coordinated" rule and needs a findings record in the act, as *Cruz* (2022) asks, and one of its two triggering tests must be staff-or-family or candidate fundraising, so shared vendors alone do not trigger it.
- **"Is this court-packing?"** No. The Court stays at nine. Once the transition ends, seats open two years apart, so each President gets two appointments a term; the nine are active Justices. A larger Court, including 13 Justices to match the 13 circuits, stays an option on the table, not enacted and not automatic. Whether it goes on the 2031 review's agenda is **decided: yes, with a findings record and no automatic trigger (D5, `DEC-2026-11-18-15`)**.
- **"Can Congress set term limits by statute?"** It is contested. The Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court was divided on exactly this (Presidential Commission, 2021) [EV-DEM-233]. The statute keeps Article III pay and tenure for life and applies to Justices appointed after it passes. The part that moves sitting Justices is separate because it is the likeliest to be struck; an amendment goes beside the statute.
- **"Can the immigration court be outside the Justice Department and still be constitutional?"** In 2024 the Court listed immigration among the matters Congress may assign to judges outside Article III, while adding that the presumption favors Article III courts (*SEC v. Jarkesy*, 2024) [EV-DEM-365]. The courts of appeals already appoint bankruptcy judges for 14-year terms [EV-DEM-333], and bankruptcy courts are units of the district courts, which refer cases to them, which is what the immigration court copies (CRT-5.01) [EV-DEM-394] [EV-DEM-395]; *Slaughter* (2026) likely does not reach judges removable only by judges, since the Court left open non-Article III courts [EV-DEM-399], and the Court has held that even an Article I court can be a "Court of Law" that appoints officers (*Freytag*, 1991) [EV-DEM-368]. If a court holds that a class of cases needs an Article III judge, those cases go to the district court and the rest of the court stands (CRT-5.10). Opponents, as the Congressional Research Service reports, raise slower hiring during a restructuring (Congressional Research Service, 2022) [EV-DEM-369]; the Justice Department keeps hiring until the transfer.
- **"Can about 1,250 sitting judges be screened by October 2031?"** Maybe not. On the immigration model about 1,250 judges sit at the transfer (section 7, part 1.4). The fix lets an unscreened judge continue for up to two years while the screens finish (item D2, decided).
- **"Can agencies really hire this fast?"** Not at the pace the money is booked. The booked path assumes full strength in FY2032, about twice the working pace of a quarter of each agency's target a year (section 7, part 1.3). The design doesn't depend on the booked pace: each agency's path is set on the working pace, money follows hiring, and a slow agency writes one corrective plan and keeps its money while it catches up. The need is about 13,500 net added staff a year across the six largest agencies at the working pace, on our estimates; the IRS's nearly 19,000 external hires in FY2024 [EV-DEM-370] show gross hiring at that scale has been done. The 141,084 people who took deferred resignation (OPM's data, 2026) [EV-DEM-083] are a pool that already knows the work.
- **"Isn't the contingency a slush fund?"** It is released only against a published written need, for programs already enacted, with 30 days' notice to the Appropriations Committees and a yearly GAO audit of every release (CAP-7S.02, 7.05). It sits inside the booked figure, not on top of it. A second contingency of $100 billion over 2030-39 is booked elsewhere in the plan; together they hold up to about $173 billion no program has claimed, and the financing settlement decides whether both are needed (section 6, part 1.3).
- **"What stops a hostile administration from sitting on the money?"** Less than we would like. The general fix, a right to sue over impoundment, belongs to the executive power topic, not yet written. Until then the election grant and the state systems grant are to be paid within 60 days of the start of each fiscal year (or of enactment in the first year), enforceable by the state (D2, decided). The released tranches of the rebuild still depend on OMB.
- **"Doesn't shared records mean a surveillance database?"** Each use is logged, a person can see what was used about them, and no record shared under the rule may be used for immigration or criminal enforcement (CAP-19.02, DEM-1.14). If a law ever required enforcement use, the matching stops; the plan would oppose the change, not work around it (section 8, part 10). The Justice Department's 2025-26 suits for unredacted voter rolls (Votebeat, 2026) [EV-DEM-021] are why the line is written into law.
- **"What if the Senate doesn't change its rules?"** Then every regular-order bill here is stuck at 60 votes. Under the narrower democracy carve-out the plan also tests, the democracy act passes in 91% of simulated Congresses, but the ethics, court and civil service bills fall to none (section 7, part 1.1). The judgeships act, whose 2024 version Congress approved before a veto (Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts, 2025) [EV-DEM-044], is the likeliest to pass on its own. The agency rebuild rides reconciliation either way.
