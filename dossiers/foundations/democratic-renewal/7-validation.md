# Democratic renewal: validation

Role: section 7 of the democratic renewal foundation. It runs the models that can be run on sections 4 to 6, sets the foundation's figures against outside ones, says what the models miss, names the outcomes the foundation moves and how they would be seen, answers the strongest objections from the left, the right, election officials and the courts, and lists the signs of failure. Part 8 checks that every one of the 137 provisions in `provisions.json` is covered. Status: **draft** (stage F, step 7), on `DEC-2026-11-17-40` to `-99`, with every provisional item of sections 4 and 5 kept provisional and one new one added (part 10). **Nothing is booked here** and no adopted input changes. Every figure is project arithmetic or a restated outside score, not an official score. Outcomes it serves (`charter/outcomes.md`): **OUT-09**, its own; **OUT-10**, with work; and, through delivery, **OUT-01 to OUT-07**.

## How to read this section

- **Conventions** follow section 6: nominal $ billion by federal fiscal year, a cost positive, on the general fund, against the January 2029 law and the budget model's booking (case `mixed`, on main after #431).
- **What each input is** (commitment, working assumption, open question or study in implementation) is in part 9, for every material input this section uses.
- **The vote model's odds are relative, not forecasts.** Its own page says so: every vote parameter is the legacy war game's or an estimate, and the Senate roster is the war game's mid-2026 map (`legacy/index/vote-simulation.md`, line 3).
- **Two notes from #430's review are carried** and closed in part 2: the match's legacy high against DEM-1's ledger high, and the JUDGES Act's seat count.
- **Legal holdings** are cited from the Court's own syllabus or opinion, saved in `library/text/` [EV-DEM-359] to [EV-DEM-368]. A holding says what the Court decided then; it is not a prediction of what this Court would do with these statutes.

### 1.3 The hiring-pace check: can CAP-7's money be spent at the pace set?

Sections 3 and 5 say hiring, not money, sets the pace [EV-DEM-277] [EV-DEM-085], and assume about a quarter of each agency's added-staff target is hired a year from FY2030 (range a fifth to a third; `3-design.md`, part G, "Timing and pace"). The draft script (`models/tools/democratic_renewal_pace.py`, rebased in the milestone review's fix step 1; unit tests in `models/tools/test_democratic_renewal.py`) asks whether the money path matches that pace, and what re-laying it would do to outlays and to the IRS's enforcement revenue. It takes part G's sustained allocation at its full run rate ($32.5 billion in FY2040, deflated at the booked path's own growth of about 3.8% a year) and lays it on staff on board; a share of each line (pay) follows staff and the rest (systems, space, contracts) follows the booked ramp.

**One basis** (`dossiers/TEMPLATE.md`, "One basis for each fiscal figure"). The budget model lays every dollar of CAP-7 on the booked ramp (33%, 67%, then 100% of the run rate in FY2030-32). On that basis part G's allocation is **$243.9 billion** over FY2030-39: the same $32.5 billion run rate on the booked ramp. Part G's stated **$249.5 billion** is on a different path, the legacy review's own ramp and 3.5% growth (rebuilt by the script at $250.7 billion), and the no-ramp path, the run rate from FY2030 with no ramp at all, is **$266.6 billion**. The draft compared the paced allocation with $249.5 billion and called the difference, $22.2 billion, the pace effect; that mixed two bases (milestone review, F-1). The pace effect below is the allocation on the booked ramp less the same allocation on the hiring pace: same run rate, only the ramp differs.

**How the yield lag is priced.** G8 nets **$150 billion** over FY2030-39 and $20.5 billion in FY2040 after the model's allowance (section 6, part 1.3), and the budget model lays it on the same booked ramp as CAP-7: it assumes the yield follows the money. The script lays it instead on IRS enforcement staff on board at the same pace as the outlays, with the capacity review's own lag rule (`models/tools/capacity-review/review.py`, `lagged_yield`): a dollar of enforcement yields after one to two years (central one), so the window's yield comes from staff on board through FY2038 (FY2037 at two years), scaled so the booked ramp returns the booked $150 billion. At the working pace that loses **about $16 billion** (about $14 billion with no lag, $19 billion at two years); at a fifth a year about $22-29 billion, at a third about $7-9 billion. The review's estimate of about $30-40 billion (`review-milestone.md`, F-1) moved the whole net yield path two years later, which drops FY2038-39's full-strength yield ($38.5 billion) from the window; a slower ramp does not do that, because every pace reaches full strength by FY2035 and the yield from then on is the same. For the IRS alone the lag is larger than the outlays it saves ($16.1 billion against $6.3 billion at the working pace, and in every run), which is what section 6 means by "a slow path delays the yield more than it saves outlays". Across the whole allocation the two roughly cancel.

**Findings.**
- **The money path runs about two years ahead of the hiring assumption.** The booked path reaches its full run rate in FY2032, which matches hiring about half of each target a year, twice the working pace. The ledger row's note says the same: "stepping up over FY2030-32" (`models/data/inputs/ledger-bills.csv`, CAP-7). The review's midpoint path does the same (about 44%, 75% and 97% of its run rate in FY2030-32). Section 3's "most hiring lands by FY2033-34" and its money path do not agree. The legacy IRS row is at the fast end: 24,000 enforcement staff "hired over three years (30/60/90% in FY2030-32)", "about 8,000 hires a year, near the FY2024 pace" (`legacy/sources/capacity-recovery-budget-review.md`:27). At the working pace agencies reach full strength in FY2034; at a third a year in FY2033, at a fifth in FY2035.
- **At the working pace, about $52 billion of the booked $279.2 billion is not spent in FY2030-39** (range $42-71 billion). On the booking's basis it has two parts, which answer different questions:
  - **(a) About $17 billion (range $7-36 billion) is the pace effect:** part G's allocation is spent as staff come on board, about $17 billion less in FY2030-33 than on the booked ramp (72.2 against 55.7). It is less outlay in the window, not a later one: every run reaches the full $32.5 billion by FY2035, so FY2040, the year the fiscal rule tests, does not change. Where the unspent money goes is in the next finding. This is the booking question (part 10, question 4).
  - **(b) About $35 billion is memo 10's sustained contingency** (CAP-7S.02), money above the allocation that is released only against a written need. On part G's own path it is the $29.7 billion section 6 quotes; on the booked ramp, where the allocation is $243.9 billion, the booking holds $35.3 billion above it. FY2040 is the same on both bases ($4.7 billion). It is not a pace effect: if never released it is never spent. Whether it (and the surge's $43.2 billion) stays booked is stage G's question, with the October 26 allowance (section 6, part 1.3), not a pace finding; stage G should read it on the booking's basis.
- **Where an unspent balance goes** (working choice for question 4, consistent with CAP-7.02 and memo 10 B). Memo 10 B made the money "available on enactment and until spent", and CAP-7.02 says a slow year does not lose it. Under the tranche rule, a tranche OMB holds stays in the agency's account: it does not lapse and does not join the contingency. It is released with the agency's next tranches when its count catches up with its path, for the added staff and the one-time costs of catching up (recruiting, onboarding, training). What the agency has not drawn when it reaches its full run rate is counted toward its later CAP-7 amounts, so the next years' new money is smaller by the same amount and nothing is spent on top of the run rate. So the money is not lost to a slow agency, outlays over FY2030-39 are lower by the pace effect, and FY2040 does not change. (The draft said both that the money "stays available" and that FY2040 does not change, without saying how; this is how both hold.) If agencies catch up faster than a straight ramp, some of the balance is spent within the window and the pace effect moves toward the low end of its range.
- **Net hiring needed** for the six agencies sized, about 54,000 added staff [EV-DEM-331]: about 13,500 a year at the working pace (10,800 at a fifth, 18,000 at a third), the IRS about 7,500 of it. Those are net of attrition; gross hiring is higher. The IRS onboarded nearly 19,000 external hires in FY2024 [EV-DEM-370], so gross hiring at the needed scale has been done recently. Against it: large agencies hired 65,953 people and lost 134,122 in January to June 2025 [EV-DEM-085]; the IRS lost more revenue agents than it hired in the first half of FY2023 with the money in hand [EV-DEM-277], and 31,273 of its staff left between January 2025 and January 2026, a 28% net decrease [EV-DEM-089]; SSA's staff fell from 56,645 in FY2024 to 48,530 in FY2026 [EV-DEM-105]. The pace needs CAP-11's tools in force from R1, and the 141,084 people who took deferred resignation [EV-DEM-083] are a pool that already knows the work.
- **For the design:** nothing in sections 4 or 5 changes now. The release rule (CAP-7.02) already pays against hiring, so a slow path holds money rather than losing it; if question 4 is answered yes, CAP-7.02 gains one sentence saying where the held balance goes (the finding above). The IRS's enforcement yield (G8) is the line most exposed, because for the IRS a slow path delays the yield more than it saves outlays (section 6, part 1.3). **For the booking step** (recommended, provisional; part 10, question 4): lay part G's allocation inside CAP-7 on the hiring pace and lag G8's yield with it: about $17 billion less outlay and about $16 billion less revenue over FY2030-39, about zero net, and no change in FY2040. The contingency is left as booked for stage G.

### 1.4 The immigration model: the court's corps

The model's central case needs about **1,673 judges in all (920 added)**, hired about 250 a year, to clear the backlog by **FY2035**; at 150 hires a year it clears in FY2037, and at the 800-judge cap that takes effect on November 1, 2028 [EV-IMM-105] it does not clear (the low and high cases are 2,403 and 863 judges). Judges on board rise from 1,003 in FY2030 to 1,253 in FY2031 and 1,503 in FY2032. So the court takes over on October 1, 2031 (working) with about 1,250 sitting judges, each needing a merit screen and a 14-year appointment by a court of appeals. That confirms section 5's "more than 1,000" and the need for provisional fix 2 (interim appointments of up to two years, CRT-5.03). The term-limited increment's steady-state need is about 726 judges, the basis for memo 9 part 2's split of about 200 of the 920 added judges on regular terms (section 6, part 5). The corps is reset in 2033 from receipts after legalization (CRT-5.06).

**Does the Court's mandatory docket outgrow its merits docket? (milestone fix 3; the review's L-2.)** CRT-11.02 makes a petition raising an acknowledged statutory circuit split an appeal the Court must decide, and CRT-3.02 adds direct appeals from the three-judge courts. Today the Court decides 64 argued cases a year in 56 signed opinions (OT2024 [EV-DEM-384]) and receives about one mandatory case a year (4 in 2018-21 [EV-DEM-388]).
- *Direct appeals:* about 3 to 90 a year (`5-delivery.md`, part 2, on our estimates).
- *Statutory splits:* the register holds no count of acknowledged splits. Working assumption: 20 to 80 petitions a year raise an acknowledged statutory split the Court now declines, about 1.5% to 6% of the 1,329 paid petitions of OT2024 [EV-DEM-384] (judgment). Splits the Court already takes are inside today's docket and add nothing.
- *Together:* **about 25 to 170 mandatory cases a year on top of today's docket.** At the low end that is under half the merits docket; in the middle (about 60 to 90) it equals it; at the high end, in a period of national litigation like 2025-26, it is between two and three times it, near the roughly 150 cases a year the Court decided in the 1980s [EV-DEM-388]. So yes: in an active period the two rules together exceed the Court's present merits docket.
- *What follows.* The Court would decide most of these summarily, as CRT-11.02 allows, so the argued docket need not grow; but more national questions would be settled without full briefing and argument, which is the legacy's caution that mandatory jurisdiction "can expand the Court's policy reach as easily as discipline it" (`domain-courts.md`:158). It is a review point, not a defect: section 8 (part 5) names the signpost and the responses, and either response would go to Espen.

**Who is not reached, and why.**
- *Voters in states that resist the floor* are reached only after notice and a suit (DEM-1.13, decided `DEC-2026-11-18-12`); the Attorney General can sue at once, without notice. With every rule due at the first covered election (D3), an office that falls behind is found in 2030, not 2032.
