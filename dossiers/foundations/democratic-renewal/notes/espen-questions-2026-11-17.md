## a. Memo 1: a voter ID design that is free, easy, more secure and more trusted

**What the evidence says.**
- *Turnout:* the best national study finds strict ID laws had no effect on registration or turnout in 2008-2018, overall or for any group (Cantoni and Pons, a 1.6-billion-observation panel, published 2021) [EV-DEM-154]. GAO found turnout fell 1.9 to 3.2 points more in Kansas and Tennessee after their laws (2014) [EV-DEM-155]; Heritage disputes GAO's comparison [EV-DEM-156]. Read together: the average effect is small or zero, but a law can still keep particular people from voting.
- *Fraud:* an ID rule only stops a person voting as someone else at the polls, which is very rare. The fraud the audits find is mostly noncitizen registration, and that is rare too: 20 noncitizens among 8.2 million Georgia registrants [EV-DEM-163]; 27 confirmed in Utah's 2.07 million records [EV-DEM-164]; about 30 suspected cases in 23.5 million votes in 2016 [EV-DEM-162]. An ID card does not prove citizenship.
- *Trust:* the same national study finds strict ID has **no effect on fraud, actual or perceived** (Cantoni and Pons, abstract) [EV-DEM-154]. So the evidence does not show that ID requirements raise trust, even though they poll well. Trust rose where the government did the work: Medicaid enrollees renewed automatically trusted the agency more (75% against 66%) [EV-DEM-186].

**What "free and easy" would require.** A requirement meets Espen's test only if no eligible voter is turned away for lacking a card:
1. *A free card, sent, not fetched.* Under memo 1 B the government registers people from its records. It can mail each new registrant a free voter card with a photo option, as the 2005 Carter-Baker commission paired photo ID with free cards and outreach (outside source, below).
2. *A broad list.* Any current or recently expired government, tribal, student or benefit card, including digital ones, counts.
3. *Free underlying papers.* No fee for the birth certificate or other record needed for the card.
4. *A fallback that counts.* A voter without a card signs a statement under penalty of perjury and casts a regular ballot, checked against records afterward. This is memo 1 B's existing rule. Without it, a requirement turns eligible people away.

**Recommendation.** Keep memo 1 B. Add the free voter card mailed from records as the floor's ID answer, accepted everywhere and paid from DEM-11 (judgment: well under $0.1 billion a year). Do **not** make a photo ID mandatory without the signed-statement fallback: the evidence says that adds burden without measurable security or trust. That gives Espen a credible "every voter gets a free ID" position, which is the politics Espen named, with B's protections intact.

## b. Memo 2 part 2: where else a results (disparate-impact) test might fit

**Where it stands today.**
- Already in law: employment (Title VII, written into the statute in 1991), housing (the Fair Housing Act, *Inclusive Communities*, 2015) and, more narrowly, age discrimination in jobs.
- Largely gone: programs that take federal money (Title VI). *Alexander v. Sandoval* (2001) ended private suits on Title VI's effects regulations. Executive Order 14281 (April 2025) told agencies to drop disparate-impact enforcement "in all contexts to the maximum degree possible", and the Justice Department removed its Title VI effects rules in December 2025 (outside sources, below).

**Candidates for a statutory results test:** Title VI programs, the largest gap (schools' discipline and tracking, environmental permits, health care, transit); health care under the ACA's Section 1557; credit (ECOA, now resting on regulation); and automated decisions by algorithms, the newest gap.

**Views.**
- Civil-rights advocates (the Legal Defense Fund, the Leadership Conference) have long backed restoring a private right to sue on Title VI's effects standard. Bills to do so date to the Kennedy-Lewis Civil Rights Act of 2008.
- Critics (Heritage, the America First Policy Institute) argue a results test pushes institutions toward quotas and conflicts with equal protection, a question Justice Scalia raised in *Ricci* (2009).
- The light search found no recent scholarly survey; that is still to find.

**Recommendation.** Keep DEM-1D held: the voting bill should not carry it. Send "a statutory effects standard and a private right of action under Title VI, with algorithmic decisions covered" to the rights topic (`agenda/rights`) as a candidate, to weigh with that topic's evidence.

## c. Memo 6: Espen's terms, and whether automatic penalties survive

**Does memo 6 A already meet Espen's terms?**
- *Funds:* only by implication. A says officials comply by "divesting into broad funds or a qualified blind trust", which implies diversified funds are allowed. It does not say so. **Treasuries, municipal bonds and the TSP are not mentioned.**
- *Forced sales:* A does not force a sale, because a blind trust is an alternative. But its "Against" line on taxes from "forced sales" reads as if it does.
- The Senate bills A matches (the ETHICS Act, the HONEST Act) list permitted holdings explicitly. Section 4 should do the same.
- **Fix in section 4:**
  - define covered holdings as individual securities, with diversified mutual funds and ETFs, Treasuries, municipal bonds, the TSP and other retirement accounts expressly excluded;
  - offer three ways to comply: hold only excluded assets, use a qualified blind trust, or divest;
  - extend the capital-gains deferral that executive officials get when they sell to comply (the certificate of divestiture, 26 U.S.C. 1043) to members, so a sale costs no immediate tax.

**Do automatic penalties survive?** Likely yes, if section 4 writes them carefully:
- *Speech or Debate Clause.* It protects legislative acts, not trading. A penalty computed from the disclosure form needs no inquiry into votes or committee work, which is the design's strength. Proving insider trading would need that inquiry.
- *Each chamber's power to discipline its members* (Article I, section 5). A statute cannot make a chamber vote to punish. So write the penalty as a civil liability under the statute, collected by the chamber's ethics office and enforceable by the Justice Department in district court if unpaid. Each chamber adopts a matching rule. The STOCK Act's late-filing fee already works this way.
- *Due process.* Give notice, a short window to contest the computation, and an appeal before collection. A fine proportionate to the trade (the memo's 10%) avoids an Excessive Fines problem. A district-court backstop also avoids *Jarkesy* (part p).
- *The President and Vice President.* This is the weakest part, since conflict-of-interest law has never reached them. Keep it severable, as A does.

## d. Memo 7: 13 Justices for 13 circuits; sitting Justices in a separable part

**History.**
- Congress set the Court's size seven times between 1789 and 1869 (`legacy/domains/domain-courts.md`, CRT-16 section).
- The size tracked the circuits, because Justices rode circuit. In 1863 there were ten Justices. The 1866 Judicial Circuits Act cut the circuits to nine and the seats to seven by attrition. The Judiciary Act of 1869 set nine Justices, one for each of the nine circuits.
- Today there are 13 courts of appeals, and each Justice is still allotted to one or more circuits for emergency applications (28 U.S.C. 42). The Judiciary Act of 2021 (Johnson and Nadler in the House; Markey in the Senate) proposed 13 Justices on exactly this argument (outside sources, below).

**For.**
- A neutral, historical rationale: one Justice per circuit, as in 1869.
- The circuits' emergency applications are spread more evenly.
- With a regional-diversity design, the Court draws from the whole country. Circuit judges already must live in their circuit (28 U.S.C. 44(c)).

**Against and risks.**
- The circuit link has been formal since circuit riding ended, so opponents will call it court-packing whatever the rationale. Support is 53/47 overall and 35% among Republicans, against 79% for term limits (legacy CRT-16 note).
- The next unified government can answer with 15, and the Court's legitimacy pays for it. This is why CRT-16's automatic trigger was removed.
- A residency requirement for Justices would be new and would invite an Appointments Clause challenge. A stated expectation is safer than a requirement.
- **It changes the term design.** Eighteen-year terms with an appointment every two years make a Court of nine. A Court of 13 needs appointments about every 1.4 years, or 26-year terms. Section 4 would have to re-solve the arithmetic.

**Recommendation.** Keep it on the table as Espen said (`DEC-2026-11-17-85`), not in the first bills. If pursued:
- phase the four seats across two presidential terms, so no one President fills them;
- tie them to the term statute, so the Court grows as terms begin;
- give it a findings record on circuit workload.

**Sitting Justices in a separable part: yes.** Memo 7 A already has "a severable provision on sitting Justices". It is the part most likely to be struck under "during good Behaviour" (memo 7, option B). A severability clause keeps the prospective statute standing if it falls, as the Court applies such clauses (for example, *Seila Law*, 2020).

## e. Memo 9 part 1: the immigration court

**What the legacy plan said.**
- The legacy file chose A, an Article I court on the Real Courts Act model, with restored Article III review and an express fallback into the judicial branch (`legacy/domains/domain-courts.md`, CRT-5).
- The Rebalancing review set out three paths (`legacy/sources/courts-rebalancing-the-three-branches-2026.md`, part 12):
  - (a) "Moving cases into Article III courtrooms": restore district-court habeas and pattern-and-practice jurisdiction, with de novo review of law and mixed questions;
  - (b) full Article III conversion;
  - (c) the Article I court.
- Its assessment: "Enact (c) now with expanded Article III review under (a); treat (b) as a long-term aspiration."
- **Espen's idea is the legacy path (a).** The legacy plan did it *alongside* the new court, not instead of one.

**Espen's idea compared.**
- *If it means faster review by federal courts* (memo 9 B):
  - it is simple and cheap;
  - but the trial judges stay Justice Department employees removable at will. The 2026 MSPB ruling held them removable (legacy source, part 12). That fails the independence requirement Espen already decided for the immigration flagship (`DEC-2026-11-12-07`; `dossiers/immigration/memos/4-asylum-courts.md`, part 2, item 4). B alone would mean reopening that decision.
- *If it means moving trial-level cases themselves into district courts:*
  - about 670 district judges cannot absorb 3.3 million cases [EV-IMM-100];
  - each trial would take longer.
- *A, the Article I court:* independent, on a model Congress has used (the Tax Court) [EV-DEM-244]. Its exposed point is the judges' removal protection after *Slaughter*.
- *C, full Article III:* the clearest independence, but 700-1,000 Senate confirmations and life tenure for a corps sized for a backlog.

**A simpler middle form worth adding: the bankruptcy-court model.** Make immigration judges a unit of the district courts, appointed by the courts of appeals for fixed 14-year terms, as bankruptcy judges are (28 U.S.C. 152).
- They sit inside the Article III courts, outside the executive, so *Slaughter* does not reach them, and no Senate confirmations are needed.
- The existing judges transfer.
- Review runs to the district court or circuit on questions of law, which is Espen's simpler path.
- Its risk: the public-rights limits on non-Article III judges, which the legacy file finds cover immigration (CRT-5).

**Recommendation.** A, written on the bankruptcy-court model (call it A′) if Espen wants the simplest independent form, with path (a)'s expanded review in every case. Not B alone, which fails the flagship's independence requirement. Espen decides; until then the row stays open.

## f. Memo 9 part 3: public defenders are separate from civil legal aid

- **Yes, separate.**
  - Criminal defense is a constitutional right (*Gideon*, 1963), paid mostly by states and counties, and for federal cases by the judiciary's Defender Services account.
  - Civil legal aid (the Legal Services Corporation) has no such right.
  - Memo 9 part 3 covers civil aid only.
- **Where it sits in the plan:** in the public safety topic, not this foundation. The legacy SAF-7, "swift courts and victims", funds "prosecutors, public defenders and judges to cut the time from arrest to trial", about $600 million a year for swift courts through the Safety Compacts' formula funding (SAF-10) (`legacy/domains/domain-public-safety.md`, lines 19, 47, 95-96; `legacy/domains/public-safety-safe-everywhere.md`, line 244). The topic (`agenda/public-safety`) has no dossier yet.
- **What is known:**
  - The 2023 National Public Defense Workload Study (RAND, the National Center for State Courts and the ABA) found the 1973 caseload standards, about 150 felonies a lawyer a year, "outdated, not empirically based"; its new standards allow far fewer cases per lawyer.
  - Coverage of the study reports rural caseloads of 350 felonies a lawyer (outside sources, below).
  - No national count of the shortage was found.
- **Recommendation:** a direction to the public safety topic. Build public defense into SAF-7's grants, with funding tied to the 2023 workload standards and parity with prosecutors, and fully fund federal Defender Services. Size it in that topic's dossier.

## g. Memo 10 part 2: is pacing plus hiring authority the best way to build capacity and hold agencies to account?

**It is necessary, not sufficient.**
- The evidence says hiring, not money, sets the pace [EV-DEM-277] [EV-DEM-085]. So releasing money against hiring paths, with direct-hire authority, avoids paying for staff who cannot be hired.
- It does not by itself make agencies deliver, or show the public whether they do.

**What else section 4 should build in:**
1. **Published delivery measures for every plan program:** time to a decision, error rates, call-wait times and take-up, reported quarterly. The money's next tranche follows these as well as hiring. A program whose agency is behind starts later; it does not shrink (memo 10).
2. **A small delivery unit at OMB** that tracks the plan's dated needs (part G's first binding dates: July 2030 for the IRS's payment file, January 2031 for CMS's wave 1) and escalates when an agency slips.
3. **Pay and hiring tools beyond direct hire:**
   - special pay rates for technology and actuarial jobs;
   - rehiring former staff without pay penalties (memo 10, CAP-11);
   - a time-to-hire target. The register has no time-to-hire series by agency (`../3-design.md`, "Evidence still needed").
4. **Product teams, not contracts alone:** the digital service corps (point 46, built on the existing digital services). Direct File cost $16.2 million against a $61.2 million estimate [EV-DEM-279], and states with modern systems started pandemic benefits 8-15 days faster [EV-DEM-295].
5. **Independent checks at set points:** GAO audits of the hiring paths and the contingency's releases, and inspectors general with the staff to follow the money (the integrity units' line).
6. **Protection from sabotage:** memo 12's civil-service statute and payment continuity, so capacity built is not undone by order.

**Recommendation.** Keep B, and add items 1 to 5 to section 4 as the accountability half of CAP-7. They cost little beyond what part G already sizes.

## h. Memo 13: an earlier House enlargement, a state option, or reform in stages

**One fact decides the options.** Since 1967, federal law requires single-member House districts (2 U.S.C. 2c), and the House's size is fixed at 435 by statute (2 U.S.C. 2a). Both are ordinary laws Congress can change.

| Path | What it does | For | Against |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A, the staged package (decided)** | Enlargement, multi-member ranked districts and the presidential method together in 2033; numbers in 2031 | One coherent reform; the numbers set after the 2030 census and the post-*Callais* modeling | Needs a second trifecta; if 2033 slips, nothing happens |
| **Earlier enlargement, single-member districts** | A larger House (about 692 on the cube-root rule [EV-DEM-249]) apportioned from the 2030 census for the 2032 election | No amendment; smaller districts (from about 761,000 people [EV-DEM-075]); the Electoral College tracks population more closely; memo 3's criteria apply | Competes with DEM-1 for floor time in 2029-30; a larger single-member House still elects winner-take-all; switching to multi-member districts later is a second redraw; the chamber build is unscored |
| **State option for multi-member ranked districts** | Amend 2 U.S.C. 2c to *permit*, not require, ranked-choice multi-member districts | Small, cheap, could ride DEM-1; lets willing states show results | States choosing it cost their own majority party seats, so uptake would be low and one-sided. That is why the Fair Representation Act requires it [EV-DEM-258]. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting [EV-DEM-255] |

**A staged version** that keeps Espen's package:
1. In 2029-30, lift 2 U.S.C. 2c's mandate for willing states, alongside DEM-2's criteria.
2. In the 2031 review, decide the size, aiming at the 2032 election if the census numbers are in time, otherwise 2034's one-off redraw.
3. In 2033, decide nationwide multi-member districts and the presidential method on the evidence from the states that opted in.

**Recommendation.** The staged version is a real improvement in feasibility, at the cost of the package's "together or not at all" logic. It is Espen's call. If Espen takes it, memo 13 becomes "A, staged".

## i. Point 8: a constitutional amendment alongside the compact

- **The precedent:** the Bayh-Celler amendment for a direct national vote passed the House 338-70 in 1969 and died to a Senate filibuster in 1970.
- **What an amendment needs:** two-thirds of each chamber and 38 states, so it is a long track.
- **What it adds:**
  - a durable rule that cannot be withdrawn;
  - an end to the compact's legal doubts: whether Congress must consent, which CRS finds unresolved [EV-DEM-266]; states leaving near an election; how recounts are counted;
  - a place to write the presidential method (a runoff or ranked choice, DEM-9).
- **Recommendation:**
  - write one amendment, not two: make the national popular vote the text of AMD-3's presidential-method amendment (wave three, tied to memo 13);
  - push the compact to 270 meanwhile, from the current 222 [EV-DEM-076];
  - with a trifecta, consider Congress **granting** the compact its consent by statute, which removes the CRS doubt at little cost. Point 8 had said "without asking consent"; this is the one change to weigh.

## j. Point 13: citizens' assemblies

**How they work.**
- About 100 to 150 people are chosen by lot, stratified to match the country by age, region, education and party.
- They are paid for their time. Over several weekends they hear experts and advocates on all sides, deliberate in small groups with trained facilitators, and vote on recommendations.
- Under the legacy design (DEM-18; `legacy/domains/domain-democracy.md`, "DEM-18"):
  - agencies may convene assemblies on questions referred to them, with an evaluation published each time;
  - Congress may refer up to three questions per Congress to national assemblies;
  - small grants support state and local ones, inside DEM-16's line.
  - Point 13 adds the duty to answer: the referring body responds in writing to each recommendation within a set time.

**What they achieve.**
- Informed public judgment on values questions politics is stuck on. Ireland's 99-member assembly broke years of deadlock on abortion, and the referendum bill followed its repeal-and-replace recommendation [EV-DEM-175] [EV-DEM-177].
- The OECD counted 289 such processes by 2019 [EV-DEM-180]. Where implementation was tracked, authorities carried out over half the recommendations in 76% of cases [EV-DEM-181].
- The failure case is France's climate convention: recommendations no one was bound to answer [EV-DEM-179]. Hence the duty to answer.

**Why they are preferable to the alternatives.**
- *Polls* measure first reactions, not judgment.
- *Public comment periods* are dominated by organized interests and mass campaigns.
- *Referendums* have no deliberation, and the federal government has none.
- *Expert commissions* lack public legitimacy.
- An assembly combines a representative sample with time and evidence.

**Limits.**
- Assemblies are advisory, and the statute says so.
- Agenda-setting and the choice of experts can tilt them, so publish both.
- U.S. evidence at the national level is thin.

**Cost:** about zero (legacy).

**Recommendation:** yes, as recommended: three national pilots with the duty to answer, evaluated.

## l. Point 16: what treating single-candidate super PACs as part of the campaign achieves

- **The problem.**
  - Since 2010, super PACs may raise unlimited sums if they spend independently.
  - A single-candidate super PAC is often run by the candidate's former aides or family, raises money with the candidate's help and supports only that candidate. It is coordination in all but name.
  - Super PACs and hybrid PACs spent more than $4.1 billion of 2024's $4.5 billion of outside spending [EV-DEM-024].
- **What INT-8 does.**
  - A super PAC that meets objective tests is presumed coordinated, and its spending counts as contributions to the campaign, subject to the limits. The tests: it supports one candidate; it is run by recent campaign staff or family; the candidate raised money for it; it shares vendors with the campaign.
  - This restores the meaning of contribution limits and cuts the leverage of single megadonors. It leaves real independent groups alone.
- **Legal footing.** Coordinated spending has been treated as a contribution since *Buckley*. *NRSC* (2026) struck the limits on **party** coordinated spending [EV-DEM-027], and its reasoning may be pressed against the others. Hence objective, rebuttable tests and severability (legacy INT-8: "put the coordination effort entirely into outside groups").
- **Recommendation:** yes, as recommended.

## m. Point 20: a broader bar on federal contractors' contributions

- **The bar exists.**
  - 52 U.S.C. 30119 already bars federal contractors, firms and individuals, from contributing in federal elections, from the start of negotiations until the contract is performed.
  - The en banc D.C. Circuit upheld it for individual contractors in *Wagner v. FEC* (2015), and the Supreme Court denied review in 2016 (outside sources, below).
- **Its gaps.**
  1. It does not reach a contracting firm's owners, officers or employees giving personally, and it lets a firm's separate PAC contribute.
  2. Enforcement depends on complaints: nothing matches contractor records (SAM, USAspending) against FEC receipts. The light search found no enforcement record, so the number of cases and penalties is still to find. The FEC has lacked a quorum [EV-DEM-031] [EV-DEM-032].
- **A tested model for (1): "pay-to-play" rules.**
  - The municipal securities rule (MSRB G-37) and the SEC's adviser rule (206(4)-5) bar firms from business for two years after their executives give to the officials who award it.
  - G-37 was upheld in *Blount v. SEC* (D.C. Cir., 1995).
- **Recommendation:**
  - extend the bar to the PACs and covered executives of contractors above a contract-size threshold, for contributions to officials who influence the award;
  - add automatic matching of contractor IDs against FEC receipts, with a refund notice to the receiving committee within 30 days;
  - enforce through memo 5's restructured FEC and its citizen suit.

## n. Point 23: paid speeches and later pay

- **What exists.**
  - The Ethics Reform Act of 1989 barred honoraria for federal employees.
  - The Supreme Court struck that ban for rank-and-file employees (*United States v. NTEU*, 1995). It still binds members and senior officials while they serve.
- **The gap is after service.** A company an official regulated pays a large speaking fee or a board seat once they leave, a deferred reward. The evidence on the revolving door is that access, not expertise, is what is sold [EV-DEM-221] [EV-DEM-222].
- **What INT-13 would do:** bar senior officials, for a set period after leaving, from taking speaking fees or pay from entities with matters they handled.
- **Legal risk:** former officials are private citizens with speech rights, and *NTEU* struck an overbroad ban. Narrow tailoring is the defense:
  - senior officials only;
  - only payers with matters the official participated in;
  - two years (judgment);
  - with five years of public disclosure of any such pay.
- **Recommendation:** yes, in that narrow form, paired with point 21's cooling-off periods.

## o. Point 31: remedies after *CASA*

- **What changed.** *Trump v. CASA* (June 2025) held that universal injunctions likely exceed federal courts' equitable power [EV-DEM-047]. It left class actions and vacatur of rules under the APA open [EV-DEM-050]. A year on, CRS finds no systemic shift: plaintiffs still win broad relief through class actions and vacatur [EV-DEM-049] [EV-DEM-246].
- **What CRT-9 does:**
  - say in statute that the APA's "set aside" means vacatur of the rule;
  - state when relief beyond the parties is available;
  - route those cases to three-judge courts with expedited review.
  - It is symmetrical: it binds challenges to any administration's actions, unlike the House's No Rogue Rulings Act, which only restricts relief (`legacy/domains/domain-courts.md`, CRT-9).
- **Why the second Congress:** there is no urgency, given CRS's finding. The plan's own laws will be challenged first, and the rule cuts both ways, so it is better written once that case law shows how the courts treat it.
- **Recommendation:** yes, as recommended.

## p. Point 32: civil penalties after *Jarkesy*

- **What changed.** *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) held that when an agency seeks civil penalties for claims like common-law fraud, the Seventh Amendment requires a jury. So penalties decided by an agency's own judges are exposed for many agencies.
- **What CRT-10 does:** let agencies bring penalty cases in district court, with a jury where required, or restructure penalties within the "public rights" category that *Jarkesy* left to agencies. Enforcement becomes slower but survives.
- **Why second wave:** the case law is still sorting which agencies' penalties are affected.
- **The plan's own new penalties should be written for district court from the start,** through CRT-8's drafting rule. Memo 6's stock-ban penalty is designed that way (part c).
- **Recommendation:** yes, as recommended, with that drafting rule now.

## q. Point 33: what mandatory jurisdiction and a screening division mean

- **Today the Court picks its cases.** The Court decides nearly all its cases by choosing them (certiorari). It decided 56 signed-opinion cases in OT2024, against more than 160 a year in the 1980s (legacy CRT-11).
- **Mandatory jurisdiction** means the Court *must* decide certain appeals. Before 1988 it had many such categories; the 1988 Supreme Court Case Selections Act removed most. The legacy proposal restores two:
  - cases where a lower court strikes down a federal statute;
  - acknowledged circuit splits.
- **A certiorari screening division** means a panel, for example of circuit judges, would choose or recommend which petitions the Court hears, taking agenda-setting partly from the Justices.
- **Limiting certiorari before judgment** stops the Court taking cases before the court of appeals has ruled.
- **Why the recommendation was "no, for now":**
  - mandatory jurisdiction makes the Court decide *more* merits questions, which "can expand the Court's policy reach as easily as discipline it";
  - a screening division drawn from lower courts raises a question under "one supreme Court".
- **Recommendation for Espen, who favors docket reform:** a narrow yes.
  - **Limit certiorari before judgment.** It supports the emergency-docket reform (point 27).
  - **Mandatory review of acknowledged circuit splits on federal statutes,** so the same law means the same thing everywhere.
  - **Hold** the screening division and mandatory review of statute invalidations.

## s. Point 38: impeachment for corruption behind a ruling; when jurisdiction-stripping has been used

**Impeachment: yes, corruption that produces a ruling fits.**
- The Constitution names "Bribery" beside "other high Crimes and Misdemeanors". The offense is the corruption, not the content of the ruling.
- Precedents:
  - **Judge Thomas Porteous** was impeached in March 2010 on four articles, including taking money from lawyers with a case before him and failing to recuse in it. The Senate convicted him on all four on December 8, 2010 (CRS; outside sources, below).
  - Alcee Hastings (1989, a bribery conspiracy over sentences), Robert Archbald (1913, favors from litigants) and Halsted Ritter (1936) were also removed for corruption.
  - Justice Samuel Chase's acquittal (1805) set the norm against impeaching over rulings or politics.
- **Recommendation:** CRT-18's declared standard should say exactly this: "never for the content of a ruling; always available for bribery, corruption or concealed interests, including where they produced a ruling" (legacy CRT-18: "corruption and serious misconduct, never rulings").

**Jurisdiction-stripping has a long record.**
- *Ex parte McCardle* (1869): Congress withdrew the Court's habeas appeal jurisdiction mid-case, and the Court accepted it.
- The Norris-LaGuardia Act (1932) stripped federal courts of power to enjoin strikes, a progressive use.
- Wartime price control channeled challenges to one court (*Yakus*, 1944).
- The 1996 and 2005 immigration laws limited review.
- The limits: Congress may not dictate a case's outcome (*Klein*, 1872), and may not suspend habeas without an adequate substitute (*Boumediene*, 2008).
- **The plan already uses this power:** channeling rule challenges to one court (point 29) and three-judge courts (point 26) are jurisdiction statutes.
- **Recommendation:** word CRT-17's norm as "no statute leaves constitutional claims with no federal forum". Channeling, timing and remedies stay available tools, as Espen asked.

## t. Point 44: a budget for the statute-tracking office

- **Comparators.** The House's Office of the Law Revision Counsel, which keeps the U.S. Code, is about $4-5 million a year: $3.9 million in the House's FY2024 bill, $5.0 million requested for FY2025 (outside sources, below).
- **The office's work:** reading every federal court decision that construes a statute, flagging those that narrow or misread one, and drafting a fix for the committees.
- **Estimate (judgment):**
  - **about $8 million a year, range $5-12 million**: 20 to 40 lawyers and analysts at about $200,000-250,000 each fully loaded, plus about $1-2 million for case-tracking data and software;
  - about **$0.06-0.12 billion over FY2030-39** and about $10 million in FY2040, indexed;
  - inside the legacy "about $0.00" (`legacy/domains/domain-capacity.md`, CAP-6).
- **Recommendation:** house it in CRS's legal division, or as a joint office reporting to both chambers' legislative counsel. That shares CRS's overhead and its record of nonpartisanship.
