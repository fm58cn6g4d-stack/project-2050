# Democratic renewal: what works

Role: section 2 of the democratic renewal foundation. It sets out the research, the programs at home and abroad, and the failures to avoid, for the seven parts section 1 framed (`1-problem.md`):
- A. the voting floor and election administration;
- B. money in politics;
- C. ethics and integrity;
- D. courts;
- E. participation and civic life;
- F. long-run institutional reform;
- G. a government that delivers: agency capacity, payment systems, and remedies when delivery fails.

Status: **draft** (stage F, step 2). **Research only: it recommends nothing and makes no design choice.** DEM-2's structure, the size of each capacity line and every agency share go to later sections and to Espen's memo set (`README.md`, "Directions for later stages"). No agenda item's level or status changes here.

How to read it:
- Every figure cites the evidence register (`evidence.json`; this section adds `EV-DEM-145` to `EV-DEM-319`), or another dossier's register where that already holds the fact.
- Each claim that may later carry a recommendation names its **evidence strength**: randomized trial, quasi-experimental, observational, program evaluation, official data, simulation, international practice or judgment. It also says where the claim was tried.
- Where the research disagrees, both views are given, and the section does not pick one.
- Analysis by executive agencies is weighed with the skepticism `DEC-2026-10-30-01` sets. Claims by the second Trump administration (2025 on) are labeled as its view and carry nothing on their own.
- The legacy designs are weighed as working choices (`DEC-2026-10-30-02`), and the legacy record as evidence with no presumption (`DEC-2026-11-13-15`). Where new evidence confirms, qualifies or cuts against a legacy design, the section says so, with file and line.
- The design lens, "guide, don't police" (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`), runs through part G: each test, document and deadline has to earn its place against its burden.

## In brief

- **Registration rules move registration more than turnout.**
  - Automatic voter registration adds many people to the rolls: the Brennan Center puts the increase at 9 to 94 percent by state, from a matching design by an advocate [EV-DEM-145]. Its turnout effect is small or uncertain: about 1 point in one study and not significant in others [EV-DEM-146] [EV-DEM-147].
  - Same-day registration raises turnout among 18-24-year-olds by 3.2 to 7.3 points (quasi-experimental) [EV-DEM-148]. Preregistration at 16 and 17 also raises youth turnout, about 8 points among Florida preregistrants (regression discontinuity) [EV-DEM-149].
  - Australia enrolls voters directly from government records and covers 97.8% of eligible people [EV-DEM-168]. Canada's permanent register covered 96.4% of electors in 2019 [EV-DEM-170].
- **Vote by mail raises turnout about 2 points with no detectable partisan effect**, in two independent peer-reviewed studies [EV-DEM-150] [EV-DEM-151].
- **Strict voter ID is contested.** A national panel finds no effect on turnout or fraud [EV-DEM-154]. GAO found turnout fell 1.9 to 3.2 points more in Kansas and Tennessee than in comparison states [EV-DEM-155]. Heritage disputes GAO's comparison [EV-DEM-156].
- **Preclearance raised long-run turnout 4 to 8 points** through minority participation (quasi-experimental) [EV-DEM-157]. Noncitizen voting is very rare in official audits, for example 20 noncitizens among 8.2 million Georgia registrants [EV-DEM-163] [EV-DEM-164].
- **Small-donor public financing broadens who gives, but not as much as hoped.**
  - New York City's matched races drew small donors from 89% of the city's block groups, against 30% for unmatched races [EV-DEM-193].
  - Whether Seattle's vouchers bring in new kinds of donors is contested [EV-DEM-190] [EV-DEM-191].
  - No study finds that full public funding reduces polarization; one finds it increases it [EV-DEM-195] [EV-DEM-196].
  - The presidential system collapsed once its limits fell behind outside money. No major-party nominee has taken the general-election grant since 2008 [EV-DEM-202] [EV-DEM-204].
- **Ethics rules work when someone enforces them.**
  - Whether members of Congress beat the market is contested. Studies after 2012 find no edge for the average member [EV-DEM-217], but one finds that leaders outperform their peers by 47 points a year [EV-DEM-219].
  - The STOCK Act's usual penalty is $200, or waived [EV-DEM-220].
  - State ethics commissions show no measurable effect on corruption [EV-DEM-227].
- **Courts.**
  - The United States is the only major constitutional democracy with neither a term nor an age limit for its high court [EV-DEM-231].
  - Whether a statute can create term limits is contested [EV-DEM-233] [EV-DEM-234] [EV-DEM-235].
  - A right to counsel in eviction cases cut possessory judgments by 32.1 points in New York (quasi-experimental, an upper bound) [EV-DEM-242]. Funding and provider staffing then fell behind [EV-DEM-243].
- **Participation.** Participatory budgeting is linked to lower infant mortality in Brazil [EV-DEM-172] [EV-DEM-173]. It raised participants' voting 8.4 points in New York City [EV-DEM-174]. Deliberative assemblies work when someone acts on them: Ireland did, France mostly did not [EV-DEM-176] [EV-DEM-179].
- **Institutional reform.** California's commission maps were more competitive than the 2001 legislative maps [EV-DEM-259], though a simulation study contests it [EV-DEM-260]. Commissions with no final authority were overridden [EV-DEM-261]. Ranked-choice voting often leaves winners short of a majority of all ballots cast [EV-DEM-251] [EV-DEM-252]. New Zealand's switch to proportional representation raised the share of women and Māori members, and voters kept it [EV-DEM-257] [EV-DEM-256].
- **Capacity pays, but it is slow to rebuild.**
  - CBO finds each added IRS enforcement dollar returns more than it costs, with falling returns [EV-DEM-270] [EV-DEM-271]. Audit research that counts deterrence finds more than $12 per $1 at the top [EV-DEM-273].
  - With Inflation Reduction Act money in hand, the IRS still lost more revenue agents than it hired in early FY2023 [EV-DEM-277].
- **Delivery depends on records the government already holds.**
  - On-demand SNAP interviews raised approvals 6 points in a randomized trial [EV-DEM-318].
  - Automatic Medicaid renewal ranged from 99% to 3% by state [EV-DEM-319].
  - Agencies attribute about 73% of FY2021-24 overpayments to failure to use existing data [EV-DEM-291].
  - The 2025 Direct File cost $16.2 million against a $61.2 million estimate [EV-DEM-279].
- **Remedies and failures.** Australia's Robodebt cost A$2.4 billion in repayments and compensation [EV-DEM-303]. The Dutch childcare benefits scandal brought down a government [EV-DEM-305]. Both were automated recovery with no hardship rule and no human check [EV-DEM-304].

## Part A. The voting floor and election administration

Section 1 measured the gaps (part A): 73.6% of voting-age citizens registered in 2024, with shortfalls by age, education and race [EV-DEM-001] [EV-DEM-003]. This part asks which rules move those numbers.

### A.1 Registration

| Reform | Finding | Strength; where | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Automatic voter registration (AVR) | Registrations rose 9 to 94 percent by state | Matching (quasi-experimental), advocate (Brennan Center); 15 states and D.C. by 2019 | [EV-DEM-145] |
| AVR, turnout | About 1 point higher in one 2018 specification; about 0.5 and not significant in another; larger for the young | Observational cross-section, one election | [EV-DEM-146] |
| AVR, by state | Youth registration up 5.1 points; state estimates from +9.4 (Oregon) to −2.6 (Connecticut), none significant | Difference-in-differences and synthetic control (PPIC and USC authors, secondary account) | [EV-DEM-147] |
| Same-day registration | Turnout of 18-24-year-olds up 3.2 to 7.3 points; 6.3 points on matching | Quasi-experimental (Census CPS); about 20 states and D.C. | [EV-DEM-148] |
| Preregistration at 16-17 | Youth turnout up 13% in a difference-in-differences model (2% lower bound in a lag model); about 8 points among Florida preregistrants | Quasi-experimental (regression discontinuity), peer-reviewed | [EV-DEM-149] |
| Online registration | Average build cost $249,000; $0.50 to $2.34 saved per transaction against paper; California saved almost $2 million in 2012 | States' self-reported data (Pew) | [EV-DEM-166] |
| Notice after rights are restored | An official mailing raised registration of people with past felony convictions 1.8 points (from 5.9%) and voting from 3.0% to 3.9% | Randomized trial, Connecticut, 6,280 people | [EV-DEM-161] |

- **How to read AVR.** The registration gains are large and well measured. The turnout gains are small and uncertain, because many of the people added were unlikely voters to begin with. The studies differ on whether that is a modest gain or none (contexts of EV-DEM-146 and EV-DEM-147). The Brennan Center's response to the turnout studies was not read (Evidence still needed).
- **Abroad, registration is a state duty.** Australia enrolls people directly from government data. It enrolled 97.8% of eligible people in September 2026 [EV-DEM-168]. Coverage rose from 90.9% in 2010 to 98.2% at the 2025 election, which the electoral commission credits partly to direct enrolment from 2012. Turnout of the roll was 89.82% in 2022, under compulsory voting [EV-DEM-169]. Canada's permanent register covered 96.4% of electors for 2019, up from 92.7% in 2015 [EV-DEM-170]. *Strength:* official data and international practice. The attribution to direct enrolment is the agency's own.
- **Against the legacy design.** The legacy DEM-1E makes every benefit point the plan creates a voter-registration agency, with automatic registration on enrollment (`legacy/domains/domain-democracy.md`, line 94). The evidence supports its registration premise, and Australia's direct enrolment is the closest working model [EV-DEM-169]. It does not support reading registration gains as turnout gains [EV-DEM-146]. The legacy worked transaction already warns that a registration without a usable signature is not a counted ballot (`legacy/sources/democracy-medicaid-registration-transaction.md`, line 114).

### A.2 Casting a ballot

- **Vote by mail.**
  - In California, Utah and Washington counties over 1996-2018, universal vote by mail raised turnout about 2% of the citizen voting-age population, with no effect on either party's share [EV-DEM-150].
  - An independent team finds turnout up 1.8 to 2.9 points, and a Democratic vote-share effect of 0.7 points (interval −0.7 to 2), not significant [EV-DEM-151].
  - *Strength:* quasi-experimental (county difference-in-differences), peer-reviewed, in ordinary elections.
  - The other side: a campaign analyst's studies found very slight advantages running in opposite directions, Republicans in Colorado in 2014 and Democrats in Utah in 2016 [EV-DEM-152]. Political claims that it decides elections rest on no study [EV-DEM-153].
- **Strict voter ID: contested.**
  - Cantoni and Pons, with a national panel over 2008-2018, find no negative effect on registration or turnout (point estimates −0.1 points) and no effect on fraud. Campaign contact of non-white voters rose 4.7 points [EV-DEM-154]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental, peer-reviewed.
  - GAO estimated that 2012 turnout fell 1.9 to 2.2 points more in Kansas and 2.2 to 3.2 points more in Tennessee than in comparison states, for those states and that election only [EV-DEM-155]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental, nonpartisan agency.
  - Heritage disputes GAO: Kansas had no Senate race in 2012 while comparison state Maine did, and Kansas turnout of eligible voters was higher in 2012 than in 2000 [EV-DEM-156]. *Strength:* descriptive comparison, advocate.
  - Both views stand. The national study is larger and newer; GAO's design is closer to the laws' first use. Neither measures the burden on people who lack ID and did not try to vote.
- **Waiting to vote.** In 2016, residents of entirely black neighborhoods waited 29% longer than residents of entirely white ones, and were 74% more likely to wait more than 30 minutes [EV-DEM-158]. *Strength:* observational (smartphone location data), peer-reviewed. It measures the gap; no entry in the register evaluates a fix (Evidence still needed).
- **Ballots that count.** Pennsylvania mail voters told of a disqualifying error before Election Day were 25 points more likely to have their vote counted. Being able to correct it before Election Day added 10 points. About 20% of mail-ballot requesters gave no email address for notice [EV-DEM-167]. *Strength:* observational, reported by a news outlet; the study itself is still to be read. National rejection counts are in section 1 [EV-DEM-018].

### A.3 The federal floor and its enforcement

- **Preclearance worked while it ran.** Coverage under the 1975 revision raised long-run turnout 4 to 8 points through lasting gains in minority participation. Democratic support fell in covered areas, which the author attributes to white backlash [EV-DEM-157]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental, peer-reviewed; only the abstract was read. Preclearance has been inoperable since 2013 [EV-DEM-011], and *Callais* narrowed Section 2 [EV-DEM-007]. The legacy DEM-1 answers both in one bill, with "no guarantee of judicial survival" (`domain-democracy.md`, line 84). Whether that answer holds up is section 3's question.
- **Noncitizen voting is rare where it is checked.**
  - In 42 jurisdictions with large noncitizen populations, officials referred about 30 suspected cases among 23.5 million votes in 2016 [EV-DEM-162]. *Strength:* survey of officials, advocate (Brennan Center).
  - Georgia's 2024 audit found 20 noncitizens among 8.2 million registrants, nine of whom had voted in earlier elections [EV-DEM-163]. Utah's 2026 review confirmed 27 noncitizens among 2,069,640 records, 13 of whom had voted, and could not confirm 5,007 either way [EV-DEM-164]. *Strength:* official audits, both by Republican-led offices.
  - The other view: Heritage's election-law lead testified that noncitizens do register and vote, and that the extent is unknown because most states do not verify citizenship [EV-DEM-165]. *Strength:* judgment. Heritage's fraud database itself was not read.
  - This bears on proof-of-citizenship rules (section 1, A.3). In Utah's review, the unconfirmed records outnumbered the confirmed noncitizens about 185 to 1 (this section's arithmetic on EV-DEM-164). A documentary rule would land on the unconfirmed group.
- **Felony disenfranchisement.** About 4 million people were disenfranchised in 2024, 1.7% of the voting-eligible population, down 31% since 2016 as states restored rights [EV-DEM-159]. About 72% live in their communities and 40% have completed their sentences [EV-DEM-160]. *Strength:* modeled estimate, advocate. Restoration alone does little without notice (A.1) [EV-DEM-161]. These rows bear on the legacy DEM-12 (re-enfranchisement).
- **Federal election money.** Since 2018 the EAC has distributed over $1 billion in HAVA election security grants. By August 2024 states had spent over $638 million of it, about $343 million on voting equipment and cybersecurity [EV-DEM-171]. *Strength:* official data from interviews and an inventory; the EAC is without commissioners (section 1, A.4). No outcome evaluation of the grants was found. That leaves the legacy DEM-11's "$1-4B a year" (`domain-democracy.md`, line 220) still without a source.

## Part B. Money in politics

### B.1 Matching funds and vouchers

| Program | Finding | Strength; where | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New York City, matched Council races (2009) | Small donors from 89% of block groups, against 30% for unmatched State Assembly races in the city; participants raised 37% of private money from donors of $250 or less, against 15% for non-participants | Observational (matched against unmatched races in the same city); Brennan Center with the Campaign Finance Institute | [EV-DEM-193] |
| New York State, first cycle (2024) | Small in-district money plus match rose from under 5% to 45% of legislative candidates' funding; about 50,800 small in-district donors | Before-after, one cycle, by the program's designer and advocate | [EV-DEM-194] |
| Seattle Democracy Vouchers (2017) | 20,727 voucher users against 10,297 cash donors; 22% of voucher users from the wealthiest fifth of neighborhoods against nearly 30% of cash donors; voucher users still whiter than registered voters | Observational, first year (McCabe and Heerwig) | [EV-DEM-191] |
| Seattle, 2005-2021 | The vouchers mostly mobilize groups already overrepresented among donors; voucher users are as or less representative than cash donors on every measure except income | Quasi-experimental (difference-in-differences), peer-reviewed (Yorgason, APSR 2024) | [EV-DEM-190] [EV-DEM-192] |

- **The Seattle studies disagree on the test, not the facts.** Both find the voucher pool less wealthy than the cash pool. They differ on race and age, and on whether the right comparison is cash donors or the people newly brought in.
- **Against the legacy design.** The legacy INT-5 is a 6:1 match with voluntary spending caps and no trigger matching (`legacy/domains/domain-integrity.md`, line 76). The no-trigger rule is confirmed by *Arizona Free Enterprise* (2011), which struck matching that rose with an opponent's and outside groups' spending but did not question public financing itself [EV-DEM-208]. The register has no evaluation of a 6:1 ratio as such, and no cost per new donor; New York City's payments are in section 1 [EV-DEM-033].

### B.2 Full public funding: who runs, and polarization

- **Take-up is high where the grant is large enough.** Almost 85% of Connecticut legislative candidates use the Citizens' Election Program [EV-DEM-199]. In Maine, full public funding went from 33% of general-election candidates (2000) to over 80% (2006 and 2008), and in Arizona from 26% to 64% (2008) [EV-DEM-200]. *Strength:* official data.
- **Who runs.** Across all state legislatures in 1976-2018, public financing went with 12 to 18 more legislative candidates per state-year, which the author calls suggestive [EV-DEM-198]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental (two-way fixed effects). GAO's 2010 review of Maine and Arizona could not attribute the one significant change it found (a narrower winning margin) to the programs, saw no change in voter choice, and found no sign that perceived interest-group influence fell [EV-DEM-201]. *Strength:* program evaluation, before and after, no comparison states.
- **Polarization: contested.**
  - Andrew Hall finds, from staggered adoption in five states, that public funding weakened incumbents' advantages but increased legislative polarization, by cutting moderate, access-seeking PAC money [EV-DEM-195]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental, chamber level.
  - Masket and Miller find fully funded legislators in Arizona and Maine no more extreme than privately funded ones [EV-DEM-196]. *Strength:* observational, individual level.
  - The Brennan Center argues small donors are not more polarized than large ones, and that the New York City and Los Angeles matching programs have not worsened polarization [EV-DEM-197] (an advocate's review, about matching rather than full grants).
  - Neither side finds that public funding reduces polarization.

### B.3 The presidential system

Each major-party nominee received $21.8 million in 1976. 2008 ($84.1 million) was the last year a major-party nominee accepted the general-election grant; it stands at $123.5 million for 2024 [EV-DEM-202]. Taxpayer checkoff designations fell from 28.7% of filers in 1980 to 5.4% in 2015 [EV-DEM-203]. CRS reports a general consensus, even among supporters, that the spending limits are too low to attract competitive candidates against super PAC money [EV-DEM-204]. *Strength:* official data and nonpartisan analysis. The lesson for any new program is a limit that keeps pace with outside money.

### B.4 Disclosure, the courts and the regulator

- **What the courts allow.**
  - *Citizens United* upheld disclaimer and disclosure requirements as "the less restrictive alternative"; only Justice Thomas dissented from that part [EV-DEM-205].
  - *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* (2021) struck California's bulk donor-list filing rule and held that exacting scrutiny requires narrow tailoring [EV-DEM-206].
  - Arizona's Proposition 211, which requires original-source disclosure above set thresholds, survived a facial challenge in the state supreme court in June 2026. An as-applied claim goes on [EV-DEM-207].
  - These bear on the legacy INT-2 (the DISCLOSE Act), which rests on the 8-1 disclosure holding (`domain-integrity.md`, line 202). A rule written to *Bonta*'s tailoring test is the safer form.
- **A four-vote, six-member regulator deadlocks.**
  - CRS counted deadlocks on about 13% of closed enforcement matters in 2008-09 and 24.4% in 2014 [EV-DEM-209]. Counts for 2016 run from 14% to 30%, depending on who counts and which votes [EV-DEM-211].
  - Two views: CRS says deadlocks may or may not show dysfunction, since Congress may have intended them, but in some cases they stop enforcement [EV-DEM-210]. A former Republican chairman says they are the exception, and beneficial [EV-DEM-211].
  - Section 1 records the 2025-26 loss of a quorum [EV-DEM-031]. This is the evidence behind the legacy INT-7 (FEC restructuring).

### B.5 Abroad

- Close to 70% of 181 countries fund parties directly; 38% cap party spending and 49% candidate spending; only 29% regulate third-party spending. Canada caps registered third parties at CAD 602,700 a year [EV-DEM-212]. *Strength:* international practice (International IDEA's database of laws).
- International IDEA finds the gap between rules on paper and their enforcement common, spending limits evaded through third parties, and weak enforcement bodies a main obstacle [EV-DEM-213].
- The United Kingdom capped party spending at £54,010 per constituency in 2024, after an 80% rise in the limits. Non-party campaigners' share of election spending rose from 3.3% (2015) to 8.5% (2019) [EV-DEM-214]. *Strength:* data reported by a UK think tank; the Electoral Commission's own report is still to be read.

## Part C. Ethics and integrity

### C.1 Members' stock trading: contested

| Study | Period | Finding | Strength | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ziobrowski and others | 1993-1998 | Portfolios mimicking Senators' purchases beat the market by 85 basis points a month | Event study (synthetic portfolios), peer-reviewed | [EV-DEM-215] |
| Eggers and Hainmueller | 1985-2001, 2004-2008 | No information advantage; the average member would have done better in an index fund | Actual holdings, peer-reviewed | [EV-DEM-216] |
| Belmont and others | 2012-2020 | No superior performance; House purchases underperformed by 26 basis points over six months | All reported trades, peer-reviewed | [EV-DEM-217] |
| Chen and Sacerdote | 2012-2023 | Underperform or match benchmarks; timing follows public sentiment | Working paper (shares an author with the row above) | [EV-DEM-218] |
| Wei and Zhou | Before and after reaching leadership | Members who rise to leadership outperform matched peers by 47 points a year afterward | Working paper; a small group | [EV-DEM-219] |

- **How to read it.** The average-member finding and the leadership finding can both be true: they differ in method and subgroup. A ban's case does not rest on average returns alone. The legacy INT-1 frames it as legibility and public trust (`domain-integrity.md`, lines 46 and 198), which is judgment, not something this evidence measures.
- **Compliance.** By January 2023 news organizations had found 78 members who failed to report trades properly; the usual penalty is $200 or waived [EV-DEM-220]. *Strength:* journalists' count; no official record exists. The House-passed bill in section 1 [EV-DEM-034] is a floor set against that record.

### C.2 The revolving door

Lobbyists who worked for a Senator lose 24% of their revenue when that Senator leaves office, more for senior and powerful Senators [EV-DEM-221]. Lobbyists follow the politicians they are connected to across committees, and connections earn a steadier premium than expertise [EV-DEM-222]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental, peer-reviewed (both in the *American Economic Review*, so the checker sees one publisher). This is the evidence that access, not knowledge, is what is sold, which is the premise of the legacy INT-3 (`domain-integrity.md`, line 68).

### C.3 The watchdogs

- **Inspectors general.** In FY2025 about 13,800 inspector-general staff reported potential savings of about $65.6 billion, which CIGIE presents as about $17 for every dollar of its budget [EV-DEM-223]. *Strength:* self-reported official data, under the second Trump administration. Brookings computed a mean of 13.41 and a median of 6.38 dollars per dollar for 2010-2014, and noted that audit returns count questioned costs and funds put to better use, not only money recovered [EV-DEM-224]. The ratio is real but overstates realized savings.
- **The Office of Special Counsel** received a record 9,820 cases in FY2025 on a $31,585,000 budget and about 123 staff [EV-DEM-225]. **The Office of Government Ethics** recorded 11 new criminal conflict-of-interest prosecutions in 2024 [EV-DEM-226]. *Strength:* official data. Neither office's figures measure deterrence.
- **State ethics commissions** show no strong or consistent effect on corruption, and tend to be adopted after scandals [EV-DEM-227]. *Strength:* observational panel; its outcome is federal corruption convictions. Abroad, there is little evidence on whether anti-corruption agencies work [EV-DEM-228] (an expert review).
- **Judges.** Per the *Wall Street Journal*, 131 federal judges sat in 685 matters (2010-2018) involving companies in which they or their families held stock, contrary to 28 U.S.C. 455. The Chief Justice called for more rigorous training and better conflict-checking software [EV-DEM-229]. *Strength:* the Chief Justice's own acknowledgment of a newspaper count. These rows bear on the legacy CRT-2 (judicial ethics and recusal).

## Part D. Courts

### D.1 Terms for the Supreme Court

- **The record elsewhere.**
  - Thirty-one states and D.C. set a mandatory retirement age for high-court justices, most at 70. Almost all states give them terms of six to fifteen years. Rhode Island alone has neither [EV-DEM-230].
  - The United States is the only major constitutional democracy with neither a retirement age nor a fixed term for its high court. At least 27 democracies limit constitutional-court terms to between five and fifteen years; Germany's judges serve twelve years, with no renewal [EV-DEM-231].
  - *Strength:* official compilation by the Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court (2021, Biden era) and international practice.
- **Both sides.** The commission set out both cases. For: membership would track elections, and appointments would be predictable. Against: weaker independence, a confirmation fight every two years, unstable doctrine, and state and foreign courts too different to compare [EV-DEM-232]. No study measures the effects in the United States. Public support for fixed terms is 79% [EV-DEM-063].
- **Statute or amendment: contested.**
  - The commission was divided on whether Congress can create the equivalent of term limits by statute [EV-DEM-233].
  - CRS reads most commentators as requiring an amendment, though Congress can change the Court's size by ordinary law [EV-DEM-234].
  - The Brennan Center argues that 18-year "senior Justice" statutes are constitutional, because they change duties on a schedule rather than remove Justices [EV-DEM-235].
  - No court has ruled. The legacy CRT-15 is the statutory form (18 years, prospective, a severable retroactive part, no deemed consent; `legacy/domains/domain-courts.md`, line 180), with AMD-2 as the amendment track. The evidence qualifies it: its legal footing is the contested point, not a settled one.
- **Court size.** Congress has set the Court at six (1789), five on paper (1801, repealed before a vacancy), ten (Civil War), seven (1866) and nine (1869) [EV-DEM-236]. CRS's history supports the legacy file's point that Congress's authority over the Court's size is clear, and that the risk is political (`domain-courts.md`, line 199).
- **Ethics enforcement.** The commission found that a binding disciplinary framework for the Justices would raise difficulties, but that even an advisory code would be a positive step [EV-DEM-248]. That code now exists, without enforcement [EV-DEM-040]. No study compares compliance under enforced and unenforced codes.

### D.2 Judges and delay

- The median time from filing to trial in federal civil cases was 33.7 months in the year to June 2026, against 28.3 in the year to June 2021 [EV-DEM-237]. *Strength:* official data; context, not an effect.
- **Whether too few judges causes delay is contested.**
  - The Brennan Center finds that a 12-month vacancy goes with a 2-point rise in the share of cases pending three years or more [EV-DEM-238]. *Strength:* observational, advocate.
  - Yang, using judges' deaths and pension eligibility as instruments, finds no significant increase in criminal case delay. Instead prosecutors decline more cases and offer better pleas [EV-DEM-239]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental.
- No study of newly created judgeships was found. The Judicial Conference's request (71 seats [EV-DEM-041]) rests on caseload standards, not a measured effect.

### D.3 Counsel and civil legal aid

- **The gap.** Low-income Americans get no or not enough legal help for 92% of their substantial civil legal problems [EV-DEM-240]. *Strength:* national survey commissioned by the Legal Services Corporation, which has a stake in the gap's size.
- **New York City's right to counsel in eviction cases.**
  - Cassidy and Currie, using the ZIP-code rollout, estimate that the program raised representation 12.4 points. Representation cut possessory judgments by 32.1 points and warrants by 32.3 points, which the authors call upper bounds [EV-DEM-242]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental, independent of the city.
  - The city reports that 84% of represented households with resolved cases stayed in their homes in FY2023 [EV-DEM-241]. *Strength:* program data with no comparison group; not an effect size.
  - **Capacity lagged the right.** The monthly representation rate stayed under 50% for three years and fell to 30% in March 2025, as funding and provider staffing failed to keep up [EV-DEM-243].
- The legacy courts file holds no civil-counsel item; its counsel provisions are inside CRT-5 (`domain-courts.md`, line 102). This evidence matters there too: a right granted ahead of the lawyers to fill it does not deliver.

### D.4 Article I courts and remedies

- **The Tax Court model.** The Tax Court began in 1924 as an executive-branch board and became an Article I court only in 1969; it has 19 judges on 15-year terms [EV-DEM-244]. Article I courts lack life tenure and salary protection; Congress creates them for expertise, efficiency and control [EV-DEM-245]. *Strength:* CRS history. No outcome evaluation of such a conversion was found. The legacy CRT-5 uses the Tax Court as its model (`domain-courts.md`, line 102), as does the House bill [EV-IMM-113].
- **After *CASA*.** One year on, CRS finds no systemic shift: plaintiffs keep winning nationwide-class injunctions and APA vacatur, and Justice Alito warned against loose class certification [EV-DEM-246]. That leaves the legacy CRT-9's question (what Congress should settle) open rather than urgent.
- **Security.** The judiciary asked for $892.0 million for court security in FY2026, up 18.9% [EV-DEM-247]. No evaluation links spending to outcomes.

## Part E. Participation and civic life

- **Participatory budgeting.**
  - In Brazil, participatory budgeting is associated with more health spending, more civil-society organizations and lower infant mortality, more strongly the longer programs ran [EV-DEM-172]. Independently, municipalities using it in 1990-2004 spent more on sanitation and health, with lower infant mortality [EV-DEM-173]. *Strength:* observational panels, peer-reviewed. Adoption was not random.
  - In New York City, taking part raised participants' probability of voting by 8.4 points against matched voters, more for groups that vote less [EV-DEM-174]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental (matching); people chose to take part.
- **Citizens' assemblies.**
  - Ireland's assembly of 99 randomly chosen citizens recommended replacing the abortion provision [EV-DEM-175]. The referendum that followed passed 1,429,981 to 723,632 [EV-DEM-176]. A reform group credits the assembly with breaking the deadlock [EV-DEM-177]; that causal role is judgment.
  - France's climate convention made 149 proposals. One count finds 18 adopted as written and 26 not at all in the May 2021 bill, and members gave the government 3.3 out of 10 [EV-DEM-178]. Another finds 10% adopted unmodified, 37% modified and 53% rejected [EV-DEM-179].
  - The OECD collected 289 deliberative processes from 1986 to 2019 [EV-DEM-180]. Where data exist (55 cases), authorities implemented over half the recommendations in 76% of them and none in 11% [EV-DEM-181]. *Strength:* international practice; the sample with data is selective.
  - These are the evidence for the legacy DEM-18. What decides their worth is whether a body with power has committed to answer the recommendations.
- **Local news.** Following the same people over time, declines in local coverage of House races were followed by lower political knowledge and participation [EV-DEM-182]. *Strength:* panel, peer-reviewed; modest effects. In 2025, 213 counties had no local news source and 1,524 more had one; about 50 million people have limited or no access [EV-DEM-183]. No evaluation of a policy that restores local news was found.
- **Civics education.** State civics exams raise civic knowledge most for young people with less exposure at home, notably Latinos and immigrants [EV-DEM-184]. The civics-test graduation requirement adopted by 18 states did not raise youth turnout (at most 1.5 points, not significant) [EV-DEM-185]. *Strength:* cross-state comparison and quasi-experimental. This bears on the legacy DEM-16: knowledge and turnout are different targets.
- **Trust through service.** Medicaid clients renewed automatically were more likely to trust the agency (75%) than those who went through the standard renewal (66%). Assignment was not random [EV-DEM-186]. *Strength:* observational, by an interested nonprofit. It is the one entry in the register linking delivery to trust; section 1's national trust figure is 17% [EV-DEM-065].

## Part F. Long-run institutional reform

### F.1 The size and shape of the House

- **House size: the yardstick is contested.** Reform authors from across the spectrum note that lower chambers in many countries roughly follow the cube root of population. For the 2020 count that rule gives a House of 692 [EV-DEM-249]. Margaritondo finds that the rule's fit was forced: the data fit an exponent near 0.45, closer to a square root, and no power law is precise enough for policy [EV-DEM-250]. *Strength:* comparative analysis and a peer-reviewed reanalysis. Both agree the House is small for its population; they disagree on how much. The legacy DEM-3's "~692 members on the cube-root rule" (`domain-democracy.md`, line 24) takes the first yardstick, which the second qualifies.
- **Ranked-choice voting.**
  - In four local elections with more than 600,000 ballots, ballot exhaustion left the winner short of a majority of all votes cast in every case [EV-DEM-251]. Across 182 U.S. ranked-choice elections, the theoretical flaws rarely occur, except exhaustion [EV-DEM-252]. *Strength:* ballot-level analysis, peer-reviewed and secondary.
  - Survey studies find voters in ranked-choice cities rate campaigns as less negative (one study disagrees). A matched study finds no difference in overall turnout but higher youth turnout [EV-DEM-253].
  - Alaska's 2024 repeal failed by 737 votes [EV-DEM-254]. Nineteen states had banned the method by March 2026, 17 of them under Republican trifectas; Alaska, Hawaii and Maine use it statewide [EV-DEM-255].
- **Proportional representation.**
  - New Zealand chose mixed-member proportional representation 53.9% to 46.1% in 1993. At the first such election in 1996, women rose from 21% to 29% of the House and Māori members from 7% to 13% [EV-DEM-257]. Voters kept it in 2011, 57.77% to 42.23% [EV-DEM-256]. *Strength:* international practice; before and after.
  - The Fair Representation Act (117th Congress) would use ranked-choice voting in three- to five-member districts for states with six or more seats and require independent commissions [EV-DEM-258]. It was not enacted. This is the legacy DEM-4's base (`domain-democracy.md`, line 25), which the legacy package ties to DEM-3 and DEM-9 (line 16).

### F.2 Redistricting commissions: contested

DEM-2's structure is a design choice for this dossier's memo set (`dossiers/scenario/memo-defense-baseline-and-dem2.md`, choice b). These rows are the evidence it will weigh; nothing here chooses.

| Study | Finding | Strength | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kogan and McGhee, California 2011 | Every commission map more competitive than the 2001 legislative maps, by 3 points (Assembly) to 13 points (Congress); only the congressional gain statistically significant | Before-after, peer-reviewed | [EV-DEM-259] |
| UCLA and Yale authors, 2010 cycle | Commissions drew maps no more competitive than legislatures, against simulated alternatives | Simulation, as reported by a magazine (Reason); the study itself is still to be read | [EV-DEM-260] |
| Wang and Sippy, 2021 cycle | Commissions with final authority, balanced membership and binding review produced lower bias and more competition; advisory or one-party commissions were often overridden | Comparative review of all fifty states, reform advocate | [EV-DEM-261] |

- **The law.** In *Arizona State Legislature v. AIRC* (2015), the Court held that a state's voters may give congressional redistricting to an independent commission [EV-DEM-262]. Two of the four dissenters (Justices Thomas and Alito) and the Chief Justice, who wrote the principal dissent, still sit (context of EV-DEM-262). That confirms the scenario memo's "memory, verify" note (`memo-defense-baseline-and-dem2.md`, line 56). The holding covers a state's own choice; it does not decide whether Congress may mandate commissions, the point that memo flags as the highest-risk part of DEM-2.
- **What the evidence shows.** Design matters more than the label: final authority and balance are what the 2021 review finds separate commissions that changed maps from those that did not [EV-DEM-261]. The size of the competitiveness gain is contested [EV-DEM-259] [EV-DEM-260].

### F.3 Senate rules, the Electoral College, D.C. and Article V

- **The Senate.** In 2013 the Senate set a precedent, by overturning a ruling of the chair, that lets a simple majority invoke cloture on all nominations except to the Supreme Court [EV-DEM-264]. In 2017 it extended that to the Supreme Court by the same method, which CRS records as the "nuclear option" [EV-DEM-265]. *Strength:* official procedure (CRS). These are the working precedents behind D2's options (section 1, F.1); this section does not reopen D2.
- **The National Popular Vote compact.** CRS finds it unresolved whether the compact needs congressional consent: supporters cite the states' plenary power over electors, opponents the Constitution's structure [EV-DEM-266]. The report predates *Chiafalo* (2020) (Evidence still needed).
- **D.C.** The 2020 census counted 689545 residents in the District, more than Vermont (643077) or Wyoming (576851) [EV-DEM-267]. The House passed H.R. 51 by 216 to 208 on party lines in April 2021 [EV-DEM-268]. The view that statehood needs an amendment, given the Twenty-third Amendment, is not yet in the register (Evidence still needed).
- **Article V.** CRS records opposition to a convention from across the spectrum, including Heritage, Eagle Forum and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, all fearing a convention whose scope cannot be held [EV-DEM-269]. The application counts are in section 1 [EV-DEM-077] [EV-DEM-078] [EV-DEM-079].
- **Legislative term limits.** In state comparisons, term limits reduce whatever more professional legislatures produce, though committee behavior and bill-passage rates held steady [EV-DEM-263]. *Strength:* comparative, peer-reviewed (2004); one source so far. It is a caution for any capacity reform that shortens tenure.

## Part G. A government that delivers

Section 1 measured the losses (G.1, G.2) and gathered what the flagships ask of the state (G.6). This part asks what the evidence says builds and keeps capacity, what delivery methods work, and what remedies catch failures. **It sizes nothing**; the capacity lines and agency shares go to a later stage F step. Take-up and delivery evidence already in other registers is cited, not repeated: `dossiers/secure-floor/2-what-works.md`, part B.1 [EV-SEC-101] [EV-SEC-103] [EV-SEC-105].

### G.1 Capacity as an investment: the IRS

| Estimate | Finding | Strength; publisher | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CBO, 2020 | A cumulative $40 billion more for enforcement over 2020-2030 raises about $103 billion | Simulation, nonpartisan agency (via CRS) | [EV-DEM-270] |
| CBO, 2024 | A $20 billion cut to IRA enforcement money lowers revenue by $44 billion over FY2024-34 | Simulation (via CRS) | [EV-DEM-271] |
| CBO, 2023 | The $1.4 billion rescission lowers revenue by $2.3 billion through 2033 | Simulation (via CRS) | [EV-DEM-272] |
| Boning, Hendren, Sprung-Keyser and Stuart | $1 of audit spending raises $2.17 at first; audits of the top 0.1% return 6.3:1; above the 90th percentile, more than $12 per $1 with deterrence | Administrative data with random audits, peer-reviewed; IRS co-authors | [EV-DEM-273] |
| Tax Foundation | CBO's $5 to $9 per dollar holds at current funding; returns fall as funding grows; CBO leaves deterrence out | Secondary analysis, market-oriented think tank | [EV-DEM-275] |
| Foundation for Government Accountability | Contests CBO: $1.3 billion collected against $7.2 billion projected by FY2024 | Advocacy testimony | [EV-DEM-274] |

- **How to read it.** The nonpartisan and academic estimates agree that enforcement returns more than it costs, with falling returns at the margin. The contesting testimony compares one announced initiative with CBO's whole-package path, before the lag CBO assumes has run (context of EV-DEM-274). It is the other side's view, given here, not a measurement. Section 1's Yale and CBO projections of the 2025 cuts' cost are in the same range [EV-DEM-093] [EV-DEM-094].
- **The legacy check.** The legacy IRS review used CBO's 2020 ratios (3.05 at $20 billion, 2.58 at $40 billion) with falling returns (`legacy/sources/capacity-recovery-budget-review.md`, line 118). CRS's restatement confirms the $40 billion point [EV-DEM-270]. The review also notes that CBO's scorekeeping excludes enforcement revenue from official scores (line 36), so this return never appears in a bill's score.
- **Rebuilding takes years, even with money.** In the first half of FY2023, with IRA funding available, the IRS lost more revenue agents than it hired: a net 82 in the large-business division and 186 in the small-business division, after a Treasury-ordered hiring pause [EV-DEM-277]. In the 2023 filing season the IRS met its 85% phone service goal, though employees answered only 35% of all calls received [EV-DEM-276]. *Strength:* official data (TIGTA, the Taxpayer Advocate). What the rebuild buys first is service; enforcement follows as agents are trained.

### G.2 Digital delivery

- **Direct File.**
  - In 2025 the IRS estimated 32 million taxpayers eligible; 751,000 registered (78% more than in 2024), and 59% of users did not file through it [EV-DEM-278].
  - TIGTA put its FY2025 cost at $16.2 million as of May 2025, against the IRS's $61.2 million estimate. The figure leaves out support functions and about $2.5 million of identity-verification costs [EV-DEM-279].
  - Treasury suspended it citing high costs and low participation (the second Trump administration's view, recorded in the context of EV-DEM-279); the IRS told states it would not run in 2026 [EV-SEC-063]. The independent audit puts actual cost at about a quarter of the estimate. The pilot's satisfaction results are in the secure floor register [EV-SEC-112].
- **The 2013 HealthCare.gov launch** failed without performance testing, and CMS recovered the site [EV-CAR-311] [EV-CAR-312]. The register holds no evaluation of the U.S. Digital Service's later results (Evidence still needed).
- **The UK Government Digital Service: mixed.** The National Audit Office found that it had successfully reshaped government's approach to technology. GDS estimated its spending controls saved £1.3 billion over five years, a figure the NAO reports without verifying [EV-DEM-280]. Its Verify identity platform reached 3.6 million users against a 25 million target, and expected benefits were cut by 75% [EV-DEM-281]. *Strength:* program evaluation by a national audit office.
- **Estonia's X-Road.** Queries rose from half a million at its 2003 launch to 340 million. Estonian law bars agencies from asking for data another registry already holds. The World Bank's estimate of 7 million work days saved a year rests on an assumed 30 minutes per interaction [EV-DEM-282]. The system now reports 2.2 billion transactions a year [EV-DEM-283] (its operator's figure). *Strength:* international practice; the savings figure is illustrative, not measured.
- **State benefit applications.** In a randomized trial with over 60,000 Los Angeles SNAP applicants, on-demand interviews nearly doubled early approvals and raised approval rates by 6 points. Over a third of applications in the county were denied for a missed interview [EV-DEM-318]. *Strength:* randomized trial (Code for America co-authors). It agrees with the secure floor's SNAP evidence [EV-SEC-103] [EV-SEC-105].
- **Medicaid automatic renewal.** By October 2023, 55% of people keeping Medicaid were renewed from records (ex parte), from 99% in North Carolina to 3% in Wyoming. Twenty-six states were out of compliance with the ex parte rules in March 2023, and household-level errors in 30 states wrongly cut off about 500,000 people [EV-DEM-319]. *Strength:* official data compiled by KFF. The end-of-unwinding totals are in care's register [EV-CAR-210] [EV-CAR-211]. Automatic renewal works where state systems and data access allow it, and those vary widely: from 99% of renewals to 3%.

### G.3 Payment systems

- **Data access, not a portal, is the bottleneck.** Agencies attributed about 73% of FY2021-24 overpayments ($556.6 billion of $765.8 billion) to failure to access existing data. The Treasury's Do Not Pay system itself prevented $45.8 million in FY2023, within $652.7 million the Fiscal Service reported [EV-DEM-291]. CRS finds that a Do Not Pay match neither stops a payment nor proves ineligibility, that not all agencies use it, and that its effect is hard to measure [EV-DEM-292]. *Strength:* official data and nonpartisan analysis. Section 1's $186 billion improper-payment total is the same agencies' method [EV-DEM-113].
- **The 2020-21 payments.** The IRS paid the first round within 14 days to people it had records for [EV-CLP-056]. Nearly 3 million people eligible for $4.7 billion of 2021 rebate credits had not claimed them, and 6.1 million had filed no 2020 or 2021 return [EV-DEM-293]. *Strength:* official data (TIGTA). Paying from records reaches most people fast; non-filers are the gap, as the secure floor found for the 2021 child credit [EV-SEC-110].
- **Instant payment rails.**
  - FedNow settled 8,413,402 payments in 2025 and 6,376,467 in the third quarter of 2026 alone [EV-DEM-290]. *Strength:* official data; fast growth from a low base, against the Treasury's 1.3 billion payments a year [EV-DEM-116].
  - Brazil's Pix reached 67% of adults within a little over a year. Its authors credit mandatory participation by large banks and the central bank's role as both operator and rule-setter [EV-DEM-288]. Fraud has shifted toward schemes combining manipulation with technical exploitation [EV-DEM-289]. No estimate of government savings from Pix was found.
- **India's direct benefit transfers: contested.**
  - The World Bank credits Aadhaar-linked payment of the cooking-gas subsidy with saving about US$1 billion a year [EV-DEM-284]. The government reports ₹3.48 lakh crore saved over 2009-24, an estimate by a foundation close to the governing party [EV-DEM-285].
  - Against: in Jharkhand, stricter biometric checks reduced corruption, but 1.5 to 2 million legitimate beneficiaries lost access at some point [EV-DEM-286] (randomized and natural experiments).
  - For: in Andhra Pradesh, a randomized smartcard rollout covering 19 million people made payments faster and less corrupt without reducing access [EV-DEM-287].
  - Both studies come from one research team, so they are not independent of each other. Read together, they say how the change is phased in decides whether a verification step saves money or excludes people.
- **Unemployment insurance under strain.**
  - In the pandemic, states took on average 50, 38 and 25 days from the CARES Act to the first payment of its three new programs, and 9 of 12 sampled states missed the 87% standard [EV-DEM-294]. *Strength:* official data (Labor's inspector general).
  - States that had modernized their systems started programs 15 and 8 days faster [EV-DEM-295] (a correlation the inspector general reports). States running COBOL systems saw at least 2.1 points more claims delayed over 70 days [EV-DEM-296] (quasi-experimental working paper, Atlanta Fed authors).
  - ARPA's $219 million in equity grants did not demonstrate improved access, and ARPA's $2 billion for UI was cut to $1 billion [EV-DEM-297]. Results of the larger modernization grants were not found.
  - Section 1's pandemic fraud estimates [EV-DEM-125] [EV-DEM-126] are the other cost of systems that could not verify or pay at scale.

### G.4 Remedies when delivery fails

- **Inside the agency.** The Taxpayer Advocate Service gave full or partial relief in 78.2% of its closed cases in FY2023 [EV-DEM-298]. Its average case took 110 days in FY2024, against 85 in FY2020 [EV-DEM-299]. *Strength:* official data; no outcome evaluation. A remedy office slows with its parent agency.
- **An ombudsman.** The UK Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman reports that its recommendations are refused only in extremely rare cases, and that 72 of 75 mediations fully resolved the complaint [EV-DEM-300]. *Strength:* self-reported; no independent study found.
- **How overpayments are recovered.** SSA cut its default withholding rate for overpayments from 100% of the monthly benefit to 10% from April 2024. Overpayments were about 0.5% of FY2022 benefits [EV-DEM-301]. In 2025, weeks after announcing a 100% default, SSA set 50% for retirement and disability overpayments, keeping 10% for SSI. Its own projection was that 100% would save about $7 billion over a decade [EV-DEM-302]. *Strength:* official data (CRS) and a news report of SSA's notice; the 2025 rationale is the second Trump administration's. Three defaults in a year is itself a finding: the remedy rests on an administrative setting, not on law.
- **The constitutional floor is thin.** Welfare cannot be ended without a prior evidentiary hearing (*Goldberg v. Kelly*) [EV-DEM-306]. Disability benefits can be, under a three-factor balancing test (*Mathews v. Eldridge*) [EV-DEM-307]. Outside need-based aid, statute and agency practice set most protections. This is the gap the flagships' remedies sections must fill, and shared rule 5 (who answers for mistakes) is this foundation's to specify (section 1, G.6).

### G.5 The civil service

- **Merit hiring goes with less corruption and better results.**
  - Across fifty-two countries, meritocratic recruitment predicts less corruption. Competitive salaries, career stability and internal promotion show no significant effect [EV-DEM-308]. *Strength:* observational, cross-national.
  - In Brazil, when a new party won a close mayoral race it replaced municipal staff, and test scores in municipal schools fell 0.05 to 0.08 standard deviations [EV-DEM-309]. *Strength:* regression discontinuity.
  - In the British colonial service, governors appointed through patronage raised less revenue and invested less, until patronage was abolished [EV-DEM-310]. *Strength:* quasi-experimental, historical.
- **At-will employment in the states: contested.**
  - Georgia removed merit protections for state employees hired after July 1996. Employees surveyed three years later were pessimistic about the reform [EV-DEM-311]. Officials claimed efficiency gains while resignations rose every year and workers disputed the gains [EV-DEM-312].
  - Texas human-resources directors said at-will employment made staff more responsive to managers, but were mixed on its effects on whistle-blowing, fairness and performance [EV-DEM-313].
  - In U.S. federal records for 1997-2019, there are almost no political cycles in the career service. Contracts overseen by procurement officers politically misaligned with the administration had cost overruns 8% higher than the mean [EV-DEM-314]. Advocates of more political control cite costs like these; the same data show tenure protection did not politicize the service.
  - *Strength:* surveys (the state cases) and quasi-experimental (federal). No study measures the results of a Schedule F-type conversion. This bears on the legacy CAP-8 (prohibiting Schedule Policy/Career) and on CAP-9 and CAP-11.
- **Funding continuity.** The one automatic continuing resolution ever enacted, the 2013 Pay Our Military Act, ran 17 days. Opponents argue a permanent one would slow regular appropriations and freeze spending; CBO scored such proposals as new direct spending [EV-DEM-315]. *Strength:* CRS's account of both sides. In the states, 23% of budgets were late in 1988-2007, driven by divided government and economic shocks [EV-DEM-316]. No study measures the effect of states' automatic-appropriation laws. Section 1 records the 2025-26 gaps [EV-DEM-127]; the legacy EXE-18 (payment continuity) is homed in `agenda/executive-power`.

### G.6 State capacity

- SNAP's administrative cost per case ran from $89 (Florida) to $848 (Wyoming) in FY2016 and fell by more than half in real terms since FY1999. Adopting broad-based categorical eligibility or simplified reporting cut cost per case about 7%; 72% of the variation was unexplained state differences [EV-DEM-317]. *Strength:* observational, a study commissioned by USDA.
- Simpler rules cost less to run, which is the design lens's administrative half (`DEC-2026-11-09-13`). How the federal match rate changes a state's capacity was not found in any study. That matters now: the federal share of SNAP administration falls to 25% [EV-DEM-136].
- State unemployment systems' pandemic record is in G.3 [EV-DEM-294] [EV-DEM-295]. The legacy CAP-13 (state and local capacity grants) is still unsourced (section 1, "Inherited assumptions, traced"); this part adds evidence on why state systems matter, not on what a grant buys.

### G.7 What part G shows (observations, not choices)

1. **The return on capacity is measurable where the output is revenue** [EV-DEM-270] [EV-DEM-273], and mostly unmeasured elsewhere: no entry prices the return on SSA, CMS or HUD staff.
2. **Money does not buy staff quickly** [EV-DEM-277]. Hiring and training time, not appropriations, set the pace of a rebuild.
3. **Records-based delivery reaches most people, and misses the people outside the records** [EV-CLP-056] [EV-DEM-293] [EV-DEM-319].
4. **Verification at the front door cuts both ways**: it can cut fraud or exclude eligible people, and the rollout decides which [EV-DEM-286] [EV-DEM-287].
5. **Remedies that rest on administrative discretion move with each administration** [EV-DEM-301] [EV-DEM-302].
6. **Systems built ahead of demand, or launched untested, fail in public** [EV-DEM-281] [EV-CAR-311].

## Failures to avoid

- **Automated recovery with no human check, no hardship rule and no legal basis.**
  - Australia's Robodebt cost A$2.4 billion in repayments and compensation. Its Royal Commission called it "neither fair nor legal" [EV-DEM-303].
  - The Dutch childcare benefits inquiry found the rule of law breached by an "all or nothing" fraud approach with no hardship clause [EV-DEM-304]. The cabinet resigned in January 2021, with as many as 26,000 parents involved [EV-DEM-305].
- **Identity checks rolled out faster than access protections** [EV-DEM-286].
- **Platforms built ahead of demand, or launched untested** [EV-DEM-281] [EV-CAR-311].
- **Household-level automation errors**, which wrongly cut off about 500,000 people from Medicaid [EV-DEM-319].
- **Money without hiring capacity** [EV-DEM-277].
- **Grants without evidence of the problem or tracking of results** [EV-DEM-297].
- **A right outrunning the people who deliver it**, as with New York's counsel rate falling to 30% [EV-DEM-243].
- **A public-financing limit that falls behind outside money**: no major-party nominee has taken the grant since 2008 [EV-DEM-202] [EV-DEM-204].
- **Matching that rises with an opponent's spending**, which the Court struck down [EV-DEM-208].
- **Disclosure rules not narrowly tailored** [EV-DEM-206].
- **An even-numbered regulator with a four-vote rule** [EV-DEM-209] [EV-DEM-211].
- **Rules with no enforcement**: STOCK Act fines of $200 or waived [EV-DEM-220], ethics commissions with no measured effect [EV-DEM-227], and political-finance rules unenforced abroad [EV-DEM-213].
- **Reading registration gains as turnout gains** [EV-DEM-145] [EV-DEM-146], or "potential savings" as realized [EV-DEM-223] [EV-DEM-224].
- **Deliberation no one has committed to answer** [EV-DEM-179].
- **Commissions with no final authority** [EV-DEM-261].
- **Restoring rights without telling people** [EV-DEM-161].
- **A remedy that rests on an administrative default** [EV-DEM-301] [EV-DEM-302].
