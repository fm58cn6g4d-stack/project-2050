# Memo 1: The voting floor

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 1 of the democratic renewal foundation (`../3-design.md`). Status: **awaiting Espen's decision**; nothing here is adopted until Espen decides. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Costs marked "judgment" are working assumptions. Agenda rows: DEM-1 Title I, DEM-1E, DEM-11, DEM-12, DEM-13 (all L1). Answers section 2's questions 1 and 2.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **B, a records-first floor**: the Freedom to Vote floor (early voting, mail voting without an excuse, same-day registration, paper ballots with audits, certification as a duty), with registration done by the government from records it already holds, identity proven by any of a broad list of documents or a signed statement, no documentary proof of citizenship, and notice and a chance to fix a rejected mail ballot. Cost: about $2 billion a year of election administration money (the booked DEM-11 line, a working assumption with no outside source), plus a few hundred million dollars once for the records match (judgment). Evidence: quasi-experimental studies of registration and mail voting, official audits, two national registers abroad; the ID evidence is contested.

## The question

What must every state offer voters in federal elections, and who does the work of getting eligible people onto the rolls?

## Why it matters

- **About 28.0 million eligible citizens were not registered in 2024** (Census, 2025) [EV-DEM-002]; 73.6% were registered and 65.3% voted [EV-DEM-001]. Young people, people without a degree and Hispanic citizens are underrepresented among voters [EV-DEM-003] [EV-DEM-004] [EV-DEM-005].
- **States moved both ways:** 47 restrictive laws in 22 states and 61 expansive laws in 32 states since 2024 (Brennan Center tracker, 2026) [EV-DEM-012].
- **The House passed documentary proof of citizenship and photo ID** (the SAVE America Act, 218-213, February 2026) [EV-DEM-015]; the Senate fell short, 48-50 [EV-DEM-016]. Whatever the floor says about ID and citizenship is the fight the bill will have.
- **Ballots are lost after they are cast:** 584,463 mail ballots were rejected in 2024 (EAC survey) [EV-DEM-018].

## What the evidence says, in six lines

1. **Registration rules move registration far more than turnout.** Automatic registration raised registrations 9 to 94 percent by state (Brennan Center, matching design) [EV-DEM-145]; its turnout effect is about 1 point or not significant (observational and difference-in-differences studies) [EV-DEM-146] [EV-DEM-147].
2. **Same-day registration and preregistration raise young people's turnout**, by 3.2 to 7.3 points (a 2022 dissertation, "Closing the Age Gap in Voter Turnout", quasi-experimental; the published journal version is still to be read) [EV-DEM-148] and about 8 points among Florida preregistrants (Holbein and Hillygus, regression discontinuity) [EV-DEM-149].
3. **Mail voting raises turnout about 2 points with no partisan effect** (Thompson and others, 2020; Barber and Holbein, 2020; both peer-reviewed) [EV-DEM-150] [EV-DEM-151].
4. **Strict ID is contested:** no turnout effect in a national panel (Cantoni and Pons) [EV-DEM-154]; 1.9 to 3.2 points lower turnout in Kansas and Tennessee (GAO, 2014) [EV-DEM-155]; Heritage disputes GAO's comparison [EV-DEM-156].
5. **Noncitizen voting is very rare where it is checked:** 20 noncitizens among 8.2 million Georgia registrants (Georgia's 2024 audit) [EV-DEM-163]; in Utah's 2026 review, records that could not be confirmed outnumbered confirmed noncitizens about 185 to 1 [EV-DEM-164]. A documentary rule falls on the unconfirmed.
6. **Where the state enrolls people from its records, coverage is near complete:** 97.8% in Australia (Australian Electoral Commission, 2026) [EV-DEM-168] [EV-DEM-169] and 96.4% in Canada (Elections Canada) [EV-DEM-170].

## The options

**A. The Freedom to Vote floor as the legacy file writes it** (legacy DEM-1 Title I with DEM-1E; `legacy/domains/domain-democracy.md`, line 75): early voting, mail voting without an excuse, same-day registration, automatic registration at every benefit-delivery point the plan creates (DEM-1E), list maintenance without partisan purges, paper ballots and risk-limiting audits, certification as a ministerial act. The legacy file says nothing on voter ID.
- *For:* a decade of drafting; DEM-1E reaches the plan's own beneficiaries.
- *Against:* registration at "delivery points" depends on contacts that automatic payments do not create (the legacy file's own caution, line 96); leaves the ID question to be fought on the floor.
- *Cost:* see the table below.

**B. A records-first floor (recommended).** A's floor, with three changes:
1. *Registration from records.* Every eligible citizen is registered from records the government already holds (state motor vehicle and benefit files, and with consent rules the plan's own IRS and SSA files), with a mailed notice and a way to decline, on the Australian model [EV-DEM-169]. DEM-1E's benefit points stay as one source among several, not the only one. Preregistration at 16 and 17.
2. *Identity without new papers.* A voter proves identity with any of a broad list of documents, or a signed statement under penalty of perjury; citizenship is checked against records, never by documents the voter must bring.
3. *Ballots that count.* Notice of a defect and a chance to cure it before the count, which in Pennsylvania made a flagged mail ballot 25 points more likely to count (University of Pennsylvania study, as reported) [EV-DEM-167].
- *For:* puts the work on the government, not the voter (the design lens, `DEC-2026-11-09-13`); the ID rule answers the burden question without the contested strict-ID evidence; it is the closest working model of near-complete rolls [EV-DEM-168].
- *Against:* a records match needs data-sharing law and state systems that vary widely (Medicaid's automatic renewal ran from 99% to 3% by state) [EV-DEM-319]; the signed-statement rule is the part opponents will attack.
- *Burden:* lower than today for voters; new duties for states, paid by DEM-11.

**C. A narrow floor of what raises turnout.** Same-day registration, early voting and mail voting only, with DEM-11's money; registration and ID left to the states.
- *For:* rests on the strongest turnout evidence [EV-DEM-148] [EV-DEM-150]; fewer mandates on counties.
- *Against:* leaves 28 million unregistered to the states that chose not to register them [EV-DEM-002]; leaves ID and proof-of-citizenship laws standing.

**D. Money and conditions only.** No federal mandate; DEM-11's grants carry conditions states may accept.
- *For:* least legal exposure; can pass faster.
- *Against:* the states with the largest gaps can decline; HAVA's security grants have run $15-55 million a year (EAC) [EV-DEM-017], a scale that has not bought a floor.

| Formula grants to election offices (DEM-11) | About $2 billion a year | Same | Same | Same, conditional |
| Records match and notices (one-time) | Inside DEM-1E (unpriced in the legacy file, line 94) | About $0.2-0.5 billion once (judgment) | — | — |
| Federal agency (EAC) | About $0.4 billion over FY2030-39 (legacy review) [EV-DEM-329] | Same | Same | Same |

- **DEM-11's size has no outside source.** The legacy "$1-4B a year" is "mine from the scale of HAVA appropriations" (`domain-democracy.md`, line 220). The budget model books $20 billion over FY2030-39 and $2.26 billion in FY2040, indexed to prices (`DEC-2026-11-12-08`, part 3: Espen decided the indexing, not the size). It stays a working assumption, range $1-4 billion a year, to be replaced by a county cost study (Evidence still needed).
- **Benchmarks.** CBO put the For the People Act of 2019's authorized spending at about $2.6 billion over 2019-2024, $2.4 billion of it estimated to be spent, with $750 million of election security grants (CBO, 2019) [EV-DEM-320]. CBO and JCT found the 2022 Freedom to Vote: John R. Lewis Act would cut the deficit by $131 million over 2022-2031, on $3.1 billion of new revenue (CBO, 2022) [EV-DEM-322]: its floor cost little in federal money. CBO did not score the Freedom to Vote Act on its own (none found). Section 2 records more than $1 billion of HAVA security grants since 2018 (EAC) [EV-DEM-171].

## What it means for people

- *A 19-year-old who moved for work:* under B she is registered from her new license record and can still register at the polls; under C she relies on same-day registration; under D it depends on her state.
- *A 70-year-old without a current photo ID:* under B he votes after signing a statement; under A and C his state's ID law decides; under the SAVE Act he would need a passport or birth certificate.
- *A county clerk:* under A and B, new duties paid from DEM-11; under D, money only if the state accepts the conditions.

## Recommendation: B

- It closes the largest measured gap, registration, with the method that works abroad [EV-DEM-168] [EV-DEM-170], and it does not read registration gains as turnout gains [EV-DEM-146].
- It answers ID and citizenship with records and a signed statement, which the audits support [EV-DEM-163] [EV-DEM-164] and which avoids resting the floor on the contested strict-ID evidence.
- *What it costs in burden:* nothing new for voters; for states, a records match and a cure process, paid by DEM-11.
- **What would change this:** a county cost study showing DEM-11 far short (then the line rises, not the floor); evidence that signed statements are abused at scale (then add a records check after the vote, not a document at the door).
