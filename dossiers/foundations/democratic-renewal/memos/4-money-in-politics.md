# Memo 4: Public financing and corporate political spending

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 4 of the democratic renewal foundation (`../3-design.md`). Status: **decided** (`DEC-2026-11-17-46`, `-47`, November 17, 2026): part 1 **B** and part 2 **A**, as recommended. The text below is the memo as sent; section 4 carries the decided design. Sources are named in plain text; bracketed tags point to `../evidence.json`. Agenda rows: INT-5 (5A to 5C), INT-15 (15A to 15D) (L2, except INT-5A to 5C at L1). Answers section 2's question 4. How the match is paid for is stage G's (the brief; `DEC-2026-11-10-05`): each option's cost is given, and INT-5B (general revenue) stands as the legacy working choice until stage G weighs it.

**Decision line.** Recommended: **part 1 B, a 6:1 match on in-state contributions up to $200 to House and Senate candidates who opt in, with no spending cap and with the program's limits indexed to past cycles' spending, and no trigger matching; part 2 A, the Exchange Act shareholder-authorization statute, written so its disclosure and board-vote sections stand alone if the shareholder vote falls.** Cost: no outside score of a federal match's full cost exists; the legacy estimate is about $0.01 trillion over FY2030-39 (range $0 to $0.03 trillion). Evidence: observational and quasi-experimental studies of city and state programs; contested findings on polarization; the presidential system's collapse; Supreme Court holdings.

## The question

Does the plan fund congressional candidates with public money, in what form, and does it regulate how public companies spend on politics?

## Why it matters

- **Outside money is the growth:** independent expenditures reported to the FEC were $4.4 billion in 2023-24 [EV-DEM-023]; dark money was $1.4 billion to more than $1.9 billion, by method (OpenSecrets; Brennan Center) [EV-DEM-026] [EV-DEM-025]. *NRSC v. FEC* (2026) ended the limits on party coordinated spending [EV-DEM-027].
- **A limit that falls behind collapses a program:** no major-party nominee has taken the presidential general-election grant since 2008 (FEC) [EV-DEM-202]; CRS reports a consensus that its spending limits are too low to compete with super PAC money [EV-DEM-204].

## What the evidence says, in five lines

1. **Matching broadens who gives:** New York City's matched races drew small donors from 89% of the city's block groups against 30% for unmatched races (Brennan Center with the Campaign Finance Institute) [EV-DEM-193]; New York State's first cycle took small in-district money plus match from under 5% to 45% of candidates' funding (Brennan Center, the program's advocate) [EV-DEM-194].
2. **Vouchers' effect is contested:** Seattle's voucher users were less wealthy than cash donors in 2017 (Heerwig and McCabe) [EV-DEM-191], but mostly from groups already overrepresented (Yorgason, American Political Science Review, 2024) [EV-DEM-190].
3. **No study finds public funding reduces polarization;** one finds full public grants increase it (Hall) [EV-DEM-195], another finds no effect (Masket and Miller) [EV-DEM-196].
4. **Trigger matching is unconstitutional** (*Arizona Free Enterprise*, 2011) [EV-DEM-208]; public financing as a voluntary subsidy is not.
5. **Disclosure rules must be narrowly tailored** (*Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta*, 2021) [EV-DEM-206]; *Citizens United* upheld disclaimer and disclosure 8-1 [EV-DEM-205].

## Part 1: public financing

**A. The legacy INT-5: a 6:1 match with voluntary spending caps, no trigger** (`legacy/domains/domain-integrity.md`, line 76).
- *For:* the most developed design; New York City's matched-race evidence [EV-DEM-193].
- *Against:* spending caps are the feature that sank the presidential system once outside money grew [EV-DEM-204].

**B. A 6:1 match with no spending cap, limits indexed (recommended).** Matches in-state contributions up to $200 for candidates who raise a qualifying number of small gifts and accept a lower contribution limit; the per-candidate payment ceiling is set from the previous two cycles' spending in comparable races and rises with it.
- *For:* keeps A's evidence base and removes the cap that makes candidates opt out; indexing answers the presidential lesson.
- *Against:* costs more than A as spending rises; critics will say public money joins an arms race.

**C. Vouchers on the Seattle model** (each voter gets vouchers to give).
- *For:* reaches people who cannot give at all.
- *Against:* the evidence on new donors is contested [EV-DEM-190]; administering a voucher for every registered voter is a larger build.

**D. No federal public financing;** disclosure and coordination rules only.
- *For:* no cost; the legacy file's own note that public financing "builds toward nothing" (line 88).
- *Against:* leaves candidates dependent on large donors as outside money grows.

## Part 2: corporate political spending (INT-15)

**A. Exchange Act statute on the S. 530 model (legacy INT-15A): shareholder authorization, board votes on spending over $50,000, reporting** (line 124), **with severable parts (recommended)**: if a court strikes the shareholder vote, disclosure and board votes stand.
**B. SEC rulemaking under existing authority** (15B): faster, reversible by a later SEC.
**C. State law only** (15C): reaches only companies chartered in states that act.
**D. Disclosure and board votes only** (15D): lowest legal risk, weakest constraint.

- *The open legal point.* The legacy file flags the First Amendment footing as unsettled and the union asymmetry as unanswered (lines 137-138). Writing the disclosure sections to *Bonta*'s tailoring test [EV-DEM-206] protects the part with the strongest footing.

## What it costs, against outside scores

| | Part 1 A | **Part 1 B** | Part 1 C | Part 1 D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FY2030-39 | About $0.01 trillion (legacy estimate, range $0-0.03 trillion; line 78) | Somewhat above A as limits index (judgment: up to the legacy high, $0.03 trillion) | Similar to B plus the voucher build (judgment) | $0 |

- **No outside score of a federal match's full cost exists.** CBO did not score the For the People Act of 2019's public financing (CBO, 2019) [EV-DEM-320]. In 2021 its partial score found $2.2 billion of spending and $3.2 billion of fines and tax revenue over ten years, with the Freedom From Influence Fund's spending starting only in FY2027 (as restated by the National Taxpayers Union Foundation) [EV-DEM-321]; in 2022, CBO and JCT found the Freedom to Vote: John R. Lewis Act cut the deficit by $131 million on $3.1 billion of revenue [EV-DEM-322]. Those bills paid the match from a surcharge on fines and settlements, which is why they score near zero; the legacy plan pays it from general revenue (INT-5B). The surcharge is a financing choice for stage G.
- New York City paid $4.6 million to 62 candidates in one pre-general round in 2025 (its Campaign Finance Board) [EV-DEM-033]: a city scale, not a federal benchmark.
- Part 2 costs SEC staffing only, inside the integrity units' capacity line (about $2.1 billion over FY2030-39 for all four units; legacy review) [EV-DEM-329].

## What it means for people

- *A teacher who gives $25 to a House candidate:* under A or B the candidate receives $175; under D, $25.
- *A first-time candidate without wealthy donors:* under B she can compete without a spending cap that would bind her if a super PAC enters.
- *A shareholder in a public company:* under part 2 A, the company needs the shareholders' approval before political spending.

## Recommendation: part 1 B, part 2 A

- B keeps the evidence for matching [EV-DEM-193] and fixes the failure that ended the presidential system [EV-DEM-202] [EV-DEM-204]; it does not claim to reduce polarization, which no study supports.
- Part 2 A reaches every public company, with the strongest part made to survive alone.
- **What would change this:** evidence that indexed limits drive costs far above the legacy high (then cap the index); a ruling against shareholder-authorization rules (then part 2 D stands by severability).
