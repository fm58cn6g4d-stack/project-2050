## The question

Under `DEC-2026-10-30-02` it is a working choice. Keep it, change it, or decide its parameters now?

## What the evidence says

- **House size:** the cube-root rule gives about 692 members for the 2020 count (a cross-spectrum reform group) [EV-DEM-249]; a peer-reviewed reanalysis finds the rule's fit forced, with an exponent nearer 0.45, and no power law precise enough for policy [EV-DEM-250]. The House has had 435 members since 1913 [EV-DEM-074]; districts average 761,169 people [EV-DEM-075].
- **Ranked-choice voting:** ballot exhaustion left winners short of a majority of all ballots in four large local elections [EV-DEM-251]; theoretical flaws rarely occur otherwise [EV-DEM-252]; nineteen states had banned it by March 2026 [EV-DEM-255].
- **Proportional representation:** New Zealand's 1996 switch raised women from 21% to 29% and Māori members from 7% to 13% of the House [EV-DEM-257]; voters kept it in 2011, 57.77% to 42.23% [EV-DEM-256].

## The options

**A. Keep the package as a direction; decide its parameters in 2031 (recommended).** House size (DEM-3A's cube-root rule, 3B's Wyoming rule or 3C's fixed increase), district magnitude (DEM-4A's five or 4B's three) and the fallback if DEM-9's amendment stalls (DEM-4C, a statutory presidential ranked-choice vote, or 4D, hold STV until ratified) go to a 2031 review, after the 2030 census and the Section 2 rerun the legacy file requires (line 158).
- *For:* the inputs that decide the parameters (the census, *Callais*'s effect on minority representation, the 2030 maps) do not exist yet; the package keeps STV from shipping with a plurality presidency (line 154).
- *Against:* the legacy file wants the fallback "chosen before 2032" (line 159); A meets that only if the 2031 review happens.

**B. Decide the parameters now** (the legacy chosen options: 692 seats, five-member districts; and pick 4C or 4D).
- *For:* the 2032 campaign has a fixed plank.
- *Against:* decides on a yardstick the evidence contests [EV-DEM-250] and before the Section 2 rerun.

**C. Unbundle: House enlargement alone, earlier.**
- *For:* enlargement is the most legible piece and needs no amendment.
- *Against:* breaks Espen's October 19 package; a larger House drawn in single-member districts is a different reform.

**D. Drop the package.** *Against:* abandons the plan's only answer to winner-take-all representation; no evidence argues for dropping it.

## The November 18 direction, as drafted (provisional)

Espen's direction (`DEC-2026-11-18-18`) sits inside the staged path: it adds seats for a new state without deciding the House's size, which stays with the 2031 review.

- **The provision** (DEM-5.01 for D.C.; DEM-6.01's admission act for Puerto Rico; DEM-3.01's review ends it). If a new state is admitted before a law enlarges or reapportions the House, it gets Representatives in addition to the House's size then in law, with no other state reapportioned: its population on the Census Bureau's latest estimate divided by the last apportionment's average district, rounded, at least one (working choice). On the July 2025 estimates, one seat for D.C. (693,645 people) and four for Puerto Rico (3,184,835), against the 2020 average of 761,169 [EV-DEM-408] [EV-DEM-075]: a House of 436, 439 or 440.
- **How long.** Until the first of the next decennial census's apportionment taking effect or a law that enlarges or reapportions the House.
- **The precedent.** The Alaska (1958) and Hawaii (1959) acts each gave the new state one Representative until the next reapportionment, in addition to the House as then prescribed, changing neither the permanent size nor the basis of apportionment [EV-DEM-405] [EV-DEM-406]; the House stood at 437 until the 1960 census's apportionment took effect in 1963 [EV-DEM-074] [EV-DEM-407]. The 1911 act did the same for Arizona and New Mexico [EV-DEM-407]. Those acts fixed one seat; this provision sizes the seats on an estimate, which is new.
- **Why** (Espen): it is how past admissions worked, and it gets the public used to a House of a different size, which helps the 2031 review.
- **Cost:** about $25-50 million a year for five seats, once an admission act passes (`../6-cost.md`, part 7); unbooked.
- **Legal risk (judgment): low to moderate.** Congress sets the House's size by statute and no existing state loses a seat; the open point is seats set on an estimate rather than the census, which a voter in an existing state could call unequal representation. The fallback is one seat until the next apportionment, as in 1959 (`../8-change-our-minds.md`, part 3).
