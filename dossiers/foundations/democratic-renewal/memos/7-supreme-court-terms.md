## The question

Should the plan limit Justices' active terms, by statute, by amendment or both, and in what order should the judiciary bills move?

## Why it matters

- **The United States is the only major constitutional democracy with neither a term nor an age limit for its high court** (Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court, 2021) [EV-DEM-231]; almost all states set terms or retirement ages [EV-DEM-230].
- **Public support for fixed terms is 79%** (Marquette, via SCOTUSblog) [EV-DEM-063]; approval of the Court was 34% in September 2026 (Gallup) [EV-DEM-062].
- **The legal footing is contested.** The commission was divided [EV-DEM-233]; CRS reads most commentators as requiring an amendment [EV-DEM-234]; the Brennan Center argues 18-year "senior Justice" statutes are constitutional because they change duties, not tenure [EV-DEM-235]. No court has ruled.

## Part 1: terms

**A. Prospective statute, severable retroactive part, plus an amendment (legacy CRT-15A; recommended).** 18 years of active service for Justices appointed after enactment; regular appointments in the first and third years of each presidential term; senior status after, with Article III pay and tenure kept (the 28 U.S.C. 371 analogy); actual Senate consent required; the amendment proposed at the same time (`legacy/domains/domain-courts.md`, lines 178-193).
- *For:* the statute's footing is the contested point, so the amendment track covers the risk; prospective application is the most defensible form.
- *Against:* the first effect lands about 18 years after enactment, in the 2040s, "not a response to the current Court" (line 182).

**B. Applied to sitting Justices (CRT-15B).** *For:* effect within a term. *Against:* the likeliest to be struck under "during good Behaviour" (line 191).

**C. Amendment only (CRT-15C).** *For:* clearest footing. *Against:* lowest probability; two-thirds of each chamber and 38 states needed to ratify.

**D. The H.R. 1074 design: the nine most junior Justices hear cases, with deemed consent (CRT-15D).** *For:* faster rotation. *Against:* deemed consent is the weakest link under the Appointments Clause (line 193).

## Part 2: packaging (CRT-P1 to P4)

- **P1:** one judiciary bill, term limits included (the ledger's row). The term-limits fight then sets the vote count for the judgeships (line 48).
- **P2:** an integrity bill first, term limits separately (the legacy recommendation, line 59).
- **P3:** integrity only in the first Congress, terms in 2033 (a departure from legacy D1's "no 2033 queue").
- **P4 with P2 (recommended):** the judgeships alone, aimed at 60 votes, phased across presidential terms; the 2024 bill passed the Senate by unanimous consent before a veto (line 67; [EV-DEM-044]).

**CRT-1's size.** The Judicial Conference now asks for 71 judgeships, 2 appellate and 69 district [EV-DEM-041], against the legacy 66 (line 65); weighted filings exceed 500 a judgeship in 20 of the 25 districts it wants enlarged [EV-DEM-042]. Whether vacancies cause delay is contested [EV-DEM-238] [EV-DEM-239]. *Recommended:* the Conference's current request, phased.

## What it means for people

- *Every voter:* under A, each presidential term brings two appointments once the statute bites, so the Court's membership tracks elections over time [EV-DEM-232]; until then, nothing changes.
- *A litigant in a district with 500+ weighted filings a judge:* more judges under P4, phased from the first year.

## Recommendation: part 1 A, part 2 P2 with P4

- Two tracks answer a contested legal question without betting the reform on one reading [EV-DEM-233] [EV-DEM-234] [EV-DEM-235].
- Judgeships alone have bipartisan history [EV-DEM-044]; the integrity record built first lowers the cost of the structural fight (the legacy sequencing argument).
- **What would change this:** a ruling or a commission of legal scholars converging on one reading (then drop the weaker track).
