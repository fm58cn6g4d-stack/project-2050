# The top-rate and capital-income stack: groundwork for stage G

Role: a working note for stage G, the financing settlement (`charter/PLAN.md` section 6). It adds up the top federal rates the plan stacks across flagships as booked on main after #394. It sets out what the evidence says about top-end responses at those rates and lists cap designs with their revenue cost. Status: **notes, November 16, 2026; research and arithmetic only.** It makes no design choice and recommends nothing: Espen decides in stage G. Every figure is project arithmetic on the ledger and the budget model (`models/data/inputs/ledger-revenue.csv`, `ledger-cuts.csv`, `legacy/sources/recovery-budget-audit.csv` case `mixed`), not an official score. The arithmetic is short enough to check by hand, and every step is shown.

Units and conventions:
- **Statutory marginal rates, federal only, both halves of payroll taxes summed.** This is the convention of `legacy/domains/domain-revenue.md` ("Combined top rates") and `models/tools/health_financing.py` (`TOP_RATE_BEFORE_HEALTH`). Part 1 also gives the wedge on total labor cost.
- **A single filer, FY2040 law.** Lines are in 2026 dollars, indexed as booked.
- **"FY2040" and "at maturity".** Two booked charges are still phasing in during FY2040:
  - the broad 2 points above $20K/$40K, at 1.2 points in FY2040 and the full 2 points from 2044 (`assumptions.json`, `tax.broad_*`; `phase()` in `models/tools/recovery-budget/model.py`);
  - SS-7.01's fallback, at 0.4 points in FY2040 and the full 1 point from 2046 (`social_security.fallback_*`; `DEC-2026-11-12-13`).
- **Revenue in FY2040 nominal $B,** from the audit lines of the budget model's `mixed` run on main (plan-wide FY2040 gap **$588B**).

## In plain terms

1. **Wages.** The plan's combined top federal rate on wages is **68.3% at $500,000 and $2 million, and 72.7% at $20 million** in FY2040. At maturity it is **69.7% and 74.1%**. Health's model figure (67.7%) leaves out C18's bracket point and SS-7.01's fallback, and it counts the broad 2 points at their full rate. Measured as a share of what the employer pays in total (the "wedge"), the figures are **63.3% and 67.4%**. The employer health contribution adds nothing at the margin above the Social Security wage base. On average it costs about $32,000 to $37,000 a top earner a year, against the premium it replaces.
2. **Capital.** Above $1 million of income, gains and dividends are taxed as ordinary income. With the investment-income tax at 5%, Social Security's 12.4% on investment income (SS-2), the income contributions and the health contribution, the combined statutory rate on gains is **71.4% (75.8% above $5 million)**. Today it is 23.8%, and the legacy table had about 37-39%.
   - On a 6% return, the 2% wealth tax adds 33 points and the 3% tier adds 50.
   - **So the combined charge passes 100% of the return for anyone above $50 million who is taxed on the return each year:** 105-109% between $50 million and $1 billion, and **126% above $1 billion**, where G11 marks gains to market every year.
   - It stays below 100% only for holders who defer gains for decades, and only because G6 taxes the gains at death, not because they escape.
   - Put another way: at the top income-tax rate, the charge passes 100% whenever the return is below 8.3% (2% tier) or 12.4% (3% tier).
3. **The evidence** is split.
   - At the plan's wage stack, the share of the stack's added statutory revenue the top tier actually yields runs from about **80%** (low taxable-income elasticity, 0.12) through **60-64%** (0.25, the project's working figure) to **19-26%** (Gruber and Saez's 0.57).
   - The booked rows assume about 80-85% (GX's 15% haircut, or the 20% avoidance allowance).
   - Estimates of the revenue-maximizing rate on gains range from about 22-31% (studies that find large realization responses) to 38-47% (Agersnap and Zidar) and 65% or more (CRS's long-run bound). Taxing gains at death and each year moves the estimates up.
   - Estimates of how much wealth tax is avoided range from 8% of the base per point of tax (Saez and Zucman) to 13% (Penn Wharton), 14-32% (European panels) and 43% (Switzerland).
4. **Cap designs (part 4)** run from no cap (no booked cost) to designs that would largely repeal the wealth tax (about $100-125B). Smaller trims to the wage stack cost about $23-45B each.

- **A top state rate** (California's 13.3%) adds about 13 points. The state tax deduction is capped, so the stack comes to about 81% statutory and about 76% on labor cost.

### 1.3 The employer health contribution's incidence, stated separately

- **The design:** HEC is 19.3% of each worker's pay between $20,000 and the Social Security wage base, less the small-employer allowance (`DEC-2026-11-15-14`). It is booked at $1,945B in FY2040.
- **At the margin it adds nothing above the wage base,** so it is not in the stack at $500,000, $2 million or $20 million. Below the wage base it is a 19.3% employer-side marginal charge. Workers between $20,000 and the base face it, not top earners.
- **On average** it is a fixed amount per top earner: 19.3% × (wage base − $20,000). That is about **$31,700 at 2026's base ($184,500)**, and about $37,000 at FY2040's base in 2026 dollars (the base grows with average wages).
- **Who bears it.** The standard view is that workers bear it in lower pay over time:
  - CRFB on Warren's similar contribution [EV-CAR-741];
  - Anand (2017), about half passed into pay within a few years (care review F-3).
- **Borne in full by the worker, it costs** about 6.3% of pay at $500,000, 1.6% at $2 million and 0.2% at $20 million.
- **But it replaces the employer's premium** (premiums become contributions; total compensation fixed, HX/HEX).
  - For a top earner with a family plan, the employer's share of the premium is about $20,800 a year in the checkpoint memo's figures (`checkpoint-financing.md` part B, 2026 dollars). Grown at the cost review's 1.40 real factor to FY2040, it is roughly $28,000. So the net change is roughly **+$4,000 to +$9,000 a year**.
  - With single coverage (about $11,000 employer share in FY2040), it is roughly **+$21,000 to +$26,000**.
  - These figures are working figures for scale, not modeled.
- **Its scale:** the incidence is a level effect on top earners that is small against their tax. It is not a marginal rate, and it does not enter part 3's response arithmetic.

### 2.3 Each layer as an effective tax on a 6% return

The method:
- **The return.** A fortune earns a 6% nominal total return. In the deferral cases, all of it is appreciation, the case most favorable to the holder.
- **Deferral.** A gain held T years and then taxed at rate t has an accrual-equivalent effective rate. That is the annual rate that leaves the same after-tax wealth.
- **The wealth tax** at rate w is a charge of w / 6% on the return.
- **No offsets.** The two taxes are not deductible against each other in the booked design.

| Layer, alone | Effective tax on a 6% return |
| --- | --- |
| Gains under current law, held to death (stepped-up basis) | 0% |
| Gains under current law, realized after 20 years (23.8%) | 15.7% |
| **HS3**: gains above $1M at ordinary rates, the plan's full stack at 71.4%, realized each year | 71.4% (75.8% above $5M) |
| HS3, deferred 10 / 20 / 30 years | 65.7 / 58.7 / 51.7% (above $5M: 70.6 / 64.0 / 57.0%) |
| **H1a** on active pass-through income (the 3.8% layer) | 3.8 points (5 with H1b) |
| **G11**, mark-to-market for billionaires | Removes deferral: the full annual rate, 75.8% |
| **G6**, gains at death | Removes the escape: deferral until death is taxed at the deferral rates above, not at 0% |
| **Wealth tax, 2% tier** ($50M-$1B) | **33.3%** |
| **Wealth tax, 3% tier** (above $1B) | **50.0%** |

The estate tax (C35: 45% above a frozen $3.5M) is outside this note's list. Spread over a 30-year holding, it is roughly equivalent to a further 2% a year wealth tax, about 33% of a 6% return. Stage G should keep it in view.

### 2.4 Where the combined charge passes 100% of the return (`health-cost-review.md` 2.8, EV-CAR-736)

| Holder (marginal dollar) | Income-tax layers | Wealth tax | **Combined, on a 6% return** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Above $1B, G11 marks gains to market each year | 75.8% | 50.0% | **125.8%** |
| $50M-$1B, return taxed each year, income above $5M | 75.8% | 33.3% | **109.1%** |
| $50M-$1B, return taxed each year, income $1M-$5M | 71.4% | 33.3% | **104.7%** |
| $50M-$1B, gains deferred 20 years, then sold or taxed at death (G6) | 58.7-64.0% | 33.3% | 92.0-97.3% |
| $50M-$1B, gains deferred 30 years | 51.7-57.0% | 33.3% | 85.0-90.3% |
| Below $50M, above $1M of income, taxed each year | 71.4-75.8% | 0 | 71.4-75.8% |

**Break-even returns.** The combined charge passes 100% whenever the return r is below w / (1 − t):
- **at t = 75.8%:** 8.3% for the 2% tier and 12.4% for the 3% tier;
- **at t = 71.4%:** 7.0% and 10.5%.

**On real returns it is worse.** With inflation at about 2.3%, a 6% nominal return is about 3.7% real. Then the 2% tier alone takes 54% of the real return, and the income-tax layers, which fall on nominal gains, take more than 100% of it by themselves.

**What this means for revenue:**
- These are marginal charges on the marginal dollar of wealth. Average rates are lower, and holders with high returns stay under 100%.
- But above $50M every holder taxed each year, and every billionaire, keeps less than nothing from the marginal 6% return. Holding taxable, easily valued assets then destroys value for them. That is the condition under which avoidance, shifts into hard-to-value private holdings [EV-CAR-733], giving, consumption and expatriation pay most.
- This is Gleckman's point [EV-CAR-736]. CRFB scored Warren's combined wealth and accrual taxes at $1.5 trillion against $3.2 trillion claimed, because interactions lead to high avoidance [EV-CAR-738].
- **The ledger already nets the revenue:** HS2 is booked "net of G11 and G6", and GX haircuts G11, G6, G9 and H1b by 15%. **The rates are not netted.** Every row's statutory rate applies in full.

## Part 3. What the evidence says about responses and revenue at these rates

This part builds on `health-financing.md` 2.4 and `legacy/domains/domain-revenue.md` ("Combined top rates"). The new sources are marked "not yet in the register". They are cited, not saved, and should be registered in stage G before stage G relies on them.

### 3.1 Wages and ordinary income

- **The revenue-maximizing top rate is contested.** On Diamond and Saez's formula, τ* = 1 / (1 + a·e) with a Pareto parameter a = 1.5, all taxes counted (federal, state, payroll, sales):

| Elasticity e | Source | τ* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.12 | Low end of Saez, Slemrod and Giertz's range (2012, not yet in the register) | 85% |
| 0.25 | Diamond and Saez; the project's working figure (`TOP1_ELASTICITY`) | 73% |
| 0.40 | High end of Saez, Slemrod and Giertz's range | 62% |
| 0.57 | Gruber and Saez, above $100,000 [EV-CAR-720] | 54% |

  The other view: Mankiw, Weinzierl and Yagan put the optimum near 48% (revenue tab in the legacy files). Their optimum rests on a welfare judgment, not on revenue alone.
- **Where the plan sits:** federal 68.3-72.7% (69.7-74.1% at maturity), plus state and sales taxes. That is above τ* for e ≥ 0.25 in high-tax states and near it elsewhere. On the low elasticity the stack is below the peak everywhere.
- **What the top tier yields of the stack's statutory increment.**
  - The method: income above the threshold responds to the net-of-tax rate with elasticity e. Current law's 40.8% at the top is the start.

  | Elasticity | Yield at $500,000-$5M (to 68.3%) | Yield above $5M (to 72.7%) |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | 0.12 | 82% | 80% |
  | 0.25 | 64% | 60% |
  | 0.57 | 26% | 19% |

  - The booked rows assume about 80-85%: their own scores' responses, GX's 15% on G9 and H1b, and the 20% avoidance allowance on the income contributions. So the booked figures are consistent with the low elasticity and optimistic on the working one.
  - DY1 (`ledger-dynamic.csv`, $0.3-1.0T over FY2030-39) is the project's existing dynamic allowance for top-end and corporate rows. This is the stacking problem `health-financing.md` 2.4 named, now measured on the full stack.
  - The response also lowers state income tax revenue, a cost that falls outside the federal ledger.
- **Where the response comes from.**
  - Withheld wages are misreported at about 1%, against 55% for income nobody reports [EV-CAR-719]. So most of the response at the top is timing, form of pay (wages to pass-through or deferred pay) and realization, not hidden wages.
  - A response through form of pay mostly moves income between the rows of part 2. Pass-through and investment income face about the same stack (71.4%) as wages (68.3%), so shifting gains little, except to income taxed late (deferred gains) or not at all (stepped-up basis, now ended by G6).
- **Migration.** Tax-motivated emigration matters less for a federal tax. The United States taxes citizens wherever they live, and expatriation triggers a mark-to-market exit tax on net gains above an exemption (IRC 877A). Within-country moves affect only state revenue.

### 3.2 Capital gains

- **The response of realizations to the rate** is the central disagreement:

| View | Elasticity | Revenue-maximizing rate on gains | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Large response (transitory timing included) | Dowd, McClelland and Muthitacharoen (2015, revised 2024): about −0.98 at a 22% rate | 22% | CRS R48562, Table 4 (not yet in the register) |
| Large response (transitory timing included) | Auten and Joulfaian: −0.79 | 28% | CRS R48562, Table 4 |
| Large response (transitory timing included) | Dowd and McClelland (2024): −0.71 | 31% | CRS R48562, Table 4 |
| Long-run, state variation | Agersnap and Zidar (2021, AER: Insights): revenue elasticity −0.3 to −0.5 over ten years | **38-47%** | AER: Insights 3(4) (not yet in the register) |
| Long-run bound | Gravelle (CRS, 2025): maximum permanent elasticity −0.19 to −0.34 | **65% or more** | CRS R48562 |

- **Under current law** (stepped-up basis at death), holders can defer forever, which is why the measured responses are large.
- **The plan changes the setting.** G6 taxes gains at death and G11 taxes billionaires' gains each year. Both reduce how much holders can respond by waiting. The legacy file (`domain-revenue.md`) makes the same point for rates near 37-39%.
- **At 71.4-75.8% the evidence splits.** The plan's rate is above every estimate except CRS's bound. Even on that bound it is near the peak, and there CRS assumes all gains would otherwise be realized each year.
- **Combined reforms are untested.** The legacy Talarico stress test notes that gains parity, mark-to-market and stepped-up basis repeal have never been enacted together. It says their combined static scores "could be off by a third either way" (`domain-revenue.md`, REV-G11).

### 3.3 The wealth tax

- **How much of the base is avoided.** Estimates are semi-elasticities: the percent of taxable wealth lost per percentage point of tax.

| Estimate | Erosion per point | At 2% | At 3% | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Saez and Zucman, for Warren's campaign | 8% | 16% | 24% | Average of four studies; PWBM's critique (not yet in the register) |
| Penn Wharton Budget Model | 13% | 26% | 39% | PWBM, December 2019 |
| Other countries' panel studies (Denmark, Sweden, Colombia and others) | 14-32% | | | Brülhart et al., Table 2 |
| Switzerland, aggregate | 43% | | | Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf and Schmidheiny (2022) |
| Switzerland, largest reforms | ≈100% | | | Brülhart et al. (2022) |

  - **PWBM** scored Warren's 2%/3% tax (2019) at $2.7T over ten years against her $3.75T: $4.8T with no avoidance, and $1.4T with extreme avoidance. PWBM's own score applies each household's marginal rate, so the 3% tier erodes more.
  - **Switzerland's response** is about a quarter migration between cantons, a fifth a housing-price rise, and the rest most plausibly evasion, where financial wealth is self-reported. It holds that "even the largest observed responses do not seem to have implied Laffer revenue effects" (Brülhart et al., not yet in the register).
- **The two sides on enforcement:**
  - Saez and Zucman argue that billionaires' wealth is highly visible and that strong enforcement (third-party reporting, an exit tax, no exemptions) keeps avoidance near 15%.
  - PWBM and Riedl answer that avoidance rises with the rate, and that Europe's record (most wealth taxes repealed since 1990) is the better guide.
  - Valuing private businesses is the weak point either way [EV-CAR-733].
- **Revenue.** HS2 is booked at $150B in FY2040, with a range of $0-250B that already spans these views. CRFB's central score of Warren's wealth and accrual taxes was about 47% of the campaign's [EV-CAR-738].
- **Legal risk** is the largest single risk, and is separate from these responses. Most scholars read a wealth tax as a direct tax that must be apportioned, and *Moore* (2024) left the realization question open (`domain-revenue.md`, REV-G11).

### 3.4 What the evidence implies for stage G (findings, not choices)

- **The booked revenue assumes the low end of top-end responses** for wages and ordinary income. On the working elasticity (0.25), the top-tier part of the stack yields roughly three-quarters of what is booked (60-64% of statutory against 80-85%).
- **The capital stack (71-76%) sits above most estimates of the revenue-maximizing rate on gains.** It sits near the peak even on CRS's long-run bound.
- **The wealth tax on top of it** brings the marginal charge above 100% for every annual taxpayer above $50M.
- **Contested, with no resolution here:**
  - the elasticity, 0.12 or 0.57;
  - the realization response once G6 and G11 are in place;
  - whether enforcement can hold wealth-tax avoidance near 8% or near 30%.
- **A joint score is the evidence that would settle it.** The legacy work asked for one before any further top-end row was stacked (`domain-revenue.md`, REV-G11 stress test), and the budget model can't produce it.

## Part 4. A menu of cap designs (for Espen in stage G; no recommendation)

**How costs are counted:**
- Each cost is booked revenue lost in FY2040 on the ledger's own scoring. Part 3 suggests some of that revenue may not arrive anyway, so the cost on the evidence can be smaller.
- Costs to the Social Security funds are marked; they don't change the general-fund gap.
- **Wealth-tax arithmetic** assumes revenue ∝ t × (1 − ε·t), with ε = 8-13% per point.
- **Working figure** (rough, to verify): wealth above $1B is about 40% of the taxable base above $50M, and billionaires pay about half of HS2. This comes from about $6.7T of billionaire wealth (Forbes) against about $15T above the $50M line (Federal Reserve distributional accounts); neither is in the register.

### Capital and wealth

| Option | What it does | FY2040 cost ($B), central (range) | What it does to the 100% problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| **B1. Drop the 3% tier** | 2% on all wealth above $50M | 16 (13-20); at ε = 25% it raises revenue instead | Billionaires: 126% → 109% |
| **B2. 1.5% / 2.5%** | Lower both tiers by half a point | 23 (21-26) | 109% → 101%; 126% → 117% |
| **B3. 1% / 2%** | Lower both tiers by a point | 50 (46-54); at ε = 25%, about 9 | 109% → 92%; 126% → 109% |
| **C1. Combined ceiling at 100% of an imputed return** | Wealth tax cut so that it plus federal income taxes on a 6% imputed return don't exceed 100% of that return | 40 (30-55) | None above 100% by construction |
| **C2. Combined ceiling at 75%** | The same at 75%, near France's former plafonnement (to verify) | 125 (100-145), nearly all of HS2 | None above 75%. In effect it repeals the wealth tax for those taxed each year |
| **C3. Ceiling on realized income** | As C1/C2, but against realized income. Spain caps its income and wealth taxes together at 60% of income (to verify) | Larger than C1/C2, and growing as holders minimize realized income | France's realized-income caps were gamed through holding companies |

## Method and sources

- **Rates:** ledger rows as booked (`models/data/inputs/ledger-revenue.csv`, `ledger-cuts.csv`), with phase-ins from `models/tools/recovery-budget/assumptions.json` and `model.py`. Health's rates are from `DEC-2026-11-15-10` to `-13`. SS-1, SS-2, SS-5 and SS-7.01 are from `dossiers/secure-floor/4-specification.md` part D.
- **FY2040 amounts:** `legacy/sources/recovery-budget-audit.csv`, case `mixed`, regenerated on main at eda35f6. The income contributions' FY2040 per-point yields ($66.9B a high point, $180.3B a broad point) come from solving 4H + 1.2B = $483.8B with the model's 2034 ratio of the two.
- **Effective rates:** accrual-equivalent rate = 1 − r_a / r, where (1 + r_a)^T = (1 + r)^T − t((1 + r)^T − 1), with r = 6%. Revenue retained = (t₁z − t₀) / (t₁ − t₀), with z = ((1 − t₁) / (1 − t₀))^e and t₀ = 40.8%.
- **Registered evidence:** EV-CAR-719, -720, -733, -736, -738, -741, all graded C or B in `dossiers/care/evidence.json`.
- **New, not yet in the register (cited, to register before stage G relies on them):**
  - Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf and Schmidheiny, "Behavioral Responses to Wealth Taxes: Evidence from Switzerland", *AEJ: Economic Policy* 14(4), 2022 (doi 10.1257/pol.20200258);
  - Agersnap and Zidar, "The Tax Elasticity of Capital Gains and Revenue-Maximizing Rates", *AER: Insights*, 2021 (NBER w27705);
  - Gravelle, *Boundaries on the Long-Run Realization Response to Changes in Capital Gains Taxes*, CRS R48562, June 2025;
  - Penn Wharton Budget Model, *Senator Elizabeth Warren's Wealth Tax: Projected Budgetary and Economic Effects*, December 2019, and its 2021 score of the Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act ($2.1T over 2022-31);
  - Saez, Slemrod and Giertz, *Journal of Economic Literature* 50(1), 2012.
- **Precedents mentioned but not checked here:** France's plafonnement and bouclier fiscal, and Spain's joint cap. Verify before relying on them.
- **Not an official score.** The menu's costs are rough working figures. A joint behavioral score (JCT, or the household model with a top-end response) would replace them.
