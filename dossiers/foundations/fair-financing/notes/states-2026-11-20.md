# The states' combined payment, state by state

Role: the state table that `../1-audit.md` part C, item 10 asked for "for the batch". It was made a blocker (B-5) by the milestone review of #469, because memo G1 A raises the states' long-term care payment from 50% to 100%. Status: **notes, November 20, 2026; public data; project arithmetic.** It decides nothing. The figures are in [`states-2026-11-20.csv`](states-2026-11-20.csv), one row for each state and DC plus a US total. A Standard-tier helper built it from public data; the author checked its totals against MACPAC's published subtotals.

**Method:**
- No source splits long-term care spending into its federal and state shares. The state share is taken as long-term care spending times (1 − FMAP).
- "Health" is each state's total state share less that long-term care share, so it includes the states' administration ($15.7B).
- The two sources are a year apart, so health is overstated by about a year of long-term care growth, roughly $8B nationally (an estimate, not a sourced figure).
- The territories are left out.

## What the table shows

1. **In the base year no state pays more than it spends today,** at any of the four shares. At 100% each state pays exactly its current state share, by construction.
2. **What 100% costs each state is very uneven.**
   - At 50% the most care-heavy states keep the most relief: North Dakota pays 70.6% of its current state share, Alaska 77.5%, New York 77.8% and Connecticut 78.7%.
   - Moving to 100% takes that relief away. New York's payment rises from $32.5B to $41.8B in the data year's dollars; Pennsylvania's from $14.2B to $17.6B.
3. **The growth risk the review raised is real, and the table can only point to it.** The payment grows with the plan's index, CBO's Medicare category, which covers both per-person cost growth and enrollment. A state whose own long-term care costs would have grown more slowly would pay more than its own path, in later years.
   - On a proxy (each state's long-term care growth over 2019-23 against the national 7.8% a year), 30 states grew more than a point a year slower.
   - The strongest cases, flagged in both windows tested: Kansas (−9.4 points a year), Tennessee (−6.9), Utah (−5.8), Massachusetts (−5.5), North Dakota (−5.0), Connecticut (−4.7), Ohio (−4.6), Mississippi (−4.5), Wyoming (−4.5) and Texas (−3.8).
   - Wisconsin's series swings 143% in one year, so its flag is probably noise. The period spans the pandemic and ARPA's home-care money. Half the states sit below an average by construction.
   - This is a proxy, not a projection.
4. **The booked health payment** in FY2040 is $466B (`health-cost-review.md`). `health-financing.md` line 244 gives $689B on an older basis (review note N-9). This table uses today's spending, not either projection.

## What it means for memo G1

- The memo's claim "no state pays more than it pays today" holds only in the base year. Later it depends on the index.
- Memo G1 now offers an index option: each state's payment grows with the lower of the plan's index and its own long-term care cost index. That would need state cost data the plan's fund doesn't hold yet; its cost isn't priced.
- The payment is collected by offset against "any federal payment to the state" (HLT-9.09). That is wider than Part D's clawback, which offsets Medicaid payments only.
