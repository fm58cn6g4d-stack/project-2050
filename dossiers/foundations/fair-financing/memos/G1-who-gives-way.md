# Memo G1: Which other spending gives way

Role: decision memo for Espen, fork 1 of stage G's batch ([`README.md`](README.md); [`../2-settlement.md`](../2-settlement.md) part 2; `../1-audit.md` part C, item 1). Status: **provisional, drafted on the recommendation, November 20, 2026.** Not decided, not booked. Nominal $B on case `mixed`, priced by `models/tools/financing-protection/packages.py` (results in [`../notes/settlement-results.md`](../notes/settlement-results.md)). Project arithmetic on exploratory models, not official scores.

## The question

Health comes first on the plan's revenue (`DEC-2026-11-15-04`). Its share, **$532.1B in FY2040**, is taken from the top-end and corporate rows first (`DEC-2026-11-15-20`; the allocation is in `../2-settlement.md` part 1). That leaves the rest of the plan without the money those rows were paying for.

You said: "We can downsize other expenditures in project 2050 plans in the financing settlement, but I want a healthcare system that works and is durable." This memo asks how much of the rest of the gap to close by giving way on other spending. Whatever this memo doesn't close, memo G3's tax does. **Every $105B a year of downsizing in FY2040 is about one point less of that VAT.**

Each flagship's own memos already priced cheaper designs (a helper's sweep of every booked dossier, listed at the end). Most would undo something you decided.

### A. End the $300 credit, and the states pay their full current share for long-term care (recommended)

**The $300 adult credit** is a refundable $300 a year for every adult, booked at $105.8B in FY2040 and $915.8B over the decade.
- It came from the October 26 exploratory budget as an illustration (`legacy/sources/recovery-and-solvency-budget.md`). It was meant to offset the payroll and income contributions for lower earners.
- **It was never adopted.** The October 26 decision lists "neither universal-credit amount" as not selected and asks for a better-targeted credit instead (`legacy/governing/project-2050-governing-decisions.md`, lines 432 and 450). No `DEC-` row adopts it.
- No flagship designs it, and D3 never counted it as a benefit.
- The protection it was meant to give now comes from the secure floor (the child benefit, the childless EITC, SNAP and SSI repairs) and from the health contribution's exemption below 250% of poverty.
- *Who loses:* every adult loses a $300 a year payment they were never promised. For a household of two on $30,000, that is $600, or 2% of income.

**The states' long-term care payment at 100%.**
- Today the states would pay 50% of their current long-term care spending into the plan, indexed (HLT-9.09, `DEC-2026-11-11-06`). For health they pay 100% of their former Medicaid spending (`DEC-2026-11-15-25`).
- Raising long-term care to 100% treats the two alike. Each state then pays what it already spends today, indexed, and the plan takes on all the growth.
- Care's memo 17 priced the first step of this (option 7, 65%: $44B in FY2040) and said it "belongs with health's share in the financing settlement".
- *Who loses:* the states. They lose about $148B in FY2040 of relief they would otherwise have had ($313B in FY2056). No state pays more than it pays today. People's benefits don't change.
- *Legal risk:* moderate, and higher than at 50%. The payment is collected by offset on health's instrument (HLT-7.17). A larger mandatory payment is a bigger target for a claim that the federal government is coercing the states (*NFIB v. Sebelius*, 2012). The nearest precedent is Medicare Part D's "clawback" of the states' former drug spending, now at 75%. It has stood since 2006; the states' challenge then never reached a ruling on the merits.

On D3 these trims cost the bottom fifth about $1,070 a year on the decade average, and the fourth fifth about $430. The VAT they replace costs the bottom fifth less, so B leaves the bottom three fifths worse off than A and the fourth fifth slightly better off (Q1 +$13,102 against +$13,896; Q4 +$4,412 against +$4,102). It also raises the revenue-mix test's share (memo G3: 60% over FY2030-39 and 52% in FY2040, against 57% and 50%).

### C. The credit only

The states keep their long-term care relief, and the VAT is about 1.4 points higher.

### D. Cuts instead of a new tax

With A done and no VAT, the plan is still **$487B short in FY2040, 0.90% of GDP, rising to $1,160B, 1.21% of GDP, in FY2056.** Closing that with spending alone would take roughly all of child care and pre-K and care parts C and D (FAM-2, HLT-10 and HLT-11: $304B in FY2040), plus the whole child benefit ($220B). The cuts would also have to keep growing, because health's budget grows faster than the economy (memo G8). This option is listed so the scale is visible. It is not recommended.

## Recommendation

**A.** It closes about $254B a year in FY2040 without cutting any benefit a person was promised. The credit was never adopted. The states keep paying what they pay today.

**What would change this:**
- If you would rather trim benefits than add VAT points, then **B**: each of its four items stands on its own, and you could take any of them.
- If the states' legal exposure worries you more than the extra VAT point and a half, then **C**, or 65-80% for long-term care (−$44B and −$89B in FY2040).

## Not recommended, for the record

The sweep also found, among others: the child benefit tapered from about $40,000 (secure floor memo 1 B, −$110-145B); stronger Medicaid in place of the home care benefit (care memo 6 A, about −$305B); no clean-energy tax credits (clean power memo 2, −$55.9B in FY2040); and a UI fix without a federal standard (work memo 7 A, about −$10B). Each would reverse a decided design at the heart of a flagship.

## Question for you

**G1.** Close part of the gap by ending the unadopted $300 adult credit and having the states pay 100% of their current long-term care spending (A, recommended)? Or add the four cheaper designs (B, each yes or no), take the credit only (C), or cut instead of taxing (D)?
