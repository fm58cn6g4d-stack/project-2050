# Morning brief: the politics after 2026, and what it means for 2029

Role: a plain-language summary for Espen of the scenario work done overnight on November 7-8, 2026: the 2027 races, what Democrats can do in 2027-28, the 2028 Senate and presidential outlook, and how it adds up for the plan that starts in January 2029 (`DEC-2026-10-29-01`). Status: synthesis only, November 8, 2026. It adds no new modelling. Every number below was checked against its source file at these heads: `main` at `34110f6`, PR #82 at `c83dd98`, PR #84 at `5b4d0f5`, PR #86 at `ee2ea63`, PR #80 at `af4e45a`.

**Where the files are.** Most files are merged into `main` and linked directly. Some files exist only on open PRs and are not merged yet. `senate-model-2028.md`, `senate-model-outputs.md` and `2030-2034-control.md` come from [PR #82](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/pull/82), updated by [PR #84](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/pull/84), which is built on top of #82; figures from them use #84's version. #84 is still in review: the reviewer asked for changes, and some figures there may move again. `2028-republican-branch.md`, `compounding-redistricting.md`, `compounding-defense.md` and `compounding-prebuild.md` come from [PR #86](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/pull/86). All the probabilities are this project's own scenario arithmetic, not forecasts. No rating service has rated 2028 yet.

## 1. The ten most important findings

1. **With a Democratic president, the 2029 Senate is probably about 57 seats.** The average is 56.7, and 90% of runs fall between 53 and 60. 55 or more happens in 84% of runs; 60 or more in only 9%. (`senate-model-2028.md`, section 4, [PR #84](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/pull/84))
2. **Democrats likely keep the Senate even if they lose the presidency, and that Senate would block a Republican budget bill.** Under a Republican president they still control it in 85% of runs, with about 53 seats. Without the Senate, a Republican president cannot pass a 51-vote tax or spending bill. (same file, section 4; `2028-republican-branch.md`, PR #86)
3. **January 2029 is the high point of the decade.** The average falls from 56.7 seats in 2029 to 54.1 after the 2030 midterm and 48.9 after 2032. Democrats hold 50 or more in every Congress through 2034 in only 46% of runs. (`2030-2034-control.md`, section 3, PR #84)
4. **Five seats drive the 2032 drop.** Mississippi, South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa and Alaska supply 63% of the expected 2032 losses. All are states Trump won by 13 to 23 points, and all were won in the 2026 wave. (same file, section 4)
5. **The best protection for 2032 is a milder 2030 midterm, and that depends on visible delivery in 2029-30.** Halving the usual midterm loss raises the chance of holding the Senate through 2034 from 46% to 56%. Statehood helps less than it looks (52-53%), because it also makes the Senate bigger, so a majority needs more seats. Stronger candidates in the exposed seats barely help (47-49%). (same file, section 5)
6. **On the filibuster, who is in the caucus matters more than how many seats it has.** About 6 Democrats are expected to oppose a rules change, against a margin of about 6-7 seats. The graduated-cloture plan (D2-F) has the votes in 62% of runs, but the range is 9-89% depending on senators whose views are unknown. (`senate-model-2028.md`, section 9, PR #84)
7. **The Minnesota Senate seat is on the ballot in November 2027, not 2028.** Its winner serves to January 2031. Losing it costs a full seat in every 2029 scenario and 13-19 points off the chance of 55+. Only 37 Democratic seats not up in 2028 are certain, not 40. (`senate-model-2028.md`, sections 1 and 9, PR #84; [2027-races.md](2027-races.md), section 4)
8. **There is probably one real legislative year, 2027, then a campaign.** Two budget bills that need only 51 votes (FY2027 and FY2028) are available without touching the FY2029 slot the 2029 plan needs. The chance a reconciliation bill passes Congress is put at 55-75%; the risk is how to pay for it. ([2027-28-playbook.md](2027-28-playbook.md), in brief and section 2; [2027-28-stress-tests.md](2027-28-stress-tests.md), question 3)
9. **In 2028 the House is the hinge, and after 2028 the map moves to the Sun Belt.** The middle House seat is Trump +4.5 against Trump +1.5 nationally, about a 3-point Republican tilt: Democrats break even near D+3 and hold the House comfortably at D+5 or better. On projected 2030 census seats, the Harris states plus Wisconsin, Michigan and Pennsylvania fall from 270 electoral votes to 258. The 2031 maps are drawn by whoever wins the November 2030 midterm, which is why a ban on mid-decade redraws plus commissions (DEM-2) is put at about 5-10 Democratic House seats a cycle. ([2028-outlook.md](2028-outlook.md), section 5; [compounding-2029-2050.md](compounding-2029-2050.md), section 4; `compounding-redistricting.md`, PR #86)
10. **The 2027-28 defense deal is locked in for a decade, and the plan's own cost checks won't show it.** The "parity" path ($905 billion in FY2028) puts about $26 billion a year more into the 2029 starting level and adds about $0.30 trillion of defense over 2030-39. NSC-1A is scored against CBO's baseline, so whatever passes becomes the baseline: the ledger and the D4 fiscal-rule checks show $0 in every path. The cost shows up only in total spending and debt. (`compounding-defense.md`, PR #86; [compounding-2029-2050.md](compounding-2029-2050.md), section 2)

## 4. Where the models disagree, or the evidence is thin

- **The filibuster head count conflicts between two files.** The playbook lists Jackson as for ending the filibuster, Turek and Andrews for a talking filibuster, and King as opposed. It cites a Washington Examiner count, a right-leaning source. The Senate model says no position was found for Jackson, Turek or Andrews, treats each as a 25-40% chance of voting no, and does not count King. If the playbook is right, the model's 62% for D2-F is probably too low. The reviewer flagged the same point on #84, and it is not fixed yet. ([2027-28-playbook.md](2027-28-playbook.md), section 6, against `senate-model-2028.md`, section 9)
- **The statehood figures were corrected after review.** The first version of #84 said D.C. and Puerto Rico statehood would raise the chance of holding the Senate through 2034 from 46% to 64%. It had kept counting 50 seats as a majority in a Senate of 102 or 104. With that fixed (`5b4d0f5`), statehood gives only 52-53%, and a milder 2030 midterm becomes the biggest lever. The same fix made the filibuster count come out the same on every run; before it, figures varied by a point or so between runs.
- **A late fix to the Alaska repeal case.** #82's review fix, now merged into #84 (`4528f45`), changes the case where Alaska repeals ranked-choice voting: if Murkowski first beats Dunleavy in a closed Republican primary (assumed 30%), she then wins about 30% of runs, where the earlier text said almost never. As an independent she still almost never wins. The same fix corrects a Tennessee seat counted twice in 2030-32, which moved some figures by about one point.
- **Older files still use the 40-seat base.** The 2028 scenarios in [2028-outlook.md](2028-outlook.md) and #80 rest on the first, simpler estimate in [5-senate-2028.md](5-senate-2028.md). #82 now marks that estimate as superseded: only 37 seats are certain. Some seat odds also differ between the two (at D+3, North Carolina 54% in the old estimate against 65% in the new one; Nevada 67% against 76%).
- **Numbers changed between rounds.** Round 2 corrected for governors' personal popularity: in a neutral year Nevada went from 53% to 59%, Arizona from 60% to 54%, and Pennsylvania from 62% to 56%. Round 3 cut the Murkowski-caucuses-with-Democrats branch from 10% to 6%. Round 4 cut the chance of the ranked-choice repeal from 15% to 4%. Between #82's first version and #84 the 2029 center moved from 56.6 to 56.7 seats.
- **Small mismatches remain.** `2030-2034-control.md` cites 13,150 runs; the outputs say 13,136. P(55+) in 2029 is 84% in one table and 85% in the chained run. None changes a conclusion.
- **The biggest input is a judgment call.** The 2028 national mood is set at D+3, give or take 4.5 points. That is below what history's reversion would suggest (D+5 to D+9) and is a labeled assumption.
- **The 2032 loss is heavier than history.** The model loses about 5 seats; past wave classes lost a median of 1 six years later. The model's authors argue this class is unusually deep in red states (the 2018 losses support them), but this is a contested point.
- **Sources from the current administration.** Under `DEC-2026-10-30-01`, the analysis these agencies produce gets extra skepticism. The playbook's figure that the Medicaid agency (CMS) expects to approve about 2 in 10 state exemption requests is that agency's own projection. The gas-price forecast ($3.91 to $3.56) comes from the Energy Information Administration's October 2026 outlook. Neither has an independent check yet. The Social Security dates (2032 and 2034) come through the Congressional Research Service, an independent source. The census claim in `compounding-2029-2050.md` (that the administration wants to change who counts for seats) is from memory and is marked "verify".
- **#86 leans on memory and trackers in places.** Rubio's role in a 2017 child-credit amendment and the 2015 Supreme Court dissent lineup on redistricting commissions are marked "memory; verify". Most litigation statuses come from case trackers, not the rulings. The 5-10 seat value of DEM-2 is this project's estimate, anchored to FiveThirtyEight (about 5) and Brennan (6-9 in four states). The Republican primary polling comes from a UK aggregator and one AtlasIntel poll.
- **Thin evidence in places.** Several facts are from search summaries or headlines not opened (for example Fetterman's newer filibuster stance in `4-2029-plan.md`, Murkowski's endorsement in the governor race). A 2006 House figure differs between Brookings and the House Clerk. The model uses no polling, because none exists yet for 2028.

## 5. The next research wave, ranked

1. **A senator-by-senator filibuster count for the 2027 caucus.** Settle the conflict in section 4 and find the unknown positions. *Why:* it decides whether the 2029 rule is graduated cloture or a talking filibuster, and it swings D2-F from 9% to 89%.
2. **Recheck after certification (November 18-25).** Alaska, Mississippi and Measure 2, and whether a repeal would apply in 2028. *Why:* these set the base of 37-39 certain seats and decide whether the Murkowski question exists at all.
3. **Does visible delivery in 2029-30 shrink the 2030 midterm loss? And what are the House odds in 2030 and 2032?** *Why:* a milder midterm is now the biggest single lever on keeping the Senate after 2032, and it is the one that policy controls. Halving the usual loss is worth about 10 points.
4. **The talking-filibuster floor-time math at about 6 holdouts.** The old estimate was 213-272 floor days against a 163-day budget. *Why:* if graduated cloture is a coin flip, this becomes the base case, and it may not fit the first Congress.
5. **Statehood as a 2029-30 bill.** Floor time, Puerto Rico's vote on statehood, and which election cycles the new senators fall in. *Why:* after the correction it is worth only about 6-7 points on keeping the Senate after 2032, so its case rests on representation. The plan should say so plainly before spending first-Congress floor time on it.
6. **Which parts of each flagship can pass with 51 votes (Byrd-rule edges).** Care, housing conditions and paid leave first. *Why:* it sorts the 2029 bills between reconciliation and regular order before the bills are fixed.
7. **The Minnesota 2027 special.** Who Klobuchar appoints, and whether Republicans recruit a serious candidate. *Why:* a full seat in every 2029 scenario, and a placeholder appointee costs about 6 points of the chance of holding it.
8. **How to write DEM-2 so it survives the Court.** Should the commission mandate be severable from the mid-decade ban and federal criteria, and what do the 2015 dissents imply? *Why:* DEM-2 is worth about 5-10 House seats a cycle only if it is in force before the 2031 maps, and it is the plan's main answer to losing the 2030 midterm.
