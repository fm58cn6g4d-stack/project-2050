# Senate control, 2029-2034: the 2030 and 2032 classes

Role: whether Democrats keep the Senate through the 2029-30, 2031-32 and 2033-34 Congresses, chained from the 2028 model in `senate-model-2028.md`. It tests Espen's prior that "it's gonna be hard for us to lose the senate before 2032." Status: draft (stage B follow-up), November 8, 2026.

**What kind of evidence this is.** This project's own scenario arithmetic (`tools/senate_model.py`, `simulate_chain`), not a forecast. The 2030 and 2032 environments are drawn from historical swings, and candidates six years out are unknown. Treat the numbers as orders of magnitude.

## In brief

- **Espen's prior holds for the period it names, and the danger is right after it.** With a Democratic president elected in 2028 (evidence-weighted environment), the share of runs in which Democrats hold 50 or more seats is:
  - 100% in the 2029-30 Congress;
  - **82%** continuously through the 2031-32 Congress;
  - **46%** through the 2033-34 Congress, that is, after the 2032 election.

  Under D1's stipulation of a Democratic president re-elected in 2032, the three figures are 100%, 84% and 60%.
- **2030 costs little; 2032 costs a lot.** The 2030 class was elected in 2024's R+1.5 year, so Democrats hold no seat it over-extends, and a typical midterm penalty costs about 2-3 seats (56.7 to 54.1 on average). The 2032 class was elected in 2026's D+13 wave. It holds Democratic seats in Texas, South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa and probably Mississippi and Alaska, all states that voted for Trump by 13 to 23 points in 2024. Even in a D+4 year most of them are underdogs: the model loses about 5 seats (54.1 to 48.9). Mississippi, South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa and Alaska supply 63% of the expected losses (section 4).
- **60 seats are never likely.** The share of runs at 60 or more is 9% in 2029, 15% in 2031 and 7% in 2033 (evidence-weighted, Democratic president). The 2031 figure is higher only because the midterm draw is wide; a midterm gain for the president's party has happened (2002) but is rare. 55 or more is held continuously through the 2031-32 Congress in about half the runs (46-51%).
- **What changes 2032 (round 4).** No single lever fixes it. The largest is a mild 2030 midterm (half the usual penalty), which raises P(holding control through 2033-34) from 46% to 56%. Statehood adds seats but also enlarges the chamber, so control needs 51 of 102 or 52 of 104: D.C. alone gives 52%, and D.C. with Puerto Rico seated by January 2031 gives 53%. Candidate levers barely move it: no retirements gives 47%, and +2 points of candidate and campaign strength in the six exposed seats gives 49%. All three kinds together reach 65%. Statehood itself needs D2's rules change, so it depends on the first Congress.
- **For the plan:** the January 2029 Senate is the peak. Work that needs D2's rules change and a large majority belongs in 2029-30, as D1's first-trifecta scope already says. The 2032 Senate map is a delivery problem as much as a campaign one: six of its most exposed seats are in states the agenda would have to reach by 2032.

## 2. Method

The 2028 run is the seat model in `senate-model-2028.md`. For each simulated 2028 result:

- **2030 midterm.** The environment moves against the president's party by a swing drawn from the ten midterms of 1990-2026: mean 7.2 points, SD 6.9. The swings are 0.1, 12.2, 0.5, −4.3, 9.0, 17.2, 6.5, 9.6, 5.6 and 15.4, from the Brookings House-vote table [SRC-0570] and Cook for 2026 [SRC-0464]. With a Democratic president the average 2030 environment comes out at about R+1.4.
- **2032.** The presidential environment drifts from 2028 by a draw with mean −0.9 and SD 5.7, the change between consecutive presidential margins, 1992-2024 [SRC-0588]. With a Democratic president elected in 2028 the average 2032 environment is about D+5, because winning in 2028 selects the better environments.
- **Each class seat.** Lean as in the 2028 model, plus the holder's candidate effect from their own last race (2024 for Class 1, 2026 for Class 2; same formula, shrunk 60% toward zero). Each holder retires with a 12% chance (an assumption), and an open seat loses the effect. The Minnesota and Tennessee holders follow each run's 2027 and 2028 results, as do Alaska's and Mississippi's in 2032.
- **Control.** Democrats need 50 seats with a Democratic Vice President, else 51.

## 4. Is the 2032 drop real?

Round 2 of the iteration (`senate-model-2028.md`, section 8) checked it against history. Brookings's seat changes [SRC-0570] show how midterm wave classes did six years later, in a presidential year:

| Wave | Six years later | Change for the wave party |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1946 | 1952 | +1 |
| 1958 | 1964 | +1 |
| 1974 | 1980 | about −12 |
| 1986 | 1992 | 0 |
| 1994 | 2000 | −5 |
| 2006 | 2012 | +2 |
| 2010 | 2016 | −2 |
| 2014 | 2020 | −3 |

The median is −1 seat; the model's −5 near D+5 is at the heavy end. Three reasons it is probably right for this class:
- **It reaches deeper than any earlier wave class.** Democrats won seats in states Trump carried by 13.7 (Texas), 16.1 (Kansas) and 17.9 points (South Carolina) in 2024.
- **Polarization punishes that.** Since 2012, 91% of Senate races have matched the state's presidential vote [SRC-0569].
- **The closest recent test was harsh.** In 2018 Democrats held 10 seats in Trump-won states and lost 4 of them (Indiana, Missouri, North Dakota, Florida) even in a D+8.7 House year [SRC-0587].

Against that, the 2026 winners will be incumbents with six years of their own record, and several beat unusually weak opponents (Paxton in Texas). The model credits 40% of each holder's overperformance. Crediting 60% leaves the drop about the same (49.4 seats in January 2033 against 49.0 on that run; section 5 shows the same for the retention lever), so the disagreement with the base rate is about how far these states revert to their lean, not about candidate effects.

**Which seats drive it** (round 4; evidence-weighted environment, Democratic president elected in 2028; `senate-model-outputs.md`, section 11):

| Seat (Democratic-held) | P(held in 2032) | Share of 2032 losses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mississippi | 10% | 15% |
| South Carolina | 27% | 13% |
| Kansas | 32% | 13% |
| Iowa | 37% | 12% |
| Alaska | 28% | 10% |
| Texas | 57% | 8% |

| Maine, Minnesota, New Hampshire | 76-78% | 4% each |

The first five supply 63% of the expected losses. All are states that voted for Trump by 13-23 points in 2024 (Iowa by 13). Mississippi and Alaska count only in runs where Democrats won them in 2026.

## 5. What would change 2032 (round 4)

Same runs as section 3; "held" means in every Congress through 2033-34. With statehood the chamber has 102 or 104 seats, so the thresholds are chamber-adjusted: "50+" means control with a Democratic Vice President (51 of 102, 52 of 104), and "55+" shifts the same way. Mean seats are raw counts. (Correction after review on #84: the first version compared statehood runs against the 100-seat thresholds and overstated them by about 6-11 points.)

| Lever | Mean seats, Jan 2031 | Mean seats, Jan 2033 | P(control), Jan 2033 | Held control | Held 55+ (adjusted) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base | 54.1 | 48.9 | 47% | 46% | 21% |
| **A mild 2030 midterm (half the usual penalty)** | 55.9 | 50.7 | 56% | 56% | 28% |
| Every 2026 winner runs again | 54.1 | 49.0 | 48% | 47% | 22% |
| 2026 winners keep 60% of their overperformance, not 40% | 54.1 | 48.9 | 46% | 46% | 21% |
| +2 points of candidate and campaign strength in the six exposed seats | 54.1 | 49.4 | 49% | 49% | 23% |
| D.C. statehood (DEM-5), seated January 2031 | 56.1 | 50.9 | 52% | 52% | 25% |
| D.C. and Puerto Rico (DEM-5, DEM-6), seated January 2031 | 57.3 | 52.1 | 53% | 53% | 26% |
| D.C. and Puerto Rico, seated January 2033 | 54.1 | 52.1 | 53% | 52% | 26% |
| D.C. and Puerto Rico 2031, a mild midterm and +2 in the exposed seats | 59.1 | 54.3 | 65% | 65% | 37% |

**How statehood is modeled (assumptions).**
- **D.C.:** its two senators caucus with Democrats.
- **Puerto Rico:** its two split by its own politics. The statehood party is led by Republicans at the national level (Gov. Jenniffer González). Shares: both Democratic 45%, one each 25%, both Republican 30%, an expected 1.15 Democratic seats.
- **Admission:** both require D2's rules change in the first Congress (◐ at 51 in `legacy/domains/domain-democracy.md`). Puerto Rico also needs DEM-6's binding plebiscite to choose statehood, which is not modeled: the row assumes it does.
- **Terms:** new senators' classes are not modeled, so a new seat that happens to be up in 2032 is treated as held.

**Reading the levers.**
1. **Candidate levers barely work.** Even every incumbent running again and a stronger campaign in the six exposed seats add under a seat in 2033, because those states sit 9-18 points right of the nation. Raising the carry-over of 2026 overperformance has no net effect: it helps the red-state winners, but they are already near the 6-point cap, and it hurts the winners who ran behind their state's lean in 2026 (Maine's Jackson, Michigan's El-Sayed).
2. **The 2030 midterm matters through 2033.** Seats lost in 2030 stay lost when 2032 comes. A 2030 penalty half the usual size is worth about 1.8 seats in both 2031 and 2033.
3. **Statehood helps less than its seat count suggests.** D.C. and Puerto Rico add about 3 Democratic seats in expectation (2 + 1.15), but they also raise the bar for control by 2. The net gain is about one seat-equivalent, and smaller still if Puerto Rico's senators split or go Republican. D.C. alone nets one (2 seats against a 1-seat higher bar), which is why it does about as well as D.C. plus Puerto Rico. Statehood's stronger case is representation and the House and Electoral College, not the 2033 Senate majority. Its passage also depends on D2 (`senate-model-2028.md`, section 9, puts D2-F at about 62%).

## 6. What it means for Project 2050 (options and questions)

- **Front-loading.** The Senate's room for defections on a rules change runs about 6-7 votes in 2029-30, about 4 in 2031-32, and is close to none in 2033-34 on the central path. D1's first-trifecta scope already puts regular-order bills in 2029-30. The model supports that, and raises a question: should any regular-order bill now planned for the second Congress move into the first? MOV-31 is the named exception.
- **Reconciliation's last window.** R3 (FY2031, summer 2030) is the last vehicle passed by the 2029 Senate. Anything slipped to an FY2032 or later resolution depends on the 2030 Senate (about 54 seats on average, 76-80% chance of 51 or more). With a Democratic president, 50 seats would still suffice.
- **D2's R3 or R4 choice.** Narrowing reconciliation to deficit reduction from 2031 (R3) or from 2037 (R4) is decided in D2's adoption resolution. A 40-55% chance of a Republican Senate from January 2033 (central to stipulated paths) bears on it either way: a narrowed rule binds a Republican majority too, which is its reciprocity case. Which matters more to Espen, the constraint on a future Republican majority or the room for a Democratic one after 2032?
- **Statehood's timing.** On the Senate alone, DEM-5 and DEM-6 are worth about 6-7 points of P(holding control through 2033-34) once the larger chamber is counted, whether seated in 2031 or 2033. The Senate is therefore not a strong reason to rush them. Their case rests on representation (and DEM-6's plebiscite), not on 2033 control. Question for Espen: should the plan say so, so that statehood is not sold internally as a Senate-majority strategy?
- **The 2030 midterm is the strongest lever on 2033.** Halving the usual penalty is worth about 10 points, more than any structural or candidate lever. That ties the Senate's future to how visibly the 2029-30 program delivers before November 2030 (D1's "felt by November 2030" tests).
- **The 2032 map as a delivery test.** Texas, South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa, Mississippi and Alaska are where Democrats are most exposed in 2032. Question: should the delivery plans (benefits felt by 2032; DEM-1E's registration points) report on reach in those states specifically?
- **The House is a separate question.** This model covers only the Senate. D1's House stipulation (possibly lost in 2030, a trifecta again from 2033 if DEM-1 is in force) is untouched.
- **Questions for Espen:**
  1. Is "before 2032" the horizon that matters, or "through 2034"? The answers differ: about 82-85% against 47-60%.
  2. Should D1's notes name the 2032 cliff as a planning constraint, alongside the stated Senate exposure in #80?
