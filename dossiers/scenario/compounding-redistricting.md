# Redistricting through 2032: the 2028 redraws, DEM-2's worth, and who draws in 2031

Role: the mid-decade redraws that could change the 2028 House map, state by state with litigation status; what DEM-2 (redistricting commissions and the mid-decade ban) would be worth in House seats if in force before the 2031 maps; and the elections that will choose who draws those maps, with the states ranked. Status: draft (stage B, step 3, round 2), as of November 8, 2026. **Options, not decisions.** Seat estimates marked "(analysis)" are this project's, anchored to the published ranges cited beside them. Most litigation statuses come from case-tracker summaries (All About Redistricting, NCSL) rather than the filings, so treat them as one level weaker than an opened ruling.

## In brief

- **For 2028 the net movement is small.** Maryland (+1 Democratic) and Mississippi (+1 Republican) are likely; Louisiana's last Black-majority district is under a new challenge; New York (up to +4 Democratic) rides on a November 2027 referendum; Wisconsin's map cases are undecided. Texas, Georgia, Ohio and Kansas now have Democratic governors' vetoes, and New Jersey's governor ruled a redraw out [SRC-1024] [SRC-1025] [SRC-1026] [SRC-1030] [SRC-1047] [SRC-0986].
- **The 2026 Republican redraws mostly failed.** In the seven states where Republicans redrew, Axios found them "on track to lose as many as eight seats, rather than gain the 15 it had hoped"; only TX-32 and TN-9 delivered [SRC-1035]. Indiana's and South Carolina's rejected maps would have backfired on 2026 votes (DDHQ) [SRC-1028]. That "dummymander" lesson is now the main brake on further redraws by either party.
- **DEM-2 is worth most in the one world where it can pass.** With a Democratic President from 2029, the 2030 midterm would probably favor Republicans in the races that pick the 2031 mapmakers. Against Republican-drawn maps in Texas, Florida, North Carolina, Georgia and Ohio, a ban plus commissions is worth about **5 to 10 Democratic seats** a cycle through 2040 (analysis, consistent with FiveThirtyEight's 2022 estimate that undoing Republican gerrymanders was worth about 5 Democratic seats [SRC-1038] and Brennan's 6-9 seats in four states [SRC-1039]). If Democrats sweep 2030 instead, DEM-2 is still worth a few Democratic seats, about +4 in b-3's 2031-pens model (`redistricting-2030-model.md`, PR #91, in review), because the North Carolina Senate, Florida's chambers, Ohio's legislature and the Texas Senate stay Republican even in a strong Democratic year; an earlier draft of this file called it "about neutral", which was wrong.
- **"2030 decides" understates the calendar.** New York's map is decided in November 2027; Virginia's in 2027 and 2029; Mississippi's and Louisiana's legislatures sit from 2027 to 2032; the Kansas, New Mexico and South Carolina senates that act in 2031 are elected in 2028; North Carolina's governor has no veto over maps at all [SRC-1021] [SRC-1045].
- **The six states that matter most** are Texas, Florida, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Georgia and Ohio, about 133 projected seats after 2030, all with legislature-drawn maps and a governor's race or near-even chamber in 2030 (analysis).

## 2. What DEM-2 would be worth

DEM-2 (legacy domain file): a federal mandate for congressional redistricting commissions under the Elections Clause, with a ban on mid-decade redistricting carrying a 2034 exception (for D1's House-size and Fair Representation package). D1 makes the 2033 House trifecta conditional on DEM-1 and DEM-2 being in force before the 2031 maps.

**What the published estimates say.**

| Estimate | Finding | Source (lean) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PNAS, Kenny and others (2023) | 2021 maps: partisan effects of +8.6 R and +6.2 D seats against simulated neutral maps, a net of about 2 R; geography adds a separate structural bias of about 8 seats against Democrats | [SRC-1037] (academic) |
| FiveThirtyEight (2022) | Undoing every Republican gerrymander would have been worth about 5 Democratic seats | [SRC-1038] (data journalism) |
| Brennan Center (2022) | Texas, Georgia, North Carolina and Ohio alone gave Republicans 6-9 more seats than neutral benchmarks | [SRC-1039] (center-left; advocates a ban) |
| Sam Wang (September 2026) | The 2025-26 round: a net Republican gain of about 5 seats likely (range 2-12); about 10 in 2028 | [SRC-0818] (reform advocate) |
| Post-election (November 2026) | Republican redraws gained about nothing net, or lost seats, in the wave | [SRC-1035] [SRC-1036] |

**This project's reading (analysis).**

| Who wins the 2030 races that pick the mapmakers | DEM-2's value, House seats per cycle, 2032-40 | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Republicans, broadly (the likelier case under a Democratic President) | **+5 to +10 D** | Removes Republican maps in TX, FL, NC, GA, OH, as in 2021 |
| Split | +2 to +5 D | Some Republican maps undone, some Democratic ones forgone |
| Democrats, broadly (likelier under a Republican President, `2028-republican-branch.md`) | About +4 D (PR #91's model; corrected from "about 0") | Forgoes Democratic maps in NY, IL, MD, PA, WI, but Republicans still hold the pen in FL and often NC, OH and TX, whose chambers sit well to the right of their states |

Two further effects not in the seat count: **lower variance** (no 2028-30 redraw wars) and **legitimacy** (the input docs' "who rigs the maps" contrast). The roughly 8-seat geographic bias survives commissions unless DEM-2's criteria include a partisan-fairness test (analysis from the PNAS split).

**Design questions the research raises.**
- **Effective date.** A ban effective at enactment in 2029 freezes the 2025-26 Republican maps (Texas, Florida, Tennessee, Louisiana) and any New York or Maryland 2028 maps until 2031. A bipartisan House bill (H.R. 10378, five Republican cosponsors) would take effect only after November 2032 [SRC-1041]; the Padilla-Lofgren Redistricting Reform Act applies commissions from the 2030 census [SRC-1042].
- **Legal risk, lowest to highest (analysis).** (1) The mid-decade ban: a "times, places and manner" rule squarely within Congress's power to "make or alter" (the single-member-district statute, 2 U.S.C. 2c, is the precedent); CRS notes about 70 such bills since the mid-2000s, ten in the 119th Congress from both parties [SRC-1040]. (2) Federal anti-partisan criteria: workable as statute despite *Rucho*. (3) Mandated commissions: the most exposed, since three justices now on the Court dissented when it upheld state commissions in 2015 (memory; verify), and scholars disagree on whether the Elections Clause lets Congress displace legislatures [SRC-1043] [SRC-1044]. A severability clause and a fallback (federal criteria or court maps where a state refuses) would hedge.

**Reading it (analysis).** The top six hold about 133 seats after 2030 and all combine legislative drawing with a 2030 governor's race or a near-even chamber.
