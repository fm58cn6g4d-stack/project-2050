# A stress test for D1: a Democratic president with a Republican House

Role: what a 2029 Democratic government could still do if it wins the presidency and the Senate but not the House, the case `house-model-2028.md` puts at about 11% of Democratic-president runs. It looks at the FY2029 reconciliation slot, the flagships and how the case relates to #80's options for D1. Status: draft (stage B follow-up), November 8, 2026. **Analysis only.** It proposes no change to `charter/` or `legacy/`, and D1 is Espen's to decide in #80.

**Planning premise (#80, 14:20 UTC, November 8, 2026).** Espen settled #80's House sub-choice: plan on a Democratic trifecta in 2029-30, with the House, Senate and White House all Democratic. This note's divided-government material is contingency analysis, prepared for 2030 and beyond and for a narrow 2028 miss. It is not the planning case.

## In brief

- **How likely.** With a Democratic president, the Senate is Democratic in effectively every run (99.9%; `house-model-outputs.md`, section 6). The House is Democratic in 89%. So the realistic divided case is a **Democratic president, a Democratic Senate and a Republican House**. It is concentrated in narrow presidential wins: at a neutral national vote, about 3 in 10 Democratic wins come with a Republican House.
- **Reconciliation disappears.** A reconciliation bill needs a budget resolution adopted by both chambers (2 U.S.C. 632, 641). A Republican House will not adopt one written for Democratic priorities, so **R1, R2 and R3 (D5) are all lost for the 2029-30 Congress**. D5's fallback (move R1 into R2) does not help, because R2 also needs the House. The next chance is a House won back in 2030. Under a Democratic president that is unlikely: the average 2030 environment in the chained model is about R+1.4 (`2030-2034-control.md`). In practice, reconciliation is gone until 2033.
- **The Senate alone still decides a lot:**
  - every nomination: cabinet, agencies, independent boards and the courts;
  - what reaches the president's desk from the House;
  - the Senate's own rules, so D2 can still be adopted. It would then govern nominations and anything the House passes, but it cannot make the House act.
- **The executive branch decides more than it used to.** *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 29, 2026, 6-3) overruled *Humphrey's Executor*. The president can now likely remove members of commission-style agencies at will, including the FTC, and probably the NLRB, FERC and others with executive power (CRS) [SRC-0547]. A Democratic president could therefore change those agencies' direction quickly with Senate-confirmed replacements, and so could a later Republican one.
- **Which flagships could still move.** Executive and state pieces of four of the six could move. Care: through waivers, payment rules and marketplace rules. Clean power: through agency standards, FERC and permitting rules. Work: through the NLRB and the Labor Department. Immigration: through enforcement priorities, parole, TPS and adjudication. Almost nothing of the secure floor (the child benefit, income support and paid leave need statute) or of the home flagship's money moves. No piece of DEM-1 does.

## 1. What needs the House, and what does not

| Tool | Needs the House? | In a 2029 divided government |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reconciliation (D5: R1-R3) | **Yes**: a concurrent budget resolution | Lost for 2029-30 |
| Regular-order bills (flagship statutes, DEM-1, D8's Social Security bill) | Yes | Only bipartisan bills the Republican House chooses to pass. With a narrow Republican majority, a discharge petition needs 218 signatures, so Democrats would need a handful of Republicans |
| Appropriations (FY2030 from October 1, 2029) | Yes | Negotiated. Each side has deadline leverage: shutdown risk, and continuing resolutions at the FY2029 base. The base set by the 2027-28 Democratic Congress (`4-2029-plan.md`, D9) becomes the bargaining floor |
| Debt limit | Yes | Leverage for the House, if a limit binds in 2029-30. D4's guardrails would be measured against whatever deal results |
| Expiring tax provisions | Yes | OBBBA's tips, overtime and senior deductions reportedly expire after tax year 2028 (`4-2029-plan.md`; secondary sources, check the statute). An extension fight is a bargaining chip for both sides, and the household fairness test (D3) would be applied to any deal |
| Nominations: cabinet, agencies, courts | **No**: Senate only | Fully available. At a central 56-57 seats, confirmation is the strongest power the government keeps |
| Treaties | Senate only, but two-thirds | Out of reach without Republicans |
| Executive action: rules, enforcement, waivers, grants under existing law | No | Available, within existing statutes and appropriations, and subject to *Loper Bright* and major-questions review in the courts |
| Congressional Review Act disapprovals | Both chambers, and the president's signature | Neither side can use them against the other |
| Senate rules (D2) | No | Can be adopted. It matters for nominations and for anything the House sends over |

## 2. The flagships without the House

| Flagship | What can still move (executive, state, Senate-only) | What cannot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Secure floor | Administrative fixes at SSA and IRS within existing money; benefit take-up through outreach | The child benefit, income support, paid leave, Social Security changes (D8) |
| 2. Care | Medicaid section 1115 and section 1332 waivers; marketplace rules and enrollment support within existing funds; Medicare payment rules; continued drug-price negotiation under current law. The 2027 Medicaid work requirements are statutory, so only implementation choices can soften them | Health restoration and the public option (R1); child care and care at home (R2) |
| 3. Home | HUD rules and enforcement; FHFA policy for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac; Treasury rules for housing credits; conditions on existing grants | The rental guarantee money and building money (R2); new zoning incentives that need statute |
| 4. Clean power | EPA standards; FERC transmission rules; DOE programs where authority and funds remain; permitting rules | The carbon fee and dividend and clean-energy credits (R2); the Clean Energy Standard (CLI-3) |
| 5. Work | A Democratic NLRB majority confirmed by the Senate, changing labor-law doctrine by adjudication (removal at will after *Slaughter* cuts both ways); Labor Department rules on overtime, joint employment and independent contractors; federal-contractor wage standards | Statutory labor-law reform, wage floors, sectoral standards |
| 6. Immigration | Enforcement priorities, parole programs, TPS designations, DACA, adjudication staffing within appropriations | Status for long-settled residents and new legal pathways |
| Democratic renewal | Justice Department enforcement (Section 2 is narrower after *Callais*); election-administration grants within existing law | DEM-1, DEM-2, statehood (DEM-5, DEM-6) |

The pattern is that **money and status need the House; rules, enforcement and appointments do not.** The flagships built mainly on new money or legal status (the secure floor, home, immigration status) stall. The ones with large regulatory components (care, clean power, work) keep part of their agenda.
