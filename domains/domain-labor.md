## Where things stand (world state, Oct 2026)

- **The NLRB after *Slaughter*.** *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 29, 2026, 6-3) overruled *Humphrey's Executor* for the FTC and rejected the "quasi-judicial" distinction. It strongly implies the NLRA's for-cause protection for Board members falls too, though the Court reserved questions about adjudicatory bodies (Proskauer, July 2026).
  - **What that means:** a president can now remove Board members at once, and Board law can swing sharply with each administration.
  - **For this file:** labor rights that depend on Board doctrine (bargaining orders, joint employer, captive audience) last only as long as the next administration lets them. Statute plus court enforcement is the durable instrument.
- **Minimum wage.** The federal minimum is still $7.25, unchanged since 2009. Twenty states remain at $7.25. Nineteen raised their minimums in January 2026. Rhode Island is at $16 and scheduled for $17 in 2027; Nebraska and Missouri reached $15 (NCSL, Jan 2026).
- **Heat.** OSHA has not finalized a heat standard. The day-one executive bridge (EXE-17) finalizes the rule; LAB-6 makes it statute.
- **National service.** AmeriCorps enrolls more than 200,000 people a year.
  - In 2025 the administration terminated grants and cut staff, and federal judges ordered funding and members restored in the states that sued (WSAW, June 2025).
  - The House's FY2026 bill would have cut most programs by about half. The final FY2026 Labor-HHS bill carried near-level funding (Voices for National Service, Sept 2025, updated Feb 2026).

## LAB-1. PRO Act core

**Design.** This follows the PRO Act (H.R. 20 / S. 567 lineage; Bill Book 10):
- **Penalties:** civil penalties for unfair labor practices, with personal liability for directors and officers who direct them. Today's remedy is back pay less interim earnings.
- **Strikes:** a ban on permanently replacing strikers.
- **Organizing:** a ban on mandatory captive-audience meetings, and bargaining orders when an employer's unfair labor practices taint an election (the *Cemex* standard, now in statute).
- **First contracts:** mediation, then binding arbitration, if the parties cannot agree within set deadlines.
- **Coverage:** an ABC test for employee status under the NLRA, and a joint-employer standard based on reserved or indirect control.
- **Fair-share fees:** allowed notwithstanding state right-to-work laws.

**After *Slaughter*.** Every doctrine above is written as statute, not left to Board discretion, so a future Board cannot reverse it by adjudication. LAB-2 takes enforcement out of the Board's sole hands.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LAB-1A (chosen) | Full core, including the fair-share override of right-to-work | The PRO Act as written |
| LAB-1B | Full core without the right-to-work override | Removes the single provision most likely to cost frontline and Republican-leaning votes; keeps the remedies, strike and organizing pieces |
| LAB-1C | Remedies and first-contract arbitration only | The minimum that changes organizing outcomes |

**The right-to-work question is Espen's call.** The override is the most contested provision. If D2 lands on F or C with only one or two defections to spare, B may be the version that passes.

## LAB-2. Enforcement after *Slaughter* and *Jarkesy*

**Design.**
- **Private right of action.** Workers and unions can sue in federal district court over unfair labor practices, for damages, injunctions and fees, without waiting on the General Counsel. The law then does not depend on who controls the Board.
- **Penalties in court.** Civil penalties are sought by the government in district court, with a jury available, not imposed by the Board. *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) makes administratively imposed penalties for common-law-like wrongs vulnerable (staging 4.10; CRT-10).
- **Injunctions.** Section 10(j) injunctions are available under the ordinary "reasonable cause" standard that *Starbucks v. McKinney* (2024) displaced.
- **Board structure.** The Board keeps partisan balance and staggered terms, with no removal protection, following the structural redesign in EXE-9.

**Why.** Design rule 7 (write for this Court) and design rule 2 (durable instruments). A Board-only enforcement model now flips with each president.

**Option LAB-2B.** Move unfair-labor-practice adjudication to an Article I labor court with fixed terms, on the CRT-5 immigration-court model. It is more durable but slower to build; held for the second term.

## LAB-3. Sectoral standards

**Design.** Federal industry standards boards for home care and fast food, the two sectors Bill Book 10 named where single-worksite bargaining cannot reach. Each board has worker, employer and public members and sets minimum wages, benefits and scheduling standards above the federal floor, subject to Labor Department approval. The models are California's Fast Food Council and state home-care wage boards.
- **Home care.** Most home care is Medicaid-funded, so the board's wage floor needs a matching pass-through in Medicaid home- and community-based services rates. That is a health-file dependency (HLT-4).

**Cost.** About zero to the general fund for the boards (estimate). The Medicaid rate effect of a home-care wage floor is a real cost and is **unscored**. It belongs in the health file's HLT-4 rescore.

**Options.** LAB-3B: multi-employer sectoral bargaining (union certification across a sector), which goes further than wage boards. Held as an option; it is the bigger change and has no U.S. statutory precedent outside the old NIRA codes.

## LAB-4. Living wage

**Design.** Bill Book 11's design:
- **The floor:** $17, then indexed permanently to two-thirds of the national median hourly wage.
- **Phase-in by firm size:** large firms (500+ workers or $1B+ revenue) over five years, the smallest over eight.
- **County circuit breaker:** where the scheduled floor would exceed about 60% of the local median, that county's phase-in extends automatically. A hard sunset keeps it from becoming a permanent regional tier.
- **Recession off-ramp:** the next step pauses if unemployment crosses a trigger. The floor never falls.
- **Subminimums:** the $2.13 tipped wage is phased out over seven years, and the youth wage and 14(c) certificates are ended.

It rides the labor bill (ledger C23 merged E8 into E7). The wage mandate is Byrd-barred, as the 2021 parliamentarian ruling on the $15 wage showed.

**Evidence.** CBO's 2021 analysis of $15 by 2025 found a median loss of 1.4 million jobs, 0.9 million people lifted out of poverty and a $54B ten-year deficit increase (sourced, CBO Feb 2021). $17 in the 2030s is roughly comparable in real terms. The longer runways and the county circuit breaker are aimed at CBO's job-loss channel, which is concentrated in low-wage local labor markets.

**Cost.** The mandate costs the general fund nothing directly. Its indirect budget effects (higher prices for federal purchases, lower means-tested benefits, payroll-tax changes) are **unscored**. On the 2021 CBO pattern they could be a modest net cost (estimate). This row needs a CBO estimate.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LAB-4A (chosen) | $17, then two-thirds of the median, with phase-ins and the circuit breaker | Bill Book 11 |
| LAB-4B | $15, then indexed to the median | Smaller first step; reaches most of the same workers by the mid-2030s |
| LAB-4C | Index only: raise to two-thirds of the median over ten years with no fixed dollar step | Quietest; the least legible |

## LAB-5. Small-business wage transition credit

**Design.** A refundable payroll-tax credit for small employers covering a declining share of the added wage cost during their phase-in years (Bill Book 11). It is fiscal, so it rides R2 even though the mandate needs the floor.
- **The trigger:** the credit pays out only once LAB-4 is law. A contingent effective date is ordinary in reconciliation (my reading; confirm with the Parliamentarian). If the mandate never passes, the credit never costs anything.

**Cost.** About $0.10T, range $0.05T to $0.15T (estimate; ledger E8).

**Pairing.** This is the rule-4 pairing: the wage floor travels with a visible cushion for small employers.

## LAB-6. Heat standard

**Design.** OSHA's heat illness and injury prevention standard in statute (the Asunción Valdivia Heat Illness, Injury, and Fatality Prevention Act model): required rest, shade and water above set heat-index triggers, acclimatization plans and recordkeeping, indoor and outdoor. EXE-17 finalizes the rule on day one; this makes it statute, so a later OSHA cannot rescind it.

**Second version folded here.** Staging 14.8 (climate) and Bill Book 10's "executive backstop" both carried the heat rule. One home: LAB-6 for the statute and EXE-17 for the bridge.

**Cost.** Zero to the general fund. OSHA enforcement staff are in the capacity note below.

## LAB-7. Wage theft and misclassification

**Design.**
- **Damages:** doubled liquidated damages for willful wage theft.
- **Retaliation:** a stronger anti-retaliation remedy.
- **Disclosure:** a federal pay-transparency floor.
- **Enforcement:** a Wage and Hour Division enforcement surge (Bill Book 10's "wage-theft enforcement surge").

**Options.** LAB-7B: extend the ABC test to the FLSA as well as the NLRA, so wage-and-hour coverage matches organizing coverage. It reaches gig work directly and is more contested than the NLRA test alone.

**Cost.** The enforcement staff come from the D9 line (see the capacity note below), so zero here.

## LAB-8. Apprenticeships

**Design.** One expansion of registered apprenticeship, with mandatory grants through R2:
- **Construction:** Bill Book 12's 450,000 construction apprentices, which supply HOU-3 and HOU-6.
- **Energy:** the map's energy apprenticeships, for CLI-1 and CLI-5 projects.
- **Care:** care-sector apprenticeships that build the child-care workforce FAM-2's supply grants assume.

Grants go to joint labor-management programs and community colleges, with completion bonuses.

**Cost.** About $0.03T, range $0.02T to $0.05T (estimate; neither source sizes its apprenticeship line).

**Why one line.** Three files named apprenticeships with no figure, and none counted them. One line, one figure. EDU-4's educator pipeline (domain-education.md) runs registered teacher apprenticeships; those are counted in EDU-4, not here.

## LAB-9. National service

**Design.**
- **The offer:** a paid service year open to every 18-to-26-year-old. It comes with a living stipend, health coverage, child care where needed, and on completion an education award equal to the maximum Pell grant. The award can also be used for a registered apprenticeship (LAB-8).
- **Mixing (Instruments #14):** cross-regional and cross-class placement written into the statute as a design requirement. A target share of each cohort serves outside its home region, and every team is composed across income bands. Instruments #14 warns that service placed near home "is a jobs program with a flag on it." In this file that requirement is what keeps LAB-9 a civic program sitting beside the workforce rows, not one more of them.
- **Delivery:** built on AmeriCorps (State and National, NCCC, VISTA) and run through the existing national service agency. It scales to about 250,000 new slots a year by 2034 (estimate), on top of current enrollment.
- **Placements:** priority goes to the agenda's own capacity needs:
  - child-care and pre-K classrooms (FAM-2);
  - disaster recovery and resilience (domain-climate.md);
  - public-health and preparedness work (NSC-8);
  - election administration (DEM-11).
- **Pathways:** completers get a direct path into LAB-8 apprenticeships and the care-worker pipeline that LAB-3's home-care board and FAM-2 depend on. Linking service to the labor market is the advantage of housing it in this file.

**Cost.** About $0.12T, range $0.06-0.20T (estimate). That assumes roughly $35,000 per slot all-in (stipend, benefits and education award; my estimate) and a ramp to 250,000 slots. **D4:** this is a permanent program, so it needs a pay-for from the revenue base.

**Vehicle.** The stipend, the education award and a formula grant to state service commissions are direct spending and could ride R2 (Byrd eligibility unchecked). ✔ The placement rules ride as conditions on the money.

**Labor standards.** Service members are not employees under the FLSA or the NLRA, which is how AmeriCorps works now. The statute bars displacing paid workers or filling positions vacated by layoffs or strikes (the existing AmeriCorps non-displacement rule, made stricter). Without that bar the PRO Act coalition will oppose it.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LAB-9A (fallback) | Universal offer, statutory mixing, mandatory funding in R2 | Durable; the largest cost. The R2 fallback if LAB-9D's authorization stalls; the ledger keeps its $0.12T until one passes |
| LAB-9B | Expand AmeriCorps by about 150,000 slots through appropriations | Cheaper (~$0.05T, estimate); reversible each year; fails design rule 2 |
| LAB-9C | LAB-9A without the mixing requirement | Easier to administer; loses the reason for the program (Instruments #14) |
| **LAB-9D (chosen Oct 20, Decision Brief 2)** | Seek a **bipartisan authorization** at about LAB-9B's scale first, with mixing kept, as a regular-order bill; LAB-9A's R2 money is the fallback if it stalls | The model passed service 68-32 as a bipartisan floor bill. It frees R2 room and makes the program harder to repeal, at the cost of floor days and a smaller start |

**Overlap (Oct 20).** AI-4F's transitional employment guarantee (domain-ai.md) also pays young people for a year of public-purpose work. If both are taken, AI-4F should be built as LAB-9's open-enrollment track for the unemployed, not as a second program.
