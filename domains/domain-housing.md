# Domain file: Housing (HOU)

Canonical (batch 3, with HOU-3C as Espen chose it). This file covers emergency housing aid, housing tax credits, construction capital, rental assistance, public housing repair, supply incentives and zoning conditions, and a social-housing pilot. It replaces Bill Book 12, ledger row E6 and staging inventory row 10.13 on substance.

**Option from the Talarico stress test.** One option row, **HOU-9, a federal builder of last resort**, comes from the Talarico scenario model's central housing finding (frameworks/scenario-stress-test-talarico-2029-2037.md): in that model housing "was never a money problem," conditional grants failed in exactly the states that most needed units, and only a direct federal construction institution moved the number.

All figures are ten-year totals for 2030-39 in trillions unless marked. Basis tags: **sourced**, **scaled** (a cited figure moved to 2030-39 or to this program's size) and **estimate** (mine). Survival at 51 (Dec. D1): ✔ survives, ◐ needs a D2 majority-precedent path, ✖ needs 60, 67 or Republican votes.

## Where things stand (world state, Oct 2026)

- **ROAD to Housing Act.** The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act (H.R. 6644) became law on July 11, 2026 without the President's signature (Orrick; Argus).
  - **What it does:** dozens of supply, cost, FHA and manufactured-housing provisions, plus a limit on large institutional investors buying single-family homes. The limit takes effect 180 days after enactment (about January 2027) and is repealed 15 years later.
  - **What it lacks:** per Bill Book 12, it appropriated no money for HUD to carry out its programs. Its specific zoning and "Build Now" provisions are not yet mapped in this project and should be read before HOU-6 is drafted.
- **LIHTC.** OBBBA made a 12% increase in 9% credit allocations permanent from 2026 and permanently cut the bond-financing test from 50% to 25% of costs.
  - **The tax-credit core is partly done:** Novogradac estimates about 80,000 more rentals from the allocation increase over 2026-35, and the bond-test change could finance more than a million (Williams Mullen).
- **Vouchers.** About 2.3 million households held Housing Choice Vouchers in September 2025 (NLIHC). FY2026 tenant-based rental assistance was $38.4B, including $34.9B for renewals (NLIHC, FY26 final). That works out to about **$15,200 per voucher-year** (scaled).
- **Public housing.** The FY2026 Capital Fund is $3.2B, down $210M. The repair backlog estimate runs as high as $169B (NLIHC); Bill Book 12 used $80B.

**Design.** $85B of rental assistance and construction gap financing (Bill Book 12), spent over 2030-31 through state housing finance agencies and the Emergency Rental Assistance infrastructure. It carries a declared housing emergency in the findings, and the program is named in statute with attribution on every payment (legibility rule).

## HOU-2. Housing tax credits

**Design.** These build on what OBBBA already did:
- **LIHTC:** a further phased allocation increase (toward the Affordable Housing Credit Improvement Act's 50%), and a 50% basis boost for units reserved for extremely-low-income tenants.
- **Middle-income credit:** a credit for rentals at 60-100% of area median income, where LIHTC does not reach.
- **Neighborhood Homes credit:** covers the gap between rehab cost and sale price for owner-occupied homes in distressed neighborhoods (Neighborhood Homes Investment Act model).

**Cost.** About $0.15T, range $0.10T to $0.20T (estimate). This needs a JCT score. Part of the AHCIA's cost was enacted by OBBBA, so older AHCIA scores overstate what remains. The ledger never sized this row separately, which is why the file runs above E6.

## HOU-3. Construction capital

**Chosen (Espen): HOU-3C, two channels.** The first is about $72B of capitalization grants to state housing revolving funds, sized to what states can stand up and lend within the window, on the clean water and drinking water state revolving fund model. The second is **$300B of federal direct-loan commitments** for mixed-income and social housing. Together the two channels put about $386B behind construction (with the states' 20% match), against Bill Book 12's $250B. They score at about **$0.075T**, against $0.25T for an all-grant fund (HOU-3A).

### Channel 1: state housing revolving funds (grants)

**Design.**
- **Capitalization grants** go by formula (renter cost burden and construction costs) to a revolving fund in each state, run by its housing finance agency. Like the water funds, states must match 20%.
- **Uses:** the funds lend construction and permanent financing for affordable and mixed-income rentals. Repayments stay in the state fund and are relent indefinitely.
- **Additional subsidization:** at least 30% of each year's grant must go out as forgivable loans or grants for units reserved for extremely-low-income households. These are the projects that cannot carry debt. It is the housing version of the water funds' "additional subsidization."
- **Mandatory funding in R2**, so the money is not subject to annual appropriations. No part of it can be redirected to earmarks. Since 2021, more than $3.7B has been taken from the water funds for earmarks (Cramer), and in FY2026 earmarks took 54% of clean-water and 64% of drinking-water appropriations (CRS). This fund should not repeat that.
- **Durability terms:** EXE-3's anti-impoundment protections apply (termination only for cause, deemed apportionment).

**How big states can go.**
- **The benchmark.** The water funds are the best evidence of what states can absorb. Under the 2021 infrastructure law, they took about $10.2B a year in supplemental capitalization on top of about $2.8B a year in regular appropriations, roughly **$13B a year** across both funds (CRS, FY2025-26 figures). Those are mature programs: the clean water fund dates to 1987 and the drinking water fund to 1996.
- **Housing starts from less.** Most states have no housing revolving fund yet. State housing finance agencies already underwrite LIHTC and HOME deals, but they have not run a revolving loan book at this scale.
- **The ramp (estimate):** $3B in FY2030 while states write intended-use plans and staff up, $5B in FY2031, then $8B a year from FY2032 through FY2039. The peak is about 60% of the water funds' recent level, reached in year three. Total: **about $72B**, plus about $14B of state match.
- **Pace risk.** Emergency Rental Assistance in 2021 showed that new state programs start slowly (not re-checked). The ramp assumes the same.

**Cost.** Capitalization grants score as outlays as states draw them. Draws lag awards by two to four years, so about 80-100% of the $72B outlays inside 2030-39. That is **about $0.06T, range $0.055T to $0.07T** (estimate). This is time-limited capital, so ledger option C21 (accepted, Decision Brief 1) finances it by borrowing inside the D4-B investment envelope.

### Channel 2: federal direct loans

**Design.**
- **A HUD Housing Construction Loan program**, funded through the Federal Financing Bank, with **$300B of loan commitments over 2030-39**.
- **Ramp (estimate):** $10B in 2030, $20B in 2031, $30B in 2032, then about $34B a year through 2039. The constraint is origination capacity, not budget cost.
- **Who borrows:** mixed-income and social housing (HOU-8 is the first borrower), public housing authorities' new development (paired with HOU-5B's Faircloth repeal), and nonprofit and LIHTC projects that need construction-to-permanent financing. Loans are priced at about Treasury rates plus a margin that covers administration (estimate: 0.5-1 point).
- **Origination through state agencies.** State housing finance agencies originate and service loans under a risk-share with HUD, on the HUD-HFA Risk-Sharing model (sec. 542(c)). The federal government sets the terms and holds most of the risk, but needs far fewer underwriters of its own. That answers the capacity problem with a federal lender.

**Cost.** Under the Federal Credit Reform Act, only the subsidy cost scores: the expected present-value loss. Assuming a subsidy rate of 2-10% (mid 5%, estimate), $300B of commitments costs **about $0.015T, range $0.006T to $0.03T**.
- **Needs a CBO credit-subsidy estimate.** CBO's fair-value estimate, which prices market risk, would be higher, and opponents will cite it. The honest answer: the money is lent, not spent, and comes back.
- **Loss risk is federal.** The risk-share puts part of it on state agencies, which also limits the federal-loss headline.

## HOU-5. Public housing repair

**Design.** The Capital Fund rises from $3.2B (FY2026) to about $9B a year for ten years (Bill Book's $9.2B target), as mandatory funding in R2. It sunsets after ten years, since it is a backlog program. Option HOU-5B repeals the Faircloth limit, which caps the number of public housing units, so new public units can be built.

**Cost.** About $0.06T, range $0.05T to $0.07T (scaled from the FY2026 level). Against a backlog of $80B to $169B, ten years at this level closes roughly a third to two-thirds of it.

**D4 reading.** A time-limited repair program is time-limited capital, so it is financed by borrowing inside the D4-B investment envelope (ledger C31, accepted in Decision Brief 1), as C30 does for Moving America.

## HOU-6. Housing Supply Fund and zoning conditions

**Design.**
- **The fund (R2, ✔).** A competitive Housing Supply Fund rewards jurisdictions that adopt by-right multifamily near transit and jobs, legalize ADUs, cut parking minimums and shorten permit timelines. Awards scale with units actually permitted.
- **Conditions (FL, ◐; 3 days per the ledger).** A share of *new* federal transportation money (Moving America's 2031 authorization) and *new* CDBG increments are conditioned on the same reforms.

**Write for this Court.** Conditions attach to new and incremental money only, never to existing base grants. That keeps them inside *NFIB v. Sebelius*'s coercion limit (staging 2.6). Federal dollars can incentivize zoning but not command it, so the fund does the work and the conditions add pressure.

**Coordination.** ROAD's own supply-incentive provisions should be mapped first so HOU-6 builds on them rather than duplicating them. The transportation file should carry the matching condition in its 2031 authorization.

**Cost.** About $0.03T, range $0.02T to $0.05T (estimate; Bill Book 12 does not size the fund).

## HOU-7. Institutional investor limit made permanent

**Design.** ROAD limits large institutional investors' purchases of single-family homes. The limit starts about January 2027 and repeals itself about 15 years later. HOU-7 removes the sunset and adds reporting so the limit can be measured. It rides HOU-6's floor bill.

**Why.** Under the durability rule, the project avoids sunsets on permanent policy. The limit is a rule, not a program, so it costs nothing. Option HOU-7B leaves ROAD as enacted and revisits the limit in the 2030s with data.

## HOU-8. Social housing pilot

**Design.** A mixed-income public development pilot (Bill Book 12): public or nonprofit ownership, with rents set to cover operating and debt costs across mixed incomes. It is financed by HOU-3's federal direct loans plus a small federal equity grant.

**Cost.** About $0.01T (estimate).

**Fit with HOU-3.** This pilot is the first borrower in HOU-3's direct-loan channel. If it works, it is the model for scaling social housing through that channel in the 2030s.

## HOU-9. Federal builder of last resort (option)

**Why it is here.** The Talarico scenario model's housing record is the stress test this file most needs: $95B of production money and a social housing authority delivered about 1.9M units by 2050 against a 4.5M shortfall, rents flat in 23 states and not in 27 (scenario figures). Its diagnosis: the binding constraints were preemption limits, municipal permitting, construction labor competing with the transmission build-out, and materials, not money; and "the instrument used everywhere, conditional grants to states, fails precisely in the states that most need the thing." HOU-3, HOU-6 and HOU-8 are the same instrument family (state channels, competitive grants, conditions on new money), so the project's base case carries the same exposure.

**Design.** A federal housing construction corporation (the scenario's "FHIC," on the TVA and federal-transmission-corporation model in CLI-10):
- **Borrowing authority** through the Federal Financing Bank, scored at subsidy cost like HOU-3's loan channel, with rents set to cover operating and debt costs (HOU-8's model at scale).
- **Land:** builds first on federal land and land the corporation controls (surplus federal property, transit-agency and Postal Service land by agreement), where local permitting does not apply to federal construction.
- **Workforce:** a standing construction workforce drawn from LAB-8 apprenticeships and LAB-9 national service, so it does not bid against the transmission build-out for the same trades.
- **Trigger:** operates in metros with rental vacancy below about 4% where state and local channels have not met production targets, which keeps it a backstop rather than a replacement.

**The legal limit the scenario found.** In the model's second-term version, federal preemption of local permitting was upheld only for federally funded and constructed housing (about 10% of the market) and eminent domain for housing was struck on public-use grounds, so the corporation could build on land it controlled but could not assemble parcels; its target fell from 800,000 to about 420,000 units by 2045 (scenario figures). Write for this Court: rely on federal land and voluntary acquisition, not condemnation, and confine preemption to federal construction.

**Cost.** Unscored. Capital is lent and repaid (subsidy cost only); operating and equity costs are the real claim. The scenario's second-term version ran $90B a year of build authority (scenario figure), far larger than anything in this file.

**Vehicle and timing.** Creating the corporation needs the floor (◐); its capitalization and loan authority could ride a reconciliation bill (✔). The scenario's lesson on timing is that waiting a term to build the institution cost most of its effect; under D1, a first-Congress authorization with a small start and a 2033-36 scale-up is the version that fits.

| HOU-9B | Fold into HOU-8: let the social housing pilot's sponsor become the federal builder if the pilot works | Cheapest; slowest |

## Survival at 51 (D1 stress test)

Every money row rides R2 and survives at 51. Only HOU-6's conditions on existing programs and HOU-7 need the floor. If D2 lands on G (carve-out only), the fund still moves; the conditions and the sunset repeal wait for a later rules change. HOU-9's corporation needs the floor; its capital does not.

**Stress test (Talarico model).** Transfers and rules are delivered fast; capacity and construction late and short. This file's chosen rows are mostly money through state channels, so they score well on durability and poorly on the construction test. HOU-9 is the option that answers it.

## Accepted housing-security development direction, October 26

Develop a guarantee for extremely-low-income renters alongside public/nonprofit acquisition, repair and construction, durable affordability and a lawful federal sponsor/backstop where state participation fails. The guarantee should replace waiting-list luck for its defined population. HOU-4D is the bounded current spending step; it is not the complete guarantee’s price or adoption of HOU-4C’s unpriced eligibility proxy. Resolve eligibility, supply, federal/state responsibilities and funding in the joint development. Preserve existing HOU-4A benefits while doing so.
