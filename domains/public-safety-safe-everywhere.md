# Safe Everywhere: A Democratic Public Safety Platform

Oct 11, 2026 · @espen

## The argument in brief

Democrats should run on a simple promise: every violent crime gets solved, every neighborhood gets real policing and real prevention, and nobody is threatened out of public life. This document sets out the case its advocates would make, the evidence behind each plank, and the strongest objections.

Three facts shape the platform.

- **Crime is at a historic low, and nobody believes Democrats did it.** The 2025 murder rate was 4.1 per 100,000, the lowest on record. Voters still trust Republicans more on crime by double digits.
- **Too many crimes go unpunished.** Even in a record year, about three in ten murders and more than half of violent crimes ended with no arrest.
- **Political violence is moving the other way.** Capitol Police threat cases rose 58% in 2025, and roughly one in five local officials reports recent threats.

The platform that follows from those facts has one organizing idea: **certainty over severity**. Safety comes from offenders expecting to be caught, from a small number of high-risk people and places getting sustained attention, and from public servants being protected. It does not come from longer sentences or soldiers on street corners.

The platform has nine planks: solve the crime; staff and reform police; focus on the few people and places that drive violence; fix dangerous places; keep guns from dangerous people; restore public order with treatment attached; stand with victims; protect democracy's front line; and stop fraud, cybercrime and identity theft.

## What the numbers say

Ordinary crime is falling fast while violence aimed at political life is rising. A serious platform has to hold both facts at once.

Nobody can fully explain the drop. CCJ notes it is happening in cities with different politics, policing strategies and levels of federal enforcement, which points to large national forces as well as local policy. That matters for the platform: no party can honestly claim sole credit, and nobody can promise the decline will continue by itself.

### The accountability gap

The FBI's reported homicide clearance rate reached 69% in 2025, up from 52% in 2022, according to the [CCJ's homicide report](https://counciloncj.org/whats-driving-the-homicide-decline/). That still leaves about three in ten killings unsolved. For violent crime overall, [about 47% of cases were cleared and about 17% of property crimes](https://kmph.com/news/nation-world/violent-crime-rates-in-2025-see-largest-decrease-since-1937-fbi-property-crime-murder-manslaughter-rape-robbery-kash-patel).

Non-fatal shootings are worse. A [Third Way memo](https://www.thirdway.org/memo/the-case-to-improve-clearance-rates.pdf) reports that some cities solve fewer than 20% of them. The national homicide clearance rate once exceeded 90%, the same memo notes. Massachusetts still tops 90%, [per R Street](https://www.rstreet.org/commentary/the-victim-act-returns-a-federal-answer-to-a-state-level-movement-on-unsolved-violent-crime/).

## Why crime is falling

No single cause has been identified. The best-supported account is that the pandemic's disruption ended while money and attention were concentrated on the small group of people most likely to shoot or be shot.

That reading comes from a [Council on Criminal Justice summit](https://counciloncj.org/whats-driving-the-homicide-decline/) in September 2026. Forty-eight scholars from across disciplines and viewpoints rated 48 possible explanations for the 2022 to 2026 homicide decline. The results are professional judgments, not causal findings.

| Explanation | Rated a major or limited contributor | Named a top contributor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expanded community violence intervention | 91% | 64% |
| Easing of pandemic social and economic disruption | 83% | 51% |
| Increased use of focused deterrence | 83% | 43% |
| Increased local government capacity | 75% | 40% |
| More public safety resources relative to demand | 68% | 28% |
| Increased proactive policing | 68% | 23% |
| Less unstructured socializing among youth | 60% | 19% |

**The pandemic story.** The table combines "major" and "limited" ratings. Counting "major" ratings alone, easing disruption leads: 57% of scholars called it a major contributor, and no other factor passed 50%. [Brookings](https://brookings.edu/articles/why-did-u-s-homicides-spike-in-2020-and-then-decline-rapidly-in-2023-and-2024) tied the 2020 spike to young men pushed out of work and school in low-income neighborhoods. State and local governments lost about 1.25 million jobs in spring 2020, and local government employment did not recover until September 2023. Homicide tracked that collapse and recovery closely, though the link has not been tested directly.

**The focus story.** Intervention programs and focused deterrence both aim at the networks where shootings concentrate, and both expanded or restarted just before the decline. The caveat is real: program evaluations of violence intervention remain mixed. Several scholars said it mattered in a handful of cities that drove the national numbers, not everywhere.

**The slow-moving story.** Young people drink less, spend less unsupervised time with peers and offend less than earlier generations. Arrests in 2025 were about 25% below 2019. A justice system built for more demand now has more capacity per case.

**Where the experts split.**

- Police staffing: 51% saw a contribution, 47% saw little or none. Staffing in large cities fell about 6% from 2019 to 2023, which past research would tie to only a 3% to 4% change in homicide.
- Federal gun policy: 43% against 55%.
- Trauma care: 45% against 53%.
- Cleaning, greening and lighting: 42% against 47%. Scholars accept that it works where done, but doubt it was done widely enough.

**What got little support.** Fewer than a quarter of scholars credited 26 of the 48 factors. These include National Guard deployments, immigration enforcement, encampment removals, increased pretrial detention and increased incarceration. The report estimates added confinement could explain 0.1% to 3.1% of the drop.

**Clearance.** Scholars rated rising clearance a modest contributor at most, since fewer murders also make each one easier to solve.

**Beyond homicide.** The summit covered homicide only, and research on other offenses is thinner. The 2020 spike was concentrated in homicide and serious assault, while property crime continued a decline that began decades ago. Some scholars suggest robbery is falling partly because people carry less cash and theft has moved online, though that idea drew little support as an explanation for homicide.

### What this means for the platform

- **It supports the focus planks.** The explanations with the most support are the ones plank 3 and the Safety Compacts are built on: concentrated attention and local capacity.
- **It warns against complacency.** Recovery from the pandemic is a one-time gain. Nothing here suggests the decline continues automatically.
- **It raises the stakes of the funding cuts.** Much of the intervention work and local capacity was paid for with $350 billion in pandemic recovery aid that was temporary, on top of the $811 million in cancelled grants.
- **It tempers two claims.** Police staffing and place-based fixes have good evidence where used, but scholars doubt they drove this decline. Murder fell by half while police headcount was flat, and pooled studies put greening's effect near 5% to 6%. The case for both is what they could do at scale, so planks 2 and 4 should promise specific deployments and modest gains.
- **It does not settle the right's objection.** Proactive policing drew real support, and scholars from the Manhattan Institute argued for incapacitation. The summit's own caution applies: a factor that contributed little recently may still be worth using.

## Why the politics do not track the numbers

Public alarm about crime is easing, but the Democratic trust deficit is not. That distinction changes what the platform has to do.

**Concern is falling.** In Gallup's October 2025 poll, [49% said crime was rising nationally](https://news.gallup.com/poll/697124/crime-seen-less-serious-second-straight-year.aspx), down from 64% in 2024 and 77% in 2023, the lowest reading since 2001. So the premise that crime is growing more salient needs a caveat: general concern has dropped, while political violence has become far more visible.

**Perception follows party.** Gallup recorded a 36-point drop in one year in Republicans' belief that crime was rising, after their party took the White House. The [Marshall Project's review](https://www.themarshallproject.org/2025/11/19/gallup-crime-perceptions-political-polarization) of 25 years of Gallup data finds people feel safer when their own party holds the presidency.

**The trust gap persists anyway.** A 2025 Washington Post/Ipsos poll put Republicans ahead on crime by 22 points, up from 13 in 2022, [as reported by CNN's Harry Enten](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/cnn-data-guru-grim-warning-211114030.html). Republicans have led on this question for most of the last four decades.

Three explanations fit the evidence, and each implies a task.

1. **Brand.** Voters remember 2020. Democrats are associated with "defund," lenient prosecutors and reluctance to say that offenders should face consequences. The task is a clear, repeated position, not a statistics lecture.
2. **Disorder.** Murder is rare and far away for most voters. Shoplifting, open drug use, encampments and erratic behavior on transit are what people see. Shoplifting is the one offense still above 2019 levels.
3. **Impunity.** When most burglaries and half of violent crimes produce no arrest, "crime is down" sounds like an excuse. People judge safety by whether the system responds when something happens to them.

The opening for Democrats is that the Republican answer is weak on its own terms. The administration [cancelled about $811 million in public safety grants in April 2025](https://robinkelly.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/reps-kelly-mcbath-brown-frost-lead-letter-trump-cancelled-funds), including violence intervention and victim services. A [Niskanen Center analysis](https://www.gpb.org/node/1489661) found the National Guard deployment in Washington cut petty property crime but had no effect on violent crime, at high cost.

## What works and what does not

The strongest evidence favors police used precisely and concentrated attention on high-risk people and places, with cheap physical fixes as a modest complement. The weakest favors blanket severity and shows of force. Rows marked \* rest on published research I did not re-open today; treat those figures as approximate.

### Strong evidence

| Approach | What the research shows |
| --- | --- |
| Police staffing | Each added officer prevents roughly 0.06 to 0.1 homicides a year, with per-capita benefits twice as large for Black victims. The same study finds 7 to 22 more low-level arrests per officer. [Chalfin et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/28202.html) |
| Focused deterrence | Direct warnings plus services for the small groups driving shootings cut crime 23% across studies, and 16% in the nine randomized trials. [Braga, Turchan and Weisburd](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/focused-deterrence-can-reduce-crime-a-systematic-review-of-random/) |
| Cleaning and greening vacant lots | A randomized trial of 541 Philadelphia lots found up to 29% less gun violence nearby in poor neighborhoods. Pooled across eight studies, the effect is about 5% to 6%. [Columbia Mailman School](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/public-health-now/news/how-reduce-crime-and-gun-violence-and-stabilize-neighborhoods-randomized-controlled-study) |
| Street lighting | Randomly assigned lighting in New York public housing cut outdoor night-time crime about 36%. A wider review found lower total crime but no significant drop in violent crime. [Branas summary of Chalfin 2019](https://dvrpc.org/Committees/HCTF/Presentations/2022-11.pdf) |
| Hot-spot policing\* | Crime concentrates on a small share of blocks. Extra patrol there reduces it without simply moving it next door. |
| Behavioral programs for teenagers\* | Chicago's Becoming a Man cut violent-crime arrests by close to half in randomized trials. Summer youth jobs showed a similar drop in violent-crime arrests. |
| Certainty of being caught\* | Decades of deterrence research find the chance of arrest deters far more than the length of the sentence. |

### Promising but uneven

| Approach | What the research shows |
| --- | --- |
| Intensive intervention for the highest-risk men | READI Chicago's randomized trial found no significant effect on its main violence index. Shooting and homicide arrests fell 65%, short of significance, with estimated benefits of 4 to 18 times cost. [Bhatt et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10898100/) |
| Street outreach and violence interrupters\* | Results differ by city and by how well the program is run. Some sites show fewer shootings, some show nothing, a few show increases. |
| Dedicated shooting investigation teams | Saint Paul raised its non-fatal shooting clearance rate from 37% to 71% in 2024. Evaluations are still few. [R Street](https://www.rstreet.org/commentary/the-victim-act-returns-a-federal-answer-to-a-state-level-movement-on-unsolved-violent-crime/) |
| Gun purchaser licensing and red-flag laws\* | Studies link permit-to-purchase laws to fewer gun homicides and suicides. Effect sizes are disputed. |
| Treatment access\* | Expanding insurance coverage and drug treatment capacity is associated with lower crime in several quasi-experimental studies. |

### Weak or counterproductive

| Approach | What the research shows |
| --- | --- |
| Military deployments | The Guard in Washington reduced petty property crime only, with no effect on violent crime. [Niskanen via NPR](https://www.gpb.org/node/1489661) |
| Longer sentences as a deterrent\* | Added years at the back end of a sentence deter little. Incapacitation is real but its returns shrink quickly as offenders age. |
| Mass stops of pedestrians\* | Very low yield of weapons, with lasting damage to cooperation in the neighborhoods that most need cases solved. |
| Cutting police budgets | The staffing research above implies more homicides, concentrated among Black victims. |
| Scared Straight and similar youth programs\* | Evaluations find they increase offending. |

Two caveats apply to the whole section. Most programs work only when run well, and many cities run them badly. And the current decline is larger than any one of these approaches can explain.

## What serious thinkers say

The researchers disagree about emphasis, but they converge on precision: violence is concentrated, so the response should be too. Characterizations below are my summaries of their published work.

| School | Voices | Core claim | What the platform takes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Thomas Abt, [Council on Criminal Justice](https://counciloncj.org/10-essential-actions/) | A few people and places drive most shootings. Cities need one plan that is focused, balanced between enforcement and help, and seen as fair. | The structure of planks 3 and 4 |
| Situations | [Jens Ludwig](https://newbooksnetwork.com/jens-ludwig-unforgiving-places-the-unexpected-origins-of-american-gun-violence-u-chicago-press-2025), University of Chicago Crime Lab | Most shootings are arguments that escalate in a ten-minute window. Change the setting and the split-second thinking, and violence falls without fixing poverty first. | Behavioral programs, eyes on the street |
| Police work | Aaron Chalfin, Jeff Asher | Officers prevent murders, and solved cases prevent more. Communities with the most violence are over-policed for small things and under-policed for serious ones. | Planks 1 and 2 |
| Impunity | Jill Leovy, Mark Kleiman | The core failure is that killing in poor Black neighborhoods often goes unpunished. Swift and certain consequences beat severe and random ones. | The organizing idea |
| Community | Patrick Sharkey | Local nonprofits and residents were a real part of the 1990s crime drop. Fund them as permanent institutions. | Plank 3 funding rules |
| What survives testing | Jennifer Doleac | Run experiments and keep what works. Intuition is a poor guide to which leniency and which toughness reduce reoffending. | Evaluation requirements |
| Incapacitation | Rafael Mangual, Manhattan Institute | A small group of repeat offenders commits a large share of violence. Releasing them early costs lives. | Pretrial detention for the dangerous |
| Political violence | Rachel Kleinfeld, Robert Pape, [Shannon Hiller](https://spia.princeton.edu/news/new-data-princeton-spia-initiative-shows-political-violence-us-rise) | Violence rises when leaders excuse it and falls when their own side condemns it. Monitoring and local preparation reduce risk. | Plank 8 |

**Where they agree.** Concentrate resources. Raise the odds of being caught. Measure results and drop programs that fail. Treat legitimacy as a tool for solving crime, because witnesses talk to police they trust.

**Where they split.** How much prison is worth its cost. Whether community programs can scale. How far gun laws matter relative to enforcement. The platform takes a side on each and says so in the objections section.

## The platform

Nine planks follow. Targets and dollar levels are proposals for debate, not sourced estimates.

The federal pieces would cost about $12.1 billion a year. The section-by-section bill summary is in Bill summary and cost.

### 1. Solve the crime

The headline promise: if you are shot, robbed or assaulted, someone is caught.

- Set national goals of clearing 80% of murders and 50% of non-fatal shootings by 2032.
- Pass the bipartisan [VICTIM Act](https://www.booker.senate.gov/news/press/booker-kennedy-reintroduce-bipartisan-bill-to-give-law-enforcement-more-tools-to-solve-crimes) (Booker and Kennedy), which authorizes $60 million a year for detectives, forensics and victim services. Then fund it at several times that level.
- Put a dedicated non-fatal shooting team in every large city, on the Saint Paul model.
- Clear DNA and ballistics backlogs so evidence comes back in days.
- Fund witness protection and relocation, since fear of retaliation is why many cases die.
- Publish every agency's clearance rates quarterly.

### 2. Staff police departments and hold them to professional standards

- Offer federal hiring grants to rebuild depleted departments, with priority for investigators and for patrol in the highest-violence neighborhoods.
- Move work that does not need a badge to trained civilians: mental health crisis calls, report-taking, traffic collisions.
- Pair funding with standards: a national decertification registry, body cameras, and public use-of-force data.
- Track low-level arrests alongside violent crime, so added officers go toward serious cases.
- Keep soldiers out of routine policing.

**Liability and licensing.** The city pays the victim, and the officer answers for the conduct.

- Make the employing agency liable when an officer violates someone's constitutional rights, and remove qualified immunity as a defense in those suits. Officers acting within policy stay indemnified.
- Trigger an independent disciplinary review after any court finding or settlement, with a deadline and a published outcome.
- Send findings to the state licensing board, which can order retraining, suspension or decertification.
- Enter decertifications in the national registry, and require a check before any hire.
- Limit arbitration rules and contract clauses that routinely reverse firings or erase records.\*
- Guarantee due process: clear standards, a hearing and an appeal.

Departments control training and discipline, so the cost belongs with them. Colorado and New Mexico have passed state versions, and evidence on their effects is still scarce.\*

### 3. Focus on the few people and places that drive violence

- Require every high-violence city receiving federal safety funds to adopt a written plan built on the [CCJ's ten actions](https://counciloncj.org/10-essential-actions/), starting with an analysis of who is shooting whom and where.
- Fund focused deterrence partnerships between police, prosecutors, service providers and community leaders.
- Restore the $811 million in cancelled grants and put violence intervention on multi-year funding.
- Tie that funding to independent evaluation. Programs that do not reduce shootings lose it.
- Scale behavioral programs and summer jobs for the highest-risk teenagers.

### 4. Fix dangerous places

- Create a Safe Blocks fund for lighting, vacant-lot cleanup and abandoned-building repair on the blocks with the most shootings.
- Staff and light transit stations, and keep them clean.
- Give residents a fast way to report broken lights and abandoned property, with a public response time.

### 5. Keep guns from dangerous people

- Require background checks on all sales.
- Reward states that adopt purchaser licensing and red-flag laws with due-process protections.
- Fund gun tracing and crime-gun intelligence centers, and prosecute traffickers and straw buyers.
- Crack down on conversion devices and untraceable guns.
- Make consequences for illegal carrying by people with violent records quick and predictable.

### 6. Restore public order, with treatment attached

- Enforce the rules of shared spaces consistently: theft, fare evasion, open drug use, harassment.
- Make the first response a citation and a path into treatment. Make repeat offending carry real consequences.
- Fund regional task forces against organized retail theft.
- Pay for treatment beds, crisis teams and supervised outpatient care for serious mental illness.
- On encampments, offer shelter first and then enforce.

### 7. Swift courts and real support for victims

- Fund prosecutors, public defenders and judges to cut the time from arrest to trial.
- Base pretrial detention on danger, not wealth. Judges should be able to hold people who pose a clear threat and release those who do not.
- Pay victim compensation within 30 days and stabilize the federal Crime Victims Fund.
- Expand trauma recovery centers.
- Send people home from prison with an ID, treatment and a job lead, because most of them do come home.

### 8. Protect democracy's front line

The promise: no one in either party should have to choose between public service and their family's safety. More than 500,000 Americans hold state and local office, and most have no security at all.

States are already acting, mostly since the Hortman and Kirk killings. Eleven states now have laws letting candidates spend campaign funds on security, [the AP reports](https://www.boston25news.com/news/national/locked-capitol-doors/624JBOWC5E7WHJPUK6LN67ZGRU/). Minnesota [approved $52 million](https://ground.news/article/one-year-later-minnesota-lawmakers-reflect-on-lasting-impacts-of-june-14), a State Patrol protection unit, and up to $4,500 per member for home security. Capitol Police tripled their agreements with local agencies in 2025, to more than 350.

**Security for people who serve**

- Offer federal matching grants so every state can run a threat-assessment unit and pay for basic home security for legislators, local officials, judges and election workers.
- Allow campaign funds to be spent on security in every state.
- Fund security at town halls and public meetings, so officials keep showing up in person.

**Privacy**

- Keep officials' and election workers' home addresses out of public filings by default.
- Bar data brokers from selling those addresses, extending the protection federal judges received in 2022.\*

**Investigation and prosecution**

- Assign a threats prosecutor in every U.S. Attorney's office and state attorney general's office.
- Build one reporting channel that a school board member can use as easily as a senator.
- Offer states a model threat statute that fits the Supreme Court's 2023 standard for true threats.\*
- Enforce bans on armed intimidation at polling places and public meetings.

**Early warning**

- Restore federal violence-prevention and election-security funding that [Bridging Divides reports](https://spia.princeton.edu/news/new-data-princeton-spia-initiative-shows-political-violence-us-rise) has been cut.
- Fund independent tracking of threats against local officials, which researchers say is undercounted.

**One standard for both sides**

- Condemn violence against opponents as loudly as violence against allies. Capitol Police say [lowering violent rhetoric](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/threats-congress-spiked-2025-rose-third-year-row-capitol-police-say-rcna256259) is among the best ways to reduce threats.
- Pledge no pardons or clemency for political violence, whoever commits it.
- Withdraw party support from candidates who endorse or excuse it.

This plank is where Democrats can ask Republicans to sign on line by line. The threat data show both parties are targets.

\*From memory of the statute and ruling; confirm before publishing.

### 9. Stop fraud, cybercrime and identity theft

The promise: your savings and your identity get the same protection as your home. This is the one large category of crime that is rising, and the one the headline crime statistics mostly leave out.

| Measure | Latest figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internet crime losses reported to the FBI, 2025 | $20.9 billion, up 26% in a year and up from $4.2 billion in 2020 | [CyberScoop on the FBI report](https://cyberscoop.com/fbi-internet-crime-complaint-center-annual-cybercrime-report/) |
| Complaints to the FBI, 2025 | 1,008,597, the first year above one million | [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/fbi-internet-crime-complaint-center-annual-cybercrime-report/) |
| Fraud losses reported to the FTC, 2025 | $15.9 billion from 3 million reports | [FTC testimony](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-testifies-joint-economic-committee-agencys-efforts-combat-fraud) |
| FTC estimate of true fraud cost, 2024 | As high as $195.9 billion, since most victims never report | [PYMNTS on the testimony](https://www.pymnts.com/fraud-prevention/2026/consumer-fraud-losses-quintupled-since-2020-ftc-says/) |
| Losses among people 60 and older, 2025 | $7.75 billion, about 37% of the FBI total | [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/fbi-internet-crime-complaint-center-annual-cybercrime-report/) |
| Identity theft reports to the FTC, 2025 | More than 1.3 million | [Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/identity-theft-statistics/) |
| Complaints involving AI, 2025 | 22,364, with $893 million in losses | [Malwarebytes on the FBI report](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/scams/2026/06/americans-lost-nearly-900-million-to-ai-powered-scams-fbi-says) |

Investment scams are the largest category at $8.65 billion, and cryptocurrency was the payment channel in $11.4 billion of losses. The two figures overlap.

**Why this needs its own plank**

The tools that work on street crime mostly do not reach this one.

- **The offenders are far away and organized.** Much of the fraud is run from scam compounds in Southeast Asia, which the Treasury estimates took at least $10 billion from Americans in 2024.
- **Local police cannot follow the money.** A victim who walks into a precinct usually meets a department with no jurisdiction and no investigators for it.
- **AI removes the tells.** Voice cloning, deepfake video and fluent scripted messages make impersonating a relative, a bank or an official cheap and convincing.
- **Speed decides recovery.** The FBI's recovery team froze $679 million of $1.16 billion in attempted thefts it acted on in 2025, a 58% success rate. That works only when victims report within days.

So the strategy leans on prevention at chokepoints. Nearly every scam passes through a phone carrier, an online platform, and a bank or crypto exchange.

**Close the channels**

- Require banks and payment apps to flag and briefly hold high-risk first-time transfers, and to share scam intelligence with each other.
- Make institutions share the loss when they miss clear warning signs. Britain requires banks to reimburse scam victims up to £85,000, and Australia's framework covers banks, phone companies and platforms, [as summarized here](https://www.outseer.com/blog/us-senators-take-action-on-consumer-and-commercial-online-scams).
- Require carriers to block spoofed calls and texts, and platforms to verify financial advertisers and remove scam ads quickly.
- Set transaction limits and fraud warnings on crypto kiosks, and require exchanges to freeze flagged funds.

**Investigate and prosecute**

- Adopt a national anti-scam strategy. Bipartisan bills already exist, including the [Strategic Task Force on Scam Prevention Act](https://menendez.house.gov/media/press-releases/ahead-of-holiday-season-menendez-and-houchin-introduce-bipartisan-legislation-to-protect-americans-from-scams) in the House and a [national strategy bill and sanctions bill](https://www.aging.senate.gov/download/02/09/2026/opening-statement_scott-011426pdf?download=1) in the Senate.
- Build one front door for reporting, shared by the FBI, the FTC and local police, with a rapid fund-freeze process in every state.
- Fund state and regional cyber-fraud units through Safety Compacts, so a report to local police leads somewhere.
- Target the networks abroad with sanctions, asset seizure, extradition and pressure on host governments.

**Get ahead of AI**

- Make it a clear federal offense to use a cloned voice or likeness to defraud, and treat AI impersonation as an aggravating factor at sentencing.
- Require voice-cloning and deepfake services to verify customers and consent, and to keep records investigators can obtain with a warrant.
- Give banks, agencies and carriers verified channels, so a call claiming to be from Social Security or a bank can be checked.
- Fund defensive AI: detection tools for carriers, banks and public agencies, and for the fraud units above.

**Protect identity**

- Make credit files frozen by default and free to unfreeze.
- Stop treating a Social Security number as a password. Fund secure, optional digital identity verification for benefits and banking.
- Hold companies accountable for breaches, and restrict data brokers from selling the personal details scammers use.
- Give every identity theft victim one caseworker and one process to clear their record.

**Count it and stand with victims**

- Add fraud and cybercrime to the headline national crime statistics and to the Safety Compact scorecard.
- Set public targets for reported losses, recovery share and cases charged.
- Open victim compensation and counseling to fraud victims, with dedicated help for older adults.

## The coalition problem

The planks most likely to earn voters' trust are the ones that divide Democrats, so the platform needs a bargain each wing can accept. The evidence usually gives both sides something, which is what makes a bargain possible.

The divide is narrower among voters than among activists. In [Pew's 2021 survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/?p=8694), 34% of Democrats wanted more local police spending and 25% wanted less, and Black and Hispanic Democrats were more likely than white Democrats to want more. In a [2022 theGrio/KFF survey](https://thegrio.com/2022/10/25/black-voters-thegrio-kff-funding-police), 17% of Black voters wanted police funding cut. Both polls are several years old.

| Issue | The split | What the evidence gives each side | A version that can pass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Police hiring | Reformers expect more stops and minor arrests. Moderates see staffing as the precondition for everything else. | One study supports both: fewer homicides, and 7 to 22 more low-level arrests per officer. | Hiring money goes first to detectives and violent-crime units. Departments report low-level arrests alongside clearance. |
| Order enforcement | Reformers see poverty and illness being criminalized. Moderates see the disorder voters meet every day. | Rigorous evidence on sweeps is thin either way. Treatment access and diversion for first-time minor offenses both show crime reductions.\* | No enforcement without an offer: treatment and shelter capacity is funded first, and penalties rise only with repeat offending. |
| Pretrial detention | Reformers fought to end cash bail. Moderates want dangerous defendants held. | Jurisdictions that ended cash bail have mostly not seen crime rise. Risk prediction is error-prone.\* | End detention based on ability to pay. Allow a narrow hold for demonstrated danger, with a prompt hearing, counsel, time limits and published data. |
| Violence intervention | Progressives treat it as a signature program. Skeptics see thin proof. | The best trial was promising and inconclusive. Results vary by site. | Multi-year funding with independent evaluation built in, and an end date for programs that do not deliver. |
| Police accountability | Reformers want liability and oversight changes. Moderates and unions fear recruitment losses. | Chicago's procedural justice training was first reported to cut complaints 10% and force 6.4%; the authors later revised those findings.\* [Wood, Tyler and Papachristos](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7211954) | Decertification registry, public force and complaint data, body cameras. On liability, make the employer answerable in place of the individual officer (see plank 2). |
| Repeat violent offenders | Reformers resist anything resembling new mandatory minimums. Moderates point to concentration of offending. | Certainty deters more than severity. Incapacitation of the most active offenders does prevent crime.\* | Prosecutors prioritize repeat violent cases and move them fast. No new mandatory minimums. |

**What each wing gets.** Reformers get an end to wealth-based detention, treatment before enforcement, stable intervention funding and enforceable police standards. Moderates get staffing, consistent enforcement, detention for the demonstrably dangerous and a party that says "consequences" without flinching. Neither gets those things alone.

**What holds it together.** Every element is measured against the same public outcomes. Reformers can test whether enforcement is fair. Moderates can test whether programs work. The shared scorecard in the next section is the enforcement mechanism for the bargain itself.

**Where it could break.** Pretrial detention and order enforcement are the likeliest fractures. A caucus that cannot agree there should still move the clearance, places and political-violence planks, which have little internal opposition and bipartisan co-sponsors.

## How the plan delivers

Washington sets the goals and pays for results; cities and counties choose the methods; everyone's performance is public. That design serves three aims: better conditions on the ground, local solutions that work, and accountability that runs in every direction.

### Safety Compacts: pay for local results

A Safety Compact is a multi-year agreement between a city or county and its state and federal funders. The mechanics below are a proposal.

1. **The locality writes the plan.** It starts from local data on who is being hurt and where. A compact board that includes residents, victims' families, police and service providers signs off.
2. **One application, one flexible stream.** Money can go to detectives, outreach workers, lighting or treatment beds, as the plan requires.
3. **Money follows need.** A formula weights funding by violence burden and by how little the local tax base can carry. Every state gets a floor, and small places get a regional track.
4. **Five-year terms.** Programs can hire and plan. Annual check-ins replace annual reapplication.
5. **Proven approaches fund fastest.** A tenth of each grant is reserved for untested local ideas that come with an independent evaluation.
6. **Improvement earns more.** Bonuses reward progress against a place's own baseline, so the hardest-hit cities are not competing with the safest.
7. **Failure has a remedy before a penalty.** A place that misses its targets gets hands-on help and a revised plan. Money moves away from specific programs that keep failing, not from the community.
8. **Capacity comes with the money.** State support teams and experienced intermediaries help places that lack grant writers and analysts.

### Accountability in every direction

| Who | Accountable for | How the public sees it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People who commit violence | Being caught and facing a prompt, proportionate consequence | Clearance rates; time from arrest to case outcome |
| Police | Solving serious crime lawfully and respectfully | Clearance by neighborhood; force and complaint data; decertification records |
| Prosecutors and courts | Moving cases quickly and explaining decisions | Case timelines; charging and dismissal rates |
| Programs and nonprofits | Reducing harm, as shown by evidence | Independent evaluations; renewal tied to results |
| Mayors, governors, Congress | Funding what works and reporting honestly | The annual scorecard, published in good years and bad |
| Parties and candidates | One standard on political violence | Public condemnation and consequences for their own side |

### Justice for all means equal protection

The same crime should get the same effort whoever the victim is. It currently does not.

- Homicides with Black victims are 3.4% to 4.8% less likely to be cleared than otherwise similar cases, according to [a 2024 study in Criminology](https://cris.fbk.eu/handle/11582/346149).
- In Chicago in 2021, 21.7% of homicides in predominantly Black neighborhoods led to prosecution, against 45.6% in predominantly white ones, [per Live Free Chicago](https://news.wttw.com/2023/02/03/homicide-clearance-rate-lower-chicago-s-black-communities-report).

The commitments that follow:

- Publish clearance rates by neighborhood and by victim race, and make closing the gap a compact target.
- Staff investigations in proportion to caseload, so detectives in the hardest-hit districts carry no more cases than anyone else.
- Apply the law the same way to the powerful: officers who break it, officials who abuse office, and political allies who commit or incite violence.

### Building the culture

Rules produce compliance. Culture decides what people do when nobody is checking.

- **Treat victims as the system's clients.** Every family of a homicide victim gets a named liaison and regular case updates.
- **Make fairness a professional skill.** Train officers in procedural justice, and promote on solved cases and community trust as well as arrests.
- **Measure trust directly.** Survey residents each year on safety and on how police and courts treated them. Count it as an outcome.
- **Give residents standing.** Neighborhood safety councils see the same data as the chief and help set priorities.
- **Model it at the top.** Leaders report bad numbers, admit failed programs and end them.

### The scorecard

Six measures, published for every compact jurisdiction:

1. Shootings and homicides per 100,000 residents
2. Clearance for murder and non-fatal shootings, overall and by neighborhood
3. Days from arrest to case outcome
4. Resident trust and safety survey results
5. Use-of-force incidents and sustained complaints
6. Reported fraud losses and the share recovered

### Guarding against bad incentives

Paying for results invites gaming the results. The design has to assume that and check for it.

- **Count only real clearances.** Bonuses count arrests that lead to charges. Cases closed administratively do not count.
- **Audit the data.** Random audits check how crimes are classified. Shooting counts are cross-checked against hospital records, and reported crime against victim surveys.
- **Never reward one number alone.** A bonus requires fewer shootings and no worsening in complaints, use of force or disparities.
- **Make fairness a condition.** Reporting clearance by neighborhood and victim race, and a plan to close gaps, are required to receive any funds.
- **Keep the evaluator independent.** Evaluations are federally funded, assigned separately from the grantee, and published whatever they find.
- **Put rules on technology.** Surveillance tools bought with compact funds need a public use policy and a yearly audit.
- **Let residents see it all.** The scorecard, the audits and the evaluations are public, at the neighborhood level.

### Built to last

Much of the recent expansion in local capacity was paid for with temporary pandemic aid. The plan is designed to avoid repeating that.

- **Permanent authorization, reviewed every five years.** Programs do not expire by default. Each one must show evaluation results to be renewed.
- **No hiring cliff.** The federal share of each new position steps down over five years, and a city must show how it will carry the cost before it hires.
- **An automatic stabilizer.** When a state's revenue drops sharply or shootings spike, the federal share rises without a new vote. Local capacity collapsed in 2020 exactly when it was needed.
- **Add, don't replace.** Federal funds must supplement existing local safety spending.
- **Build the workforce.** Money cannot hire detectives, outreach workers, treatment clinicians or forensic analysts who do not exist. Each title sets aside funds for training, credentials and loan repayment.
- **Reinvest savings.** A share of documented savings from fewer shootings and jail stays returns to the compact that produced them.
- **Start with what both parties support.** Clearance grants, victim funding, official security and anti-scam measures already have bipartisan sponsors. A floor for every state and a rural track give every member a stake.
- **Name the revenue.** The bill tab proposes a package covering about $11 billion of the $12.1 billion: an alcohol tax change, an assessment on firms that carry fraud, firearms charges, and an end to domestic military deployments. Each has real political costs, set out there.

## How to talk about it

The message is a promise about what happens to you, stated before any statistic: "If someone hurts you, we will catch them. If your block is dangerous, we will fix it. If you serve your community, we will protect you."

- **Lead with victims, not trends.** "Three in ten murders go unsolved" lands harder than "murder is down 18%," and it is equally true.
- **Say the plain words.** Police, detectives, arrests, consequences. Voters hear hedging as reluctance.
- **Claim competence, not sole credit.** The decline spans cities of every political stripe. The honest claim is that Democrats will lock it in and finish the job.
- **Draw the contrast on results.** Republicans cancelled $811 million for crime prevention and victims, and spent heavily on a Guard deployment that did not reduce violent crime. Democrats will pay for detectives.
- **Name the 2020 problem once and move on.** "Cutting police was wrong. More and better policing is the plan."
- **Hold one standard on political violence.** Credibility here depends on condemning attacks on Republicans in the same breath as attacks on Democrats.
- **Report back.** Publish clearance rates and shooting counts on a schedule, including when they get worse.

## Objections and open disputes

This platform is one side of a live argument, and several of its premises are contested. The strongest objections follow.

### From the right

- **Incapacitation is undersold.** A small number of repeat offenders commit much of the violence, and keeping them locked up prevents crime with certainty. Critics say the platform's skepticism of long sentences repeats the mistakes of 2020.
- **The decline happened on Republicans' watch.** The record 2025 drop coincided with the current administration's enforcement push, and the FBI director credits it. Researchers note the decline began in 2022 and cannot yet apportion causes.
- **Democratic prosecutors and bail laws are the credibility problem.** No federal platform fixes that unless local Democrats change course.
- **Violence intervention has thin evidence.** The best randomized trial missed significance on its main outcome. Critics see the cancelled grants as waste, not safety.
- **Gun laws burden the law-abiding.** Most crime guns are obtained illegally, and the research on licensing laws is disputed.

### From the left

- **More police means more low-level arrests.** The same study that shows lives saved shows 7 to 22 extra minor arrests per officer, falling hardest on Black residents.
- **Order enforcement criminalizes poverty and illness.** Citations and sweeps can push people further from housing and treatment.
- **Root causes are missing.** Housing, income and health investments may prevent more crime per dollar over time than any policing plank.
- **Pretrial detention based on danger expands jailing.** Risk predictions are error-prone and racially skewed.
- **Crime is at a record low.** Critics ask why the party should adopt a tougher posture now, and whether doing so validates a misleading narrative.

### On specific planks

- **Employer liability and licensing (plank 2).** Police groups expect more lawsuits, hesitant officers and higher insurance costs for small towns. Some reform advocates say only personal liability changes behavior. Budget officials note that payouts come from service budgets. Unions will fight the arbitration limits hardest.
- **Fraud and cybercrime (plank 9).** Banks argue that mandatory reimbursement invites carelessness and false claims. Civil libertarians warn that identity checks and transaction monitoring expand surveillance. AI developers say customer-verification rules burden legitimate uses and are easy to evade. Reported losses also reflect more reporting and higher crypto prices.

### Open empirical questions

- **Why crime fell.** No consensus exists. A platform built on "what works" should admit the largest effects are unexplained.
- **How reliable the data are.** Clearance figures and FBI estimates depend on incomplete reporting. Victim surveys and police reports sometimes diverge.
- **Whether salience is rising.** Gallup shows concern about crime falling for two years. The case for this platform rests on the trust gap and on political violence, not on a public panic.
- **Whether threats predict attacks.** Threat counts measure reports, and reporting has become easier. Most threats are not followed by violence, though a few are, with severe effects on who serves.
- **Whether any platform moves the trust gap.** The Republican advantage has survived decades of changing conditions. Policy may matter less than candidates and local records.
