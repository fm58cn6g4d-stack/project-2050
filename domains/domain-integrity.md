Canonical as of Oct 19, 2026 (batch 2; follow-ups applied the same evening). This file covers staging inventory sections 6 (anti-corruption) and 7 (money in politics), which become pointers. **The 2026 fact that reshapes this domain.** *NRSC v. FEC* (June 30, 2026, 6-3) struck down federal limits on party coordinated expenditures and overruled *Colorado II*. Contribution limits, earmarking rules and the coordination rules for outside groups stand (Wiley, Skadden, Covington, Mayer Brown, June-July 2026). The practical instruction: spend no floor time on party coordination limits, and put the coordination effort entirely into outside groups (INT-8). Note that sources/courts-rebalancing-the-three-branches-2026.md still lists the outcome as unconfirmed; that caveat is out of date and the source file should be annotated.

## INT-1. Stock ownership and trading ban

**Design.** A ban on individual stock ownership and trading for members of Congress, their spouses and dependent children, extended to the President, Vice President and Cabinet. Compliance by divestiture or a qualified blind trust, with real penalties and a short transition. Packaged with congressional staffing (domain-capacity.md CAP-1) so that accountability buys capacity — the trade that makes a pay-and-staffing increase defensible.

**The floor this must hold.** The House passed the narrower Stop Insider Trading Act 232-198 on July 22, 2026 (no new purchases, notice before sales, SAVE America Act provisions attached); Democrats called it a Trojan horse. The Senate Homeland Security Committee advanced a ban in July 2026. Both Trump and Jeffries back a ban in principle (NOTUS; Gray DC, Sept 10, 2026; NBC Palm Springs, July 22, 2026).

**What that means for 2029.** Some version may well be law before the trifecta. This row is therefore written as the **floor the Democratic version must hold**, not as a new idea: ownership not just trading, spouses and dependents included, the executive branch covered, and no attached provisions that trade the ban for something else. If a weaker version enacts in 2027-28, the 2029 bill is an amendment to it and should be drafted that way now.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Effect | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| INT-1A (chosen) | Ownership ban, Congress plus senior executive, divest or blind trust | Strongest; one day on the fast track (C27) | estimate |
| INT-1B | Trading ban only (the July 2026 House shape) | Easier; leaves held positions in place | sourced |
| INT-1C | Congress only, executive branch excluded | Drops the hardest political fight and the clearest conflicts | estimate |

## INT-2. DISCLOSE Act (merged)

**Design.** Disclosure of the sources of political spending, including through intermediaries. Disclosure survives the *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* exacting-scrutiny standard, and was upheld 8-1 in *Citizens United* itself — this is the most legally secure campaign-finance row available.

**Vehicle.** Merged into the electoral and civil-rights package (DEM-1) under ledger cut C9. Listed here because the substance is this domain's.

## INT-3. Revolving door

**Design.** Extended cooling-off periods for executive-branch officials joining regulated industries, with a matching bar on entering government from a position where recent paid advocacy creates a direct conflict.

## INT-4. Emoluments enforcement

**Design.** Statutory enforcement of the foreign and domestic emoluments clauses with a private cause of action — necessary because the 2017-21 emoluments litigation died on standing and mootness rather than on the merits. The cause of action is the whole content of the row.

## INT-5. Small-dollar matching

**Design.** A 6:1 federal match on small contributions, paired with voluntary spending caps for participating candidates. **No trigger-based matching** — *Arizona Free Enterprise* (2011) bars matching funds that rise in response to an opponent's spending, which is the design most reform bills reach for first. Public financing is *Buckley*-compatible as a voluntary subsidy; the trigger is the part that is not.

**Cost and financing (decided Oct 19, Espen: INT-5B).** About $0.01T, range $0.00T to $0.03T (estimate; ledger DEM1's range), paid from general revenue under D4 with no separate channel. The ledger's fines surcharge (FS1, unsized) was a poor fit for the trust-fund rule, which reserves a dedicated channel for a permanent benefit with a natural revenue stream covering much or all of its cost: fine revenue is volatile and politically awkward as a funding source for campaigns. At this size the channel bought nothing and complicated the accounts, so the small-dollar matching channel and row FS1 are retired in the ledger regenerated Oct 19.

**Options.**

| Option | Financing | Assessment | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| INT-5B (chosen, Oct 19) | General revenue under D4, no separate channel | Simplest; the base closes it with everything else | estimate |
| INT-5A (retired) | Fines surcharge, own channel | The earlier ledger design; unsized; weak fit to the trust-fund rule | estimate |
| INT-5C | A dedicated assessment on federal contractors or lobbying registrants | Closer to a natural stream; invites a First Amendment challenge the match itself would not face | estimate |

**Strategic note.** Public financing is a parallel track that builds toward nothing (Radical Means, rule 1): it does not create a constituency or a precedent that the next reform stands on. It is worth doing on its merits; it should not be sequenced ahead of things that compound.

## INT-6. Candidate tax disclosure

**Design.** Mandatory tax-return disclosure for presidential and vice-presidential candidates, as a ballot-access or qualification condition at the federal level.

## INT-7. FEC restructuring

**Design.** Break the 3-3 deadlock by structure: either an odd-numbered commission, or a separated enforcement bureau with independent authority to act on complaints. Designed **without relying on removal protection**, per domain-executive-power.md EXE-9: staggered terms, quorum rules that a vacancy strategy cannot defeat, and appropriations and reporting conditions.

**Honest limit.** Post-*Slaughter*, a restructured FEC is more functional than today's and less insulated than the 1975 design intended. The deadlock is fixable; the independence is not fully restorable by statute.

**Cost.** Nominal. The FEC and the other integrity-enforcement units are now named in the agency-rebuild line with allocations (domains/domain-capacity.md, CAP-7): the FEC about $3B, and about $12B for the FinCEN beneficial-ownership unit, the DOJ FARA unit, the Office of Government Ethics and SEC compliance (all estimates).

## INT-8. Outside-group coordination

**Design.** Tighten coordination rules for outside groups and treat single-candidate super PACs as coordinated with the candidate they exist to support. Narrowed after *NRSC*: outside-group rules stand, party coordination limits are gone.

**Why this is now the centerpiece.** With party limits struck down, the single-candidate super PAC is the structure doing the most work, and the coordination rules are the only live lever against it.

## INT-15. Shareholder approval of corporate political spending

**Short answer: Congress can do it for public companies, and the states can do it for companies chartered in them; the two are different instruments with different reach.**

**The federal route (chosen).** The vehicle is the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. The most developed text is the Shareholder Protection Act of 2021 (S. 530, 117th Congress), which amends the Exchange Act (new Sections 14C, 16A and 13(s)). As introduced it:

- requires a separate shareholder vote, by a majority of outstanding shares, before an issuer makes covered political expenditures (independent expenditures, electioneering communications, and dues to 501(c) groups used for such purposes);
- requires a board vote on any political expenditure over $50,000, with individual directors' votes posted publicly within 48 hours;
- makes violations a breach of fiduciary duty, with officers and directors who authorize them jointly and severally liable for three times the amount;
- adds quarterly reporting to the SEC and shareholders, and directs exchanges to delist noncompliant issuers;
- requires institutional investors to disclose how they voted on these questions, with a safe harbor for divesting over an issuer's political spending.

**Reach and limits.**

- It covers *issuers* — publicly traded companies — wherever they are chartered. It does not reach private companies, LLCs or most nonprofits, except through the dues provision, so it is a partial answer to the dark-money problem, not a complete one.
- State corporate law, not federal law, has traditionally governed what managers may do with corporate funds. A 2009 Brennan Center report describes the baseline as the business judgment rule with no clear state standard for when political spending is wasteful, and says federal securities law for the most part does not address the question. Federal securities law is the lever Congress has chosen the same way it did for say-on-pay, which is why the bill sits in the Exchange Act.
- S. 530 cites no constitutional provision (its severability clause names none). Securities regulation under the Commerce Clause is the conventional basis; that inference is mine, not the bill's.
- **First Amendment exposure is the real question and is not settled by anything in the project's sources.** The Brennan report predates *Citizens United* and does not analyze whether a shareholder-authorization rule would itself survive review. My recollection is that the *Citizens United* majority pointed to the "procedures of corporate democracy" as the answer to dissenting shareholders, which cuts in favor of the design; I have not re-checked the opinion text. **Flagged for legal review before drafting.**
- Reciprocity question: unions are not issuers and the bill does not reach their political spending. Expect that asymmetry to be the first objection; whether to answer it with a member-consent provision is a design choice not made here.

**The state route.** States can require shareholder authorization for corporations chartered in them. Delaware is the leverage point because so many large companies are chartered there, but a federal statute does not depend on any state moving. Treat the state version as a deposit and a fallback (INT-17), not the main route.

## INT-17. The state track

**Design.** Montana-style charter-forfeiture initiatives in every initiative state, which build the legal theory that artificial persons are not citizens; state-level public-financing programs as proof of concept for INT-5; and state-law shareholder-authorization statutes as the INT-15C fallback.

**Why these and not others.** All compound: the forfeiture initiatives build toward the anti-corruption amendment (staging 17.1), state public financing builds the administrative record and the political case for the federal match, and state shareholder rules build the record for the federal one. ✔ at 51 because none needs Congress.
