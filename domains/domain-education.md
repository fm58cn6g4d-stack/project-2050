# Domain file: Education (EDU)

Canonical as of Oct 20, 2026 (education batch). Education was the framework's blind spot: until today it was one row, FAM-4, inside the family file. This file takes it over and builds it out from two session analyses:
- **sources/education-transforming-education-act-analysis-and-trust-fund.md**: Rep. Bonamici's Transforming Education for the Future Act (introduced Sept 14, 2026; 466 pages, about 22 bundled bills), extended in session into a birth-through-college package with a payroll-financed trust fund.
- **sources/education-ai-education-and-workforce-readiness-act-analysis.md**: Rep. Bonamici's AI Education and Workforce Readiness Act, which the session reads as the omnibus's AI chapter.

**Scope.** K-12 formula funding, special education, school meals, the educator workforce, higher education, the Education Department's structure and students' rights, the federal scholarship tax credit, AI in education and student data, financing architecture, and research and evaluation. **Not here:** child care and pre-K stay in domains/domain-family.md FAM-2 (one home; this file's financing option EDU-9B would carry FAM-2's money); AI workforce programs are AI-4 and LAB-8; national service is LAB-9; civic education is DEM-16.

**What this file changes in the ledger.** FAM-4 ($0.65T) moves here at the same total and splits into EDU-1 (K-12 formula funding, carrying EDU-2) and EDU-5 (higher education). Four rows are new: EDU-3 universal school meals ($0.27T), EDU-4 the educator pipeline ($0.03T), EDU-7 repeal of the scholarship tax credit (a $0.04T saving) and EDU-8 AI in education, student data and research ($0.015T, appropriations). Net effect on the general-fund gap: **+$0.275T** at the midpoint. The trust-fund design (EDU-9B) is **not chosen**: it needs decisions under D3 and D4.

All figures are ten-year totals for 2030-39 in trillions unless marked. Basis tags: **sourced**, **scaled**, **estimate** (mine), **session estimate** (the education sessions' own figures, built from CBO and CRS published estimates; none is a CBO score). Survival at 51 (Dec. D1): ✔ survives, ◐ needs a D2 majority-precedent path, ✖ needs 60, 67 or Republican votes.

## Where things stand (world state, Oct 2026)

Facts below are as reported in the two session analyses (Oct 19, 2026) and were not independently re-checked here, except the omnibus's introduction, which the Congressional Record confirms.

- **The omnibus.** Rep. Bonamici introduced the Transforming Education for the Future Act in morning hour on Sept 14, 2026 (Congressional Record, vol. 172, no. 144): tripled Title I, free universal preschool for 3- and 4-year-olds, free school meals and summer meals, multiple measures beyond test scores, a stronger educator pipeline, full IDEA funding, civil-rights protections, and protection of the Education Department. Universal pre-K and meals are mandatory in the text; everything else is authorized, about $73B a year in fixed amounts against roughly $28B for the same programs today. Fully funded, the session puts it at roughly $100-125B a year in new federal spending at first and $150-190B by FY31 (session estimate).
- **Funding.** FY2026 gave ED $79.0B in the omnibus signed Feb 3, 2026, about 0.1% below FY2024; most Trump-era cuts happened outside appropriations (withheld funds, canceled grants, layoffs, transfers). The House FY27 bill would give ED $70.7B, cutting Title I by nearly $1.9B and zeroing Titles II-A and III-A.
- **The Department.** By June 2026 ED had signed 14 interagency agreements with six agencies moving at least 148 programs; on June 16 it announced special education would move to HHS and civil-rights enforcement to DOJ. The FY26 law neither unwinds those agreements nor prevents new ones.
- **Vouchers.** OBBBA (P.L. 119-21, sec. 70411) created a dollar-for-dollar credit of up to $1,700 for donations to scholarship organizations, uncapped in total, in states that opt in, from Jan 1, 2027.
- **Accounts.** Trump Accounts give every citizen child born 2025-2028 a $1,000 federal deposit; Treasury reported applications for about 3 million children. The deposit program's 2029 expiry is a natural vehicle question (see EDU-5's "considered and set aside").
- **Politics.** Rep. Jahana Hayes has launched a bid for Education and Workforce ranking member against Bobby Scott; Rep. Bonamici has said she is "not ruling anything out." The omnibus reads as a platform.

## EDU-1. Title I

**Design.** Title I grants rise from about $18.4B (FY24 and FY26) to $26B a year, as mandatory funding in R2, through the existing formulas so money reaches districts in the first school year after enactment (ESEA reached districts the summer after April 1965; ESSER moved $122B in weeks).

**Cost.** About $0.08T (estimate; FAM-4's split).

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Cost | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EDU-1A (chosen) | $26B a year, mandatory | ~0.08 | estimate (carried from FAM-4) |
| EDU-1B | **A $30B mandatory floor** with discretionary appropriations layered on top (the mandatory Pell add-on model) | ~0.14 (+0.06) | session design; estimate |
| EDU-1C | The omnibus as written: $55B a year, authorized (appropriations decide) | ~0.35 if appropriated in full; 0 if not | session; an authorization is not durable |
| EDU-1D | EDU-1B plus the omnibus's accountability package (subgroup size 10, improvement set-aside to 10%) | as B | held for an ESEA reauthorization (EDU-6) |

**Why A.** It is the filed design and the cheapest durable step. **B is recommended if the financing allows:** a guaranteed floor is the durable instrument, and the session's trust-fund model uses exactly $30B. The session's warning on C: a $55B authorization erodes toward $30-40B within two or three appropriations cycles. **Drafting dependency:** universal meals (EDU-3) end the free and reduced-price applications that Title I's poverty counts and many state formulas use, so EDU-1 must carry a replacement poverty measure (direct certification plus Census estimates).

## EDU-2. Special education (IDEA)

**Design.** A statutory glide path from about 11% (IDEA's $14.2B FY24 grant covered about 10.9% of average per-pupil spending) to the full 40% federal share, written as a direct appropriation ("hereby appropriated"), on the IDEA Full Funding Act model. The 40% target self-adjusts to actual per-pupil spending.

**Restraint and seclusion, as a condition on the new money.** The practices fall overwhelmingly on students with disabilities, so IDEA is the strongest hook (Title I's relatedness argument is strained). The passable version: ban prone, supine, mechanical and chemical restraint; restrict rather than abolish seclusion (imminent danger, time limits, monitoring, documentation); 24-hour parent notification and incident reporting; funded training and positive behavior supports; **no private right of action**. It conditions only the incremental funds above the current share, so a state that declines loses the increase, not its whole grant, which keeps it clear of *NFIB* coercion. Written as a use-of-funds restriction with scoreable training spending. **Byrd risk:** conditions whose budget effect is "merely incidental" can be struck; the fallback is the same text on the floor or in an IDEA reauthorization (overdue since 2004). The House passed a restraint bill 262-153 in 2010; the blockage is superintendent and principal opposition to absolute bans, not ideology.

**Cost.** About $0.25T, range $0.19-0.33T (estimate; FAM-4's $0.2-0.3T). The session projects the federal increase at about $21-22B in FY27 rising to $62-67B by FY33 at 40% (session estimate), which is faster than this file's glide path; the chosen path reaches 40% around FY2035-37.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Cost | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EDU-2A (chosen) | Mandatory glide path to 40%, with restraint-and-seclusion conditions on new money | ~0.25 | estimate |
| EDU-2B | Faster path: 40% by FY2033 (the omnibus's date) | ~0.35-0.40 | session estimate |
| EDU-2C | The omnibus as written: "authorized to be appropriated" | 0 durable | not recommended: full funding would depend on appropriators every year |
| EDU-2D | Glide path without the conditions; restraint and seclusion as a separate floor bill | ~0.25; +1-2 floor days | estimate |

## EDU-3. Universal school meals

**Design.** Free breakfast and lunch for every enrolled child, effective one school year after enactment; reduced-price and paid tiers ended; unpaid meal debt reimbursed; Summer EBT raised from $40 to $60 a month; a bonus for 25% local sourcing. Reimbursement at **current** free-meal rates, indexed. School meals are already an open-ended entitlement paying per meal served, which is the model of a durable instrument.

**The rate correction.** The omnibus copies 2023 rates ($4.63 lunch, $2.80 breakfast), below current 2026-27 free reimbursement ($4.76-4.87 and $2.54-3.05); the Senate's 2026 version uses $5.42 and $3.28. At the Senate rates, roughly 5.5B lunches and 3.2B breakfasts plus CACFP, summer meals, the larger Summer EBT and commodities come to about $54B in current dollars against about $32B, an **increment of about +$22B a year**, not the +$13-18B the session first estimated (session estimate).

**Cost.** About $0.27T, range $0.22-0.33T (estimate: +$22B a year in 2026 dollars, grown about 2% a year and phased in from the 2030-31 school year).

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Cost | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EDU-3A (chosen) | Universal, current rates indexed, Summer EBT at $60 | ~0.27 | estimate from session |
| EDU-3B | Expand community eligibility instead (lower the threshold, raise the multiplier) | ~0.08 | estimate; ledger option C36 saves ~$0.19T |
| EDU-3C | The omnibus as written, at its 2023 rates | ~0.15 | not recommended: per-meal payments would fall for many high-poverty schools |

**Why A.** It is the fastest benefit in the file (meals can go universal mid-year; it is administratively simpler than tiered eligibility), it reaches the fourth quintile (incidence v1.3 moves Q4 on scenario B from +$546 to +$779 a year), and both the session and the Talarico scenario lead with it. Universality also removes the deservingness frame (Talarico finding: "a universal one cannot be racialized").

## EDU-4. Educator workforce

**Design.** Sequencing matters more than the amount: teacher preparation takes one to four years, so pipeline money must land two to three years ahead of the demand shock (FAM-2's pre-K hiring, peaking 2031-33), not alongside it (design rule 5). The tools exist and are underfunded:
- **Teacher residencies** through Teacher Quality Partnership grants (about $70M now; residencies cost $20-30K per candidate, so 50,000 residents a year is a $1-1.5B program).
- **Registered teaching apprenticeships**, the cheapest paraprofessional-to-teacher on-ramp, run through LAB-8's apprenticeship line.
- **Grow Your Own** and Hawkins Centers, front-loaded so cohorts enrolling in fall 2030 finish in 2031-32.
- **Targeting:** special education, bilingual education and early childhood, where prep capacity binds and pre-K's BA requirement bites.

It is a time-limited surge (2030-36) that tapers to a small permanent line.

**Cost.** About $0.03T, range $0.02-0.05T (estimate; the session's pipeline line is about $5B a year in 2030-33, tapering).

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Cost | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EDU-4A (chosen) | Pipeline surge | ~0.03 | estimate |
| EDU-4B | Add a **$60,000 minimum teacher salary**, CPI-indexed: federal grants covering the gap, at least 85% passed to districts, priority to Title I and rural, with a state sustainability plan; temporary grants declining by design (the trust-fund model's version) | +0.06-0.08 (declining grants); +0.15-0.20 as the omnibus's open-ended "such sums" | session estimate ($12-20B a year at full gap) |
| EDU-4C | Add paraprofessional wages (PARA Act, on the Title I formula) and school mental-health staff | +0.03-0.06 | session estimate ($2-5B and $0.5-1B a year) |
| EDU-4D | Add a temporary teacher bridge credit (2030-31) so money lands in year one | ~+0.01 | session design |

**On B.** The binding constraint on teacher supply is usually compensation, not prep slots, so the salary floor may do more for the pipeline than pipeline programs do (session). The session's long-run view: by 2040 the package adds 600,000 to 1 million workers across pre-K, K-12 and child care, with a nominal teaching median of $80-90K, and composition shifts show up around 2042-45. The early risk runs the other way: hiring 250,000 people fast means average quality dips before it rises, just when Congress asks whether the money worked (see EDU-10).

Any account policy belongs in the family or revenue files as a postsecondary or wealth instrument, and EDU-9B's fund must never be described in account terms.

## EDU-6. The Education Department and students' rights

**Design (chosen).** Three rules that protect the capacity everything else in this file depends on:
1. **No transfers by interagency agreement.** ESEA, IDEA, Perkins and education-research functions and funds cannot be moved out of ED, including by interagency agreement (the omnibus's Sec. 30113), and existing agreements are voided.
2. **A staffing floor** for ED's program offices and the Office for Civil Rights. The omnibus has none, despite its summary.
3. **Civil-rights enforcement back in ED.** The omnibus's bar does not reach civil-rights enforcement, which rests on separate statutes, so the June 2026 move to DOJ would survive it. This closes that gap.

**Vehicle.** Proposed to ride CAP-8 (civil service modernization and successor agencies, the C27 fast track) at no extra floor days, since it is the same kind of rule (reversing reorganizations by statute). Pending Espen; the fallback is the AI-1 title (EDU-8) or a standalone bill. ◐ Money for ED's staff is already in D9's line: Education is on the agency list, and batch 4 proposes $6B for the civil-rights offices including ED's.

**Held for an ESEA reauthorization (not first-Congress).** Under D1 there is no 2033 regular-order queue, so the omnibus's other mandates wait for a later rules change, as other files' held items do. The session's realistic 2029 outcome is "most of the dollars and few of the mandates." Held:
- the charter-school for-profit management ban written into the ESEA and IDEA definition (with knock-on Title I and IDEA effects);
- subgroup reporting at 10, new indicators (advanced coursework, school climate), the improvement set-aside to 10%, and the no-exit rule;
- limits on tests as the sole factor in retention, diplomas or evaluations (largely codifying practice), and the innovative-assessment pilot to 20 states;
- the bullying policy requirement (ESEA Part G, enumerated categories including sexual orientation and gender identity);
- right-to-read and school-library provisions;
- maintenance of equity (FY27-31, no disproportionate cuts to high-need districts);
- the desegregation aid program at $1B a year.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EDU-6A (chosen) | The three capacity rules, riding CAP-8 | Zero floor days if CAP-8 carries them |
| EDU-6B | Add a ban on corporal punishment in federally funded programs (legal in about 17 states), as a condition on new money | ◐; drop the private right of action if it rides R2 |
| EDU-6C | A first-Congress ESEA reauthorization carrying the held items | Adds a contested floor bill (≥4.5 days under F) when the calendar is already 1 day over under E |

## EDU-7. The federal scholarship tax credit

**Design.** Repeal P.L. 119-21 sec. 70411 **prospectively** (for contributions after enactment). The omnibus repeals it "as if not enacted," which is messy once credits have been claimed from Jan 1, 2027. Revenue, so it rides R2. ✔

**Cost.** A saving of about $0.04T, range $0.02-0.06T (estimate). No JCT figure for the enacted credit was found; the size depends on how many states opt in (the credit is uncapped in total, but states without school choice or with opposed governors are unlikely to join).

**Option EDU-7B.** Also repeal the D.C. Opportunity Scholarship Program (omnibus). Small; a separate fight.

## EDU-8. AI in education and student data

**Design.** The education chapters of the AI Education and Workforce Readiness Act, which the session reads as a governance-first counter-model to an industry-partnership approach:
- **Money (appropriations):** AI-literacy career pathway consortia pairing humanities and AI literacy with paid work-based learning; school-leader training; IES practice guides and expanded RELs; NSF regional centers barred from commercializing products; multilingual AI literacy and MSI capacity grants. About $0.9B a year across the bill's Titles I, III (education parts) and V (estimate from the bill's $350M, $450M and $100M annual authorizations).
- **Rules (ride AI-1 as an education title, ◐, no extra days):**
  - AI firms cannot control curricula in Title IV-funded programs or require exclusive platforms (Sec. 104), which becomes a de facto national edtech procurement standard because nearly every district takes ESEA money;
  - an ED student data-breach portal, an AI risk-assessment framework for edtech, and generative-AI guidance with harmful-content safeguards and parental opt-outs (Title V);
  - human review of automated decisions in funded programs (overlaps AI-5, which governs federal agencies; this reaches grantees);
  - the Sec. 6 compliance regime (open licensing, independent evaluation, student opt-outs, no commercial use of student data), **with its own funding line for small grantees** such as libraries and tribal colleges (the bill gives the whole regime $10M a year).
- **Not here:** the workforce titles (occupations list, displacement reporting, reskilling grants, the Sec. 208 credit) go to AI-4 and LAB-8; Title IV's data-center disclosure goes to CLI-6E.

**Cost.** About $0.015T with EDU-10, range $0.01-0.025T (estimate).

**Why it rides AI-1.** AI-1 must pass in the first trifecta (Espen, Oct 19), it is already a floor bill, and these are the AI harms "already showing up in classrooms" that the session says make the stronger public pitch than frontier risk. Children's safety is also where Republicans engage without endorsing a slowdown.

## EDU-9. Financing architecture

### EDU-9A (chosen): mandatory spending from the general fund

Every money row above is direct spending in R2 (meals already is), paid from the general-fund base under D4. Competitive and innovation programs stay discretionary: they need flexibility and buy little durability. This adds $0.275T to the gap. Ledger options C36 (meals at community-eligibility scale, $0.19T) and C35 (the estate tax, now counted at about $0.15T as the increment beyond REV-G9) together roughly cover it; neither does alone (restated Oct 20).

### EDU-9B (option, not chosen): an education fund

The session's trust-fund design, recorded in full because it is the largest structural idea in either source.

**Scope.** Inside the fund: meals, CACFP and Summer EBT; IDEA at 40%; pre-K and child care (FAM-2's money); a $30B Title I floor (EDU-1B); the teacher floor (EDU-4B); PARA and school mental-health staff (EDU-4C); the pipeline. Outside: Title I above the floor, competitive ESEA programs and higher education (roughly $90-120B a year more, mostly new) stay on the discretionary or general-fund track. About 60% of the package becomes structurally protected.

**Revenue.**
- **An uncapped payroll contribution** of 1.28 points in the session's model (payroll base about $135B a point in 2030, growing 4%), kept structurally separate from Social Security because reconciliation bars changes to Title II of the Social Security Act. Called a contribution, and the fund named for what it buys.
- **The estate tax at 2009 parameters** ($3.5M exemption, unindexed), dedicated to facilities, the pipeline and reserve. Freezing the exemption makes revenue grow about 5% a year with wealth, against 3% if price-indexed; at least one source must grow as fast as wage-indexed spending.
- **Correction for this project:** the session's estate component also ends stepped-up basis, which is REV-G6 ($0.70T), already in the base. Counting only the estate tax (REV-E1, about $0.35T in-window gross, estimate), the payroll rate that keeps the fund positive rises from 1.28 to roughly **1.4 points** (estimate). **Second correction (Oct 20):** REV-G9 already reverses most of the 2025 estate exemption, so only about $0.15T of REV-E1 is new money; on that basis the rate would sit at the top of the 1.4 range or slightly above (not re-modeled).

**Rates and the stress test (session, with the session's estate component).**

| Rate | Trough | Reserve 2049 | Cost a year at the median wage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.20 | −$103B (2040) | +$121B | ~$780 |

A recession of 2.5 points of unemployment in 2036-38 at 1.28 needs about $89B of borrowing, repaid by 2043, with every entitlement payment made; at 1.35 the reserve never reaches zero. The design answer is **statutory borrowing authority with a repayment schedule in the original act**, so a draw executes the plan rather than requesting a bailout (the FAM-3 pattern).

**What it does to the general fund (estimate).** The fund would carry about $2.6T of outlays in 2030-39. About $1.23T is general-fund claims already in this ledger (FAM-2 $0.60T, EDU-1 $0.33T, EDU-3 $0.27T, EDU-4 $0.03T); about $0.77T is current spending it absorbs (today's meals, Title I and IDEA baselines); about $0.63T is new benefits (a larger pre-K and child care share, the teacher floor, PARA and mental-health staff, Title I and IDEA top-ups). So it relieves the general fund of about **$2.0T** while raising about $2.3T from workers and $0.35T from estates.

**D3 (incidence model v1.3, scenario G).** Used as the closer in place of C19 (the exclusion cap) and C20 (the surtax), which it roughly matches in general-fund effect, EDU-9B leaves the first three quintiles better off than the full options list (Q1 +$11,090, Q2 +$9,361, Q3 +$5,612 a household a year against +$10,670, +$9,025, +$5,325) and the fourth slightly worse (+$636 against +$779). Every quintile below the top stays positive, so it **passes the quintile test**, but a fourth-quintile household without children pays the contribution and gets no school benefit, and 2030-31 are worse than under B until pre-K seats ramp.

**D4.** Two problems, both Espen's call:
- **A new dedicated fund.** D4 bars a trust fund "just to hold a pay-for" and allows one where a permanent benefit has a natural revenue stream covering much or all of its cost. The payroll piece is arguable on the FAM-3 pattern (the session's case: a worker pays in for forty years and draws for eighteen per child, close to the Social Security bargain, and a contributory frame changes the politics of cutting). Dedicating the estate tax is a pay-for placed in a fund and fails the rule as written.
- **What a fund buys.** The session itself concedes a trust fund does not insulate against reconciliation (51 votes can amend it); its value is the contributory frame, a permanent appropriation, and a constituency for the revenue. Mandatory spending (EDU-9A) delivers the permanent appropriation without the fund.

**Framing (session).** "Better education for our country's children" is an easier universal ask than "early childhood": folding both under one banner (school starts at three) puts early childhood inside an institution people already accept.

**Note for a later revenue strategy session (Espen, Oct 20).** EDU-9B stays unchosen, but its structure is worth revisiting alongside the agenda's other payroll raisers, possibly instead of one of them or harmonized with them, rather than judged on its own. The structure is: an uncapped payroll contribution of about 1.28-1.4 points split between employer and employee; receipts dedicated to a separate education fund outside Social Security; Treasury borrowing authority with a statutory repayment schedule so spending can ramp before receipts; an estate component. The list of payroll raisers to harmonize (REV-P1, REV-ED1, W1, SS-7, SS-1, SS-2, REV-H1a) is in domain-revenue.md under REV-ED1.

## EDU-10. Research and evaluation

**Design.** A National Center for Advanced Development in Education inside IES (the NEED Act design; about $500M a year), state longitudinal data systems restored and expanded (about $39M now), the IES evaluation protected from cancellation, and an ed-tech clearinghouse rating vendors on effectiveness, privacy and data monetization (which EDU-8's procurement rules can use). **Every major row carries an independent evaluation with pre-registered expectations.**

**Why it matters more than its cost.** The session's biggest failure mode is not fiscal: visible costs arrive a decade before measurable benefits, and the hiring-scramble quality dip lands when critics are looking. "A 2036 headline reading '$2 trillion later, NAEP is flat' would be technically accurate, analytically meaningless, and extremely effective." The answer is to front-load what shows results fast (meals, child-care costs, maternal employment, vacancy rates) and commit in advance to what the slower measures should show.

**Cost.** Inside EDU-8's row (estimate).

## Evidence the design rests on (session)

- **Scale-up returns.** Perry Preschool's 7-to-1 does not hold at scale; honest scaled estimates land nearer 2-to-1 or 3-to-1 (changed counterfactual, tiny samples, unscalable intensity, heterogeneity, crime benefits that don't transfer, discount rates). Head Start benefit-cost ratios usually land at 1.5-3; Boston raised college enrollment with no lasting test gains; Tulsa stayed positive; **Tennessee's statewide program turned negative by sixth grade**; Quebec's low-quality universal care produced worse outcomes. Quality determines where a program lands, which is why FAM-2D's standards matter more than the funding level.
- **Labor supply is the strongest economic argument.** Capping child-care costs plus universal pre-K plausibly adds 1-1.5 million workers, mostly mothers of young children, within three or four years: about 0.3-0.5% of GDP and $25-45B a year of federal revenue at maturity (session estimate). CBO's conventional scores will not credit most of it. Deficit-financed, the long-run GDP effect is plausibly near zero; financed by taxes on high-income consumption or inheritance, it stays positive.
- **Fungibility.** States reallocate their own money, so the effective increase is about 60-70% of nominal (session); maintenance-of-effort and maintenance-of-equity terms matter.
- **Child poverty.** Meals, Summer EBT and child-care affordability together take 1-2 points off child poverty, a quarter of what the 2021 credit did; for a poverty number, FAM-1 is the instrument, not schools.
- **What the package does not touch:** housing segregation, school assignment, curriculum, phones, and post-pandemic absenteeism.

## Speed and capacity

Formula money through existing pipes can start immediately; only pre-K seats (facilities, licensing) and new hires face real capacity constraints. The Head Start precedent is the caution: its summer-1965 launch produced quality problems that shaped criticism for decades, so speed is right for formula money and dangerous for new seats. Capacity (design rule 6): **ED** (program offices, OCR, IES, FSA) is on D9's agency list; **USDA's Food and Nutrition Service** runs EDU-3 through existing state agencies (not on D9's list; administration inside EDU-3's cost, estimate); **HHS's ACF** runs FAM-2; **state education agencies** carry most delivery and could draw on CAP-13's state capacity grants.

## Politics and vehicles

- **The split.** Reconciliation carries the money (meals, IDEA written as mandatory, Title I converted to mandatory, Pell, the scholarship-credit repeal); the mandates (restraint bans, charter definition, accountability changes, ED protections) need the floor. D5 already puts education in R2.
- **Conference shrinkage to expect (session):** Title I phased over five to eight years, the IDEA path stretched, pre-K's 100% federal share trimmed.
- **2027-28 grand bargain (AI bill session):** a deal with Trump would likely pass a skinny workforce-and-credit AI bill and keep the money while gutting the rules; data-center transparency (CLI-6E) is the piece most likely to move first.

## Survival at 51 (D1 stress test)

EDU-6 and EDU-8's rules need a D2 path; under G (carve-out only) they wait, and only the money moves.
