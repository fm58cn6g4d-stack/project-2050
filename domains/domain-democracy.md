# Domain file: Democracy — representation, elections and civic life (DEM)


**October 23 approval and timing amendment.** DEM-1 floor consideration begins no later than March 2029. The normal sequence follows early R1 signature and its benefits announcement, but an R1 delay must trigger an explicit recovery decision rather than automatically postpone DEM-1 beyond March. The spring-2030 enactment target and mid-2030 outer deadline remain.

Congressional terms begin January 3, before the presidential term begins January 20 (Twentieth Amendment). Organizing, rules, introduction, committee work, scoring and agreed legislative preparation can proceed before inauguration. Other priorities may reach the floor early and move in parallel; R1 and DEM-1 are not a serial gate on the rest of the agenda. Pre-drafted and agreed text can accelerate consideration, subject to actual procedures, votes and floor capacity. Congressional action before inauguration does not assume the incoming President already holds office.

## DEM-1. The electoral and civil-rights package

**Design.** One bill, three titles plus a funding title.

- **Title I, the federal floor (Freedom to Vote lineage):** early voting, no-excuse mail voting, same-day registration, automatic registration **at every federal benefit-delivery point (DEM-1E; see below)**, standardized list-maintenance procedure with a ban on partisan purges, paper ballots with risk-limiting audits, certification as a ministerial act hardening what the ECRA left as ordinary statute, and mandatory GSA ascertainment triggers for presidential transitions.
- **Title II, preclearance (John Lewis VRAA):** a modern coverage formula built on recent violations, answering *Shelby County*.
- **Title III, the Callais response:** restores a results test for Section 2, authorizes race-conscious remedial districting, and carries the Fifteenth Amendment enforcement record the remedy needs.
- **Title IV:** the 6:1 small-dollar match, designed in domains/domain-integrity.md (INT-5). Under INT-5B it is **paid from general revenue**, with no separate matching channel or fines surcharge. It rides this bill because the ledger carries it here.

**Why one bill.** Title III is the piece that must be in force before the 2031 maps, and it is the piece with the least independent constituency. Attaching it to the floor provisions, which poll well and have a decade of drafting behind them, is the whole reason for the package shape. D1 makes this must-pass by mid-2030.

**Cost.** About $0.01T, range $0.00T to $0.03T (estimate), all of it the small-dollar match, a general-fund cost. The floor provisions impose costs on states that DEM-11 funds.

**Legal exposure.** Title II's coverage formula is written for a Court that struck the last one: the record has to be contemporaneous, violation-based and non-formulaic in the way *Shelby County* said the 1965 formula was not. Title III faces Fourteenth Amendment strict scrutiny of race-conscious remedies — per the *Callais* syllabus, Section 2 now imposes liability only on a strong inference of intentional discrimination, which is in effect the intent standard Congress overrode in 1982. The restoration direction remains chosen, but Section 2 already contains results language and Callais supplies constitutional as well as statutory reasoning. A textual re-override and 1982 history alone cannot establish constitutional sufficiency. Develop a contemporary record, actual liability/proof/remedy clauses and separable protections with specialist review; no guarantee of judicial survival is claimed.

**Timing (DEM-1F, adopted Oct 20; October 23 timing clarification).** DEM-1 floor consideration begins no later than March 2029; normally after early R1 signature and its benefits announcement. The design rule 4 bridge: announce DEM-1 together with R1's benefits. **The cost, stated plainly:** registration provisions take time to land (the model's Texas Valley turnout recovered only in 2032), so a later enactment helps 2030 turnout less. ## DEM-2. Redistricting commissions and the mid-decade ban

**Design.** Independent commissions required for congressional maps in every state, under the Elections Clause — the approach *Rucho* invited when it closed the federal-court route and *Moore v. Harper* protected when it rejected the independent state legislature theory. Paired with a ban on mid-decade congressional redistricting, which is reciprocal by construction: it binds whichever party holds a legislature when the temptation arrives.

**The 2034 exception.** D1 moves House size (DEM-3) and STV (DEM-4) to 2033 with a one-off 2034 reapportionment and redraw. The ban must therefore carry an express exception for a congressionally mandated redraw, or the 2030 bill forecloses the 2033 agenda. This is the single most important drafting interaction in the file.

**Cost.** About $0.00T, range to $0.01T (estimate) — commission start-up grants only.

**Held: state legislative districts.** Extending the commission requirement to state maps (staging 2.6) has no Elections Clause hook, and the spending-power lever is *NFIB*-exposed. It stays a state-track item, not a federal mandate.

**Urgency.** Texas, California, Missouri and North Carolina adopted new maps; Florida and Tennessee redrew after *Callais*; Virginia's map was blocked from certification (Ballotpedia, May 2026; NCSL). The ban is the row whose value decays fastest if it slips past 2030.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Effect | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DEM-2A (chosen) | Commissions plus the ban, with the 2034 exception | Reciprocal; preserves the 2033 agenda | estimate |
| DEM-2B | Ban only | Cheapest and most bipartisan-capable; leaves partisan commissions in place | estimate |
| DEM-2C | Commissions plus a ban with no exception | Cleanest reciprocity story; kills DEM-3 and DEM-4 | estimate |
| DEM-2D | Add conditional funding for state legislative maps | Reaches the bigger problem; NFIB exposure | estimate |
| DEM-2E (Talarico stress test; open) | Substantive criteria (compactness, communities of interest, partisan-symmetry limits) enforced by a three-judge district court backstop, with independent commissions as a voluntary **safe harbor** rather than a mandate; the ban with the 2034 exception kept; all titles severable | Less reach than A; closer to what the model's Court upheld, so more likely to survive | scenario; estimate |

**On E.** The scenario's lesson is that "redistricting timing is not invertible": the 2031 maps are drawn by legislatures and governors elected in 2030 (a Democratic governor of Texas would hold a veto over 38 House seats). E is the fallback if A's commission mandate looks exposed when drafted; it is not a substitute for winning the 2030 governorships.

## DEM-3. House enlargement

**Design.** The House rises to about 692 members on the cube-root rule, with automatic decennial recalculation — about 480,000 constituents per member against about 760,000 now (Instruments and Consequences #1; scaled, arithmetic not re-checked). **Second-term package with DEM-4 and DEM-9:** run on it in 2032, enact in 2033, apply through the one-off 2034 reapportionment and redraw.

**Chamber capacity.** The chamber seats about 448. A reconstruction or distributed-voting architecture has to start in the first Congress for a 2034 seating to be physically possible. This is time-limited capital, so once scored it can be proposed for the D4-B investment envelope. Unscored; a Capitol-scale construction program is plausibly $0.01-0.03T (estimate) but needs an Architect of the Capitol figure before it goes in any total.

**Cost.** About $0.01T, range $0.00T to $0.02T (estimate) for member offices and staff, plus the unscored capital line.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Size | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DEM-3A (chosen) | Cube-root rule, ~692, automatic recalculation | 692 | scaled |
| DEM-3B | Wyoming rule: every district the size of the smallest state's population | ~585 | scaled (the old ledger figure) |
| DEM-3C | A fixed increase (e.g. to 600) with no formula | 600 | estimate |

**Why A.** The automatic recalculation is the durable instrument: B ties House size to the smallest state's growth rate, which is an arbitrary driver, and C needs re-legislating every time it binds.

**Open.** A mid-decade *reapportionment* would be unprecedented, though within Congress's apportionment power (my judgment; needs legal review). That review should happen before 2032, not after the election is run on it.

## DEM-4. Fair Representation Act (single transferable vote)

**Design.** Repeal the single-member district mandate at 2 U.S.C. sec. 2c and require multi-member districts elected by STV: five members wherever population permits, three only where geography forces it. Magnitude is the variable that decides whether third parties actually appear (Instruments #2), so the five-member default is the policy, not a detail.

**Cost.** None federally.

**Package decision (Espen, Oct 19).** DEM-4 is handled together with DEM-3 and DEM-9 in the second term. They are one package, so STV never ships into a plurality-president configuration by design. The residual risk is that the amendment half (DEM-9) fails while the statutory half passes; the options below say what happens then.

**Hard holds that remain.**

1. **Section 2 modeling must be rerun on the post-*Callais* baseline** before the package is final. STV changes what minority representation looks like, and the interaction with a narrowed Section 2 is unmodeled. This is staging's ledger cut C14.
2. **DEM-9 failure fallback must be chosen before 2032**, because the run-on-it-in-2032 plank needs to say what happens if the amendment stalls. DEM-4C and DEM-4D are the two fallbacks.

**Interim path while the amendment is slow.** The compact re-enactment with RCV operative language (DEM-8, ✔ at 51, state-track) delivers a de facto presidential method without Congress or the amendment, if enough states re-enact. It is the bridge that makes the package less hostage to ratification.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Effect | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DEM-4A (chosen) | Five-member STV districts, enacted with DEM-3 and DEM-9 in 2033, 2034 maps | Multi-party dynamics in the House, with the presidential method moving alongside | estimate |
| DEM-4B | Three-member districts | Lower threshold for the states that resist; much weaker third-party effect | estimate |

## DEM-5. D.C. statehood

**Design.** Admission act for the state, with the residual federal district's electors directed to the national popular vote winner or left unappointed. The 23rd Amendment gives Congress authority over the manner of appointment (Instruments #7), so the statutory fix travels inside the admission act and repeal of the amendment (AMD-6, formerly staging 17.6) becomes hygiene rather than a precondition.

**Cost.** None in-window beyond transition administration.

## DEM-6. Puerto Rico status

**Design.** A binding status plebiscite with a congressional pre-commitment to honor the result. The pre-commitment is the operative part: previous plebiscites failed on Congress's unwillingness to be bound in advance.

**Cost.** About $0.00T, range to $0.01T (estimate), plebiscite administration and transition planning. Statehood's fiscal consequences (full Medicaid and SNAP participation, tax treatment) are large and are not scored here or in the ledger. **Open item:** if the plebiscite produces statehood, the fiscal effect belongs in the health and revenue files, and nobody has sized it.

## DEM-7. Territorial, tribal and procedural representation

**Design.** Three pieces, second wave: voting delegates for Guam, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Marianas, sequenced rather than gestured at; tribal congressional representation with enforceable consultation standards; and a codified statehood admission procedure so admission stops being ad hoc.

**Why the procedure matters.** Codifying admission is the reciprocity move in this group: it is the rule we would accept in the minority, and it removes the "they will just admit states whenever they are losing" objection to DEM-5 and DEM-6.

**Cost.** None material in-window.

## DEM-8. The National Popular Vote Compact

**Design.** A three-part state track.

- **Reach 270.** 19 jurisdictions with 222 electoral votes have joined; 48 more are needed. The live targets are Arizona, Michigan, New Hampshire, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin (65 EV together). Sourced: nationalpopularvote.com, April 2026; NPR, Apr 14, 2026 on Virginia's signing; the Daily Signal, Oct 3, 2026, still shows 48 short.
- **Do not ask for congressional consent.** Pass a resolution confirming none is required, and hold consent in reserve as a litigation response. Under *U.S. Steel v. Multistate Tax Commission*, asking concedes that consent is required (Instruments #6).
- **Tighten the withdrawal blackout window,** the compact's softest joint. This needs re-enactment by member states, which is the same legislative lift as DEM-9's operative language, so the two should travel together.

**Cost.** None federally.

**Note.** This is the only major democracy item that is ✔ at 51, because it does not run through the Senate at all. It is also the one with the shortest path to a 2028 effect, and under the package decision it is the interim path for DEM-9.

## DEM-9. Presidential election method

**Design.** National ranked-choice voting or a two-round runoff for the presidency, written both into the popular-vote amendment (AMD-3 in domain-amendments.md, which holds the amendment vehicle; this row holds the method) and into the compact's operative language. The compact as enacted awards electors to the plurality winner, so the compact route needs member-state re-enactment, not just the amendment.

**Status.** ✖ at any Senate size as an amendment. **Part of the second-term package with DEM-3 and DEM-4** (Espen, Oct 19): run on it in 2032, enact in 2033. Presidential primary calendar reform (staging 2.16), now set by party rules, rides with it as a statutory companion. Because ✖ means it needs 60, 67 or Republican votes regardless of Senate size, the package's weakest link is this row; DEM-4C and DEM-4D are the fallbacks, and the compact re-enactment is the interim.

## DEM-10. The state multi-party track

**Design.** Fusion voting authorization (banned in most states) and ranked-choice voting with open primaries, framed as anti-corruption. Both are state-level, ✔ at 51, and both are deposits toward DEM-4: they build the operational experience and the legal record that an STV bill would otherwise have to create from nothing.

**Cost.** None federally.

## DEM-11. Election administration funding

**Design.** A permanent, indexed federal line for election administration, distributed by formula, replacing the episodic HAVA-style grant cycle. Capacity first: DEM-1's floor provisions impose real costs on county election offices, and a mandate without the money is the standard way a federal floor fails in practice.

**Cost.** About $0.02T, range $0.01T to $0.04T (estimate) — roughly $1-4B a year. **Unscored and flagged:** no source in the project files sizes this, and the estimate is mine from the scale of HAVA appropriations. It needs a real figure before it enters the ledger as anything but a placeholder. The EAC's own administrative capacity is a separate allocation (about $3B) inside the D9 agency line (domain-capacity.md CAP-7), not part of this formula funding.

**Instrument.** Mandatory funding if a reconciliation-eligible committee can carry it (unchecked for Byrd eligibility); otherwise annual appropriations, which is the weaker form and the reason the durability rule prefers the first.

## DEM-12. Re-enfranchisement

**Design.** Federal felony re-enfranchisement on release for federal elections, plus polling access and voting rights for pretrial detainees, which is consistent with the danger-not-wealth pretrial rule in domain-public-safety.md SAF-7 (Safe Everywhere plank 7).

**Cost.** None material.

## DEM-13. Time to vote

**Design.** Election Day as a federal holiday, or a mandatory paid-time-to-vote requirement. The paid-time version reaches hourly workers the holiday misses, since a federal holiday mostly moves federal and salaried workers.

**Cost.** About $0.00T, range to $0.01T (estimate) for the federal-workforce holiday cost. The mandate's cost falls on employers, not the general fund, and so enters the D3 test only through wages — a second-order effect not modeled.

## DEM-14. Election-worker protection (pointer)

Canonical in domains/domain-public-safety.md, SAF-8 (Safe Everywhere plank 8): threat-assessment grants, address privacy, a threat statute fitting the 2023 true-threats standard, and dedicated prosecutors. The old inventory's separate criminal-penalty and private-cause-of-action rows are options there, not here. Listed in this file only so the domain's coverage is complete. ## Civic life (DEM-15 to DEM-20)

Staging section 15 (civic infrastructure) lives in this file rather than a file of its own, because four of its six rows are about how citizens take part in self-government (assemblies, initiatives, local self-rule, civic education), which is this file's subject. National service (staging 15.1) is canonical in domain-labor.md as LAB-9, where it sits beside apprenticeships and the care workforce; its statutory mixing requirement (Instruments #14) travels with it, and DEM-15 is kept as a pointer so the ID stays stable.

**World state (Oct 2026).**

- **Libraries.** The administration moved to dismantle the Institute of Museum and Library Services in 2025. A settlement filed Apr 9, 2026 commits it to no reductions in force and continued statutory research (Bloomberg Government).
- **Ballot initiatives.** Legislatures in the 24 initiative states passed 51 bills restricting the process in 2025, against an average of 34 a year in 2018-23 (Fairness Project, via the Boston Globe, Apr 8, 2026). Examples:
  - North Dakota, South Dakota and Utah put 60% supermajority requirements on the November 2026 ballot.
  - Missouri referred a measure requiring citizen amendments to win in every congressional district, and repealed a voter-approved sick-leave law.
  - Florida's new rules left all 22 proposed 2026 initiatives off the ballot.
  - Counter-measures protecting the process have qualified or filed in Arkansas, Missouri, Montana (CI-133) and Nebraska (Ballotpedia, July 9, 2026).

Listed here so the civic-life coverage is complete.

### DEM-16. Civic education

**Design.** Formula grants to states for civic education, with funds tied to **participation outcomes** (youth registration and turnout, local-meeting participation, student-government and service-learning access), not to curriculum content. Content stays with states and districts, which keeps the program out of the curriculum fights and within the federal role.

**Cost.** About $0.01T, range $0-0.02T (estimate). **Vehicle.** Appropriations, or a small authorization riding the DEM-1 bill. ◐

### DEM-17. Public space, libraries and community infrastructure

**Design.** A capital fund for libraries, parks, community centers and public plazas, weighted toward places with low fiscal capacity, with a maintenance-endowment match so that buildings are not opened and then abandoned. The Institute of Museum and Library Services' statutory programs are kept, and the fund is administered through it and the Interior Department's outdoor recreation grants.

**Cost.** About $0.03T, range $0.02-0.05T (estimate), as **time-limited capital**: a fixed number of years of grants, then the fund closes. **D4:** it is borrowed for inside the D4-B investment envelope (C32, accepted in Decision Brief 1), so it takes no revenue from the base; the ledger shows it as borrowing-financed.

**Vehicle.** Capital grants as direct spending in R3 (Byrd unchecked) ✔, or appropriations. Pairs with CAP-13's state and local capacity grants and domain-housing.md's community investments.

### DEM-18. Citizens' assemblies

**Design.**

- **Executive (✔):** agencies may convene randomly selected citizens' assemblies on questions referred to them, with an evaluation published each time. Day-one guidance from OMB.
- **Statute (◐):** Congress may refer up to three questions per Congress to national assemblies, with a guaranteed committee hearing on each recommendation. The recommendations are advisory, and the statute says so.
- **Grants:** small grants for state and local assemblies (inside DEM-16's line).

**Cost.** About zero (estimate).

### DEM-19. Protect the citizen initiative

**Design.** A state track, ✔. Support constitutional amendments in initiative states that:

- protect the initiative and referendum power as a right (Montana CI-133);
- require a supermajority for a legislature to repeal or weaken a voter-approved measure (the Nebraska and Arkansas designs);
- bar legislatures from raising thresholds or adding geographic-distribution rules without voter approval (the Missouri design).

**Why there is no federal row.** The Guarantee Clause is nonjusticiable (*Luther v. Borden*; *Pacific States Telephone v. Oregon*, my knowledge). Congress has no clean hook to protect a state's initiative process. The work is state-level, and the November 2026 results in the four counter-measure states set the starting map.

### DEM-20. Local self-government

**Design.** A state track, ✔. Model legislation limiting **punitive** preemption: laws that fine or remove local officials for local ordinances, or that preempt a subject without setting a state standard. Ordinary preemption, where a state sets its own standard, is left alone. The design follows the reciprocity rule: a state-level Democratic majority should be bound by it too.

**Note.** Federal grant conditions that override state preemption (for example on housing or transit) are designed in domain-housing.md and Moving America, not here.
