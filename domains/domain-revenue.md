## Chosen design

Revenue is planned **by channel**. Each dedicated fund is kept whole on its own terms (D4), and the general fund pays for everything else. No new trust fund is created only to hold a pay-for. A program gets a dedicated fund only when it is a permanent benefit with a natural revenue stream that covers much or all of its cost. Paid leave qualifies; most programs don't.

**Accounting rule (added Oct 19).** Every instrument in the agenda is accounted for and visible, whatever channel it sits in. Channel revenues and outlays are counted **separately** from general-fund inflows and outflows — never netted against them, and never netted against each other. A channel that raises money and spends it on its own benefit still shows both halves at gross. Not touching the general fund is a reason to count an instrument in its own column, not a reason to leave it out of the agenda's accounts. The general-fund gap is computed from the general-fund column alone.

### REV-G11: full mark-to-market, in the base in place of G4 (decided Oct 19)

**Design.** The model is Wyden's Billionaires Income Tax (2021 draft, reintroduced in November 2023). It covers people with $100M or more of income, or more than $1B of assets, for three straight years.
- **Tradable assets:** stocks and other traded assets are taxed on each year's gain, and each year's loss is deducted, whether or not anything is sold.
- **Non-tradable assets:** real estate and private business stakes are taxed only when sold. The seller then pays a deferral charge, interest at the short-term federal rate plus 1 point, on the tax postponed while the asset was held.
- **Transition:** the first year's built-up gain can be paid over five years.
- **Founders:** up to $1B of one company's stock can be treated as non-tradable.

**Revenue.**

| --- | --- | --- |
| JCT preliminary: $557B, with $346B of it in the first five years because of the transition | 2022-31 | sourced |
| About $0.55T to $0.8T, mid $0.65T. Enacted in 2029, the transition years fall inside the window, and billionaire wealth has grown since 2021 | 2030-39 | estimate |

**How it fits.** It would **replace G4**, not stack on it, since both tax the same unrealized gains of the same households. The swap adds about $0.07T at the midpoint. It also shrinks G6 (gains at death) somewhat, since gains already taxed each year are no longer untaxed at death. So the base would move by roughly zero to +$0.1T.

**Legal footing.** It is more defensible than a wealth tax, but not safe:
- **Wealth tax:** an annual tax on net worth is the hardest case. Most scholars read it as a "direct tax," which the Constitution requires to be apportioned among the states by population. Apportionment is unworkable for a wealth tax.
- **Mark-to-market:** it taxes income (gains), so it does not need apportionment if it falls under the Sixteenth Amendment. The open question is whether income must be *realized* to count. In *Moore* (2024) the Court upheld a tax on undistributed foreign earnings attributed to shareholders and expressly declined to decide the realization question. Two justices (Thomas, Gorsuch) said realization is required, and two more (Barrett, Alito) signaled doubt.
- **Precedent on the government's side:** the code has long taxed unrealized gains in narrower settings. Examples are futures contracts (section 1256), securities dealers (section 475) and the exit tax on people who give up citizenship (section 877A).
- **Severability:** only the yearly tax on tradable assets turns on realization. The deferral charge on non-tradables is collected at sale, which is ordinary realization. A severability clause that falls back to the deferral-charge method for all assets would keep most of the revenue if the yearly piece fell.

G4 has the same realization exposure, because its minimum tax also counts unrealized gains. G11's fallback structure arguably makes it the sturdier of the two. Its legal risk is **Medium-High**, versus G4's High.

**Decided Oct 19 (ledger C22):** G11 replaces G4 in the base. Design rule 7 already prefers mark-to-market to a wealth tax, and G11 carries a built-in fallback that G4 lacks. The pairing rule carries over: G11 never pays alone for a permanent program. G4 is now listed under options not chosen.

**Stress test (Oct 20, Talarico scenario).** The scenario's mark-to-market line raises about $1.1T over ten years against G11's $0.65T, because it reaches further down ("above a high threshold") and is not haircut for overlap (scenario figure, not a score). Its own soft-spot note is the useful part: capital gains parity, mark-to-market and stepped-up basis repeal enacted together have never been tried, and their combined static scores "could be off by a third either way." The base already pairs G11 with G6 under a 15% haircut (GX); the scenario supports keeping that haircut rather than shrinking it, and supports a joint behavioral score before any further top-end row is stacked.

## The household net test (D3)

D3 allows any instrument if, for every income group below the top, the agenda's combined taxes, fees and benefits leave households better off on net. New general-fund revenue must come mostly from the top 1% and corporations. In practice:

1. **The unit is the agenda, by income quintile, plus the top 10%, top 1% and top 0.1%.**
2. **Count every instrument that reaches the middle:** fuel-tax steps, the carbon fee's residual after the dividend, the paid-leave contribution, the public-safety channel's alcohol tax, any broad-based option chosen below, and the incidence of corporate taxes on workers (25% under the JCT/CBO convention).
3. **Count every benefit:** the Child Tax Credit, child care, health premium and cost-sharing savings, the carbon dividend **at its post-carve-out size**, paid leave, the Social Security pieces and Moving America's service gains (where quantifiable).
4. **Pass/fail:** each of the bottom four quintiles must come out positive on net, by year five (2034) and by year ten. The revenue mix passes if the top 1% and corporations bear more than half of new general-fund revenue.
5. **Report:** a published table, shown alongside every financing choice.

The accounting rule matters here: instruments netted out of the ledger are the ones most likely to be missed when the incidence model is built, and five of the ten channels reach the middle directly.

### Tariff note (rechecked Oct 19, after *Learning Resources*)

- **The ruling:** in *Learning Resources v. Trump* (Feb 20, 2026, 6-3) the Court held that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs. About $166B of IEEPA duties is subject to refund.
- **What replaced them:**
  - A 10% Section 122 surcharge ran from Feb 24 until it expired on July 24.
  - Section 301 tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on about 60 economies took effect July 24, plus 25% on Brazil.
  - Section 232 tariffs (steel and aluminum at 50%, autos at 25%, and others) stand.
- **Size:** the effective rate was about 7.1% in August. CRFB puts the new 301 and 338 tariffs at about $950B through FY2036, under 60% of the lost IEEPA revenue. Tax Foundation puts the 232 tariffs at about $635B over a decade.
- **What it means for the ledger:** current tariffs are in CBO's baseline, so keeping them raises **no new revenue**. The old "keep current tariffs, $1.5-2.5T" row was an avoided loss, not a pay-for, and it is retired. The live question runs the other way: repealing or narrowing the 232 and 301 tariffs (staging inventory row 5.12) would be a new general-fund claim of up to roughly $1.5-1.9T over 2030-39 (estimate, scaled from the two figures above). Any narrowing must be paid for under D4.
- **REV-C2 is not a tariff in this sense.** The border adjustment is priced off embodied carbon at the domestic fee's rate, is new revenue rather than baseline revenue, and is counted in its own channel.

## Options added Oct 20 (education, climate durability and Talarico reviews)

### REV-E1. The estate tax at 2009 parameters (ledger option C35)

**Design.** Restore the estate tax to its 2009 parameters, a **$3.5M exemption and a 45% top rate**, with the exemption **frozen in nominal terms** rather than indexed. The education session's point about indexing: estates grow with asset values at roughly 5-6% a year, so a frozen threshold makes revenue grow about 5% a year (as fast as wage-indexed spending), price indexing about 3%, and wage indexing shrinks real revenue over time. Freezing is automatic and needs no future vote, but it is a quiet compounding tax increase and would be the first piece amended.

**Revenue: counted as the increment beyond G9 (restated Oct 20).** About **$0.15T, range $0.05-0.25T** (estimate). The gross figure for 2009 parameters against current law is about $0.35T (range $0.25-0.45T); the education session sizes the estate component at $30-50B a year *including* an end to stepped-up basis, and the step-up piece is already REV-G6 ($0.70T, in the base).

**Why the increment, not the gross.** REV-G9 reverses the 2025 tax law's relief above $450K, sized as TPC's share of that relief going to the top. The 2025 law made a **$15M estate exemption permanent** (indexed from 2026), which is part of that relief. JCT scored the estate provision at about $20B in FY2027 against the old law's expiration ([ACTEC](https://www.actec.org/capital-letter/one-big-beautiful-bill-commentary)); carried over 2030-39 that is about $0.2-0.3T (scaled), already inside G9's $1.45T. G9 therefore takes the exemption back to roughly $7M. REV-E1 adds only the step from about $7M to a frozen $3.5M and the rate from 40% to 45%. **G9's own note now says so**, and the ledger carries a conflict row so the two are never summed at their gross figures. If G9 is ever designed to exclude the estate tax, REV-E1 reverts to its gross figure.

**D3.** Falls on roughly the top 0.2% of estates; scenario H in the incidence model moves no quintile below the top. **D4.** As general-fund revenue it is unrestricted. Dedicating it to an education fund (EDU-9B's design) would be a pay-for placed in a fund, which D4 bars.

**Use.** At the increment it covers a little over half of the education batch's +$0.275T; it is no longer a one-row answer to the education claims. Legal risk low.

### REV-E2. A public investment corporation (one version of two proposals)

**Two second versions, folded.** The climate durability session proposes a national investment corporation taking equity in clean steel, cement, minerals and grid equipment, with returns to the dividend fund. The Talarico scenario's "Prosperity Fund" (2032 in the model) is a sovereign wealth fund capitalized by equity stakes taken in exchange for access to public goods: compute access, data licensing, interconnection priority, and later spectrum and federally funded R&D. Both are public equity with returns paid out per person; under the one-version rule they are one option here.

**Design (option).** A federally chartered corporation that (1) takes equity in strategic clean-industry and grid projects alongside CLI-9's contracts and DOE loans, and (2) receives equity or royalty stakes as a condition of specified federal grants of access (interconnection priority under CLI-6, federally funded IP, spectrum). Returns go to the Climate Dividend's payment channel (CLI-2), itemized on the same notice, which is also AI-6's recommended channel. Precedent the session cites: the current administration's own equity stakes in private firms.

**Fiscal treatment.** Capitalization is scored at subsidy cost; the session counts about $0.10T of subsidy cost across its lending and equity instruments, against about $1T or more of balance-sheet commitments (session estimate). Dividends to households are small in-window; the Talarico model's per-adult dividend grows from about $340 to $1,150 only in the 2040s, after equity-for-access is extended at scale (scenario figures).

**Why it is interesting beyond climate.** The Talarico model's most consequential late finding is that labor's share of income fell from 58% to 51% over its eight years and that the fund was "the resolution of the labor share problem, found four years late." Public equity is the instrument in this file that grows with capital income rather than wages. **Not chosen:** unscored, novel governance, and the Court's treatment of exactions conditioned on federal permits needs a legal review (CRT-8). **Vehicle:** capitalization R2 (✔); charter and access conditions need the floor (◐).

### REV-ED1. The education contribution (held; behind option EDU-9B)

An uncapped payroll contribution of about **1.28-1.4 points**, dedicated to an education fund (domain-education.md EDU-9B), structurally separate from Social Security so it can ride reconciliation. The education session's model needs 1.28 points with an estate component that includes step-up repeal; counting only REV-E1 (since G6 is already in the base) raises the rate to about 1.4 (estimate). In-window it raises about $1.95-2.30T (mid ~$2.1T; session base of about $135B a point in 2030 growing 4%; CRFB scores one uncapped point at $1.3T for 2026-35). It reaches every worker, about $830-900 a year at the median wage if all of it falls on the worker. **Held:** it needs Espen's call under D3 (scenario G passes the quintile test, but a fourth-quintile household without children pays the contribution and gets no school benefit) and D4 (whether a dedicated education fund meets the trust-fund rule). It is the same base as W1 (a general-fund 1% payroll contribution), dedicated.

**Note for a later revenue strategy session (Espen, Oct 20).** EDU-9B's structure is worth revisiting as one of several payroll instruments, possibly instead of or harmonized with the others, rather than on its own. Its structure, for that session:
- **Base and rate:** an uncapped payroll contribution of about 1.28-1.4 points, split between employer and employee (the session's design), with no wage cap.
- **Dedication:** receipts go to a separate education fund, not Social Security, so the fund can ride reconciliation; the fund would carry **borrowing authority from the Treasury and a statutory repayment schedule**, so spending can ramp before receipts.
- **Pairing:** designed with an estate component; at the REV-E1 increment it needs the higher end of the rate range.
- **Open D3 and D4 questions:** a fourth-quintile household without children pays and receives no school benefit; whether a dedicated fund meets D4's natural-revenue test.

## Vehicle and floor time

Every general-fund revenue row can ride reconciliation; G8 is counted in the base but may not be credited by an official score on any given vehicle (see its note). The base splits across vehicles:
- **R1:** G1, G7 and G9, paired with health restoration and the Child Tax Credit.
- **R2:** G3, G11, G5, G6, G10 and the carbon fee (REV-C1), paired with Medicare upgrades, child care, paid leave, education and climate. REV-E1, if chosen, belongs here with the education rows (and with G9's estate piece, which it extends).
- **R3:** REV-C2 with the Thriving Economy add-ons it finances, and whatever closes the gap.

REV-C2 rides R3 with CLI-5 so the revenue and the spending it covers move on the same bill, which the accounting rule and the legibility rule both favour. The revenue rows need no regular-order floor days. Social Security's revenue (REV-O1, REV-O2) is barred from reconciliation by section 310(g) and rides the regular-order Social Security bill.
