## Chosen design

Revenue is planned **by channel**. Each dedicated fund is kept whole on its own terms (D4), and the general fund pays for everything else. No new trust fund is created only to hold a pay-for. A program gets a dedicated fund only when it is a permanent benefit with a natural revenue stream that covers much or all of its cost. Paid leave qualifies; most programs don't.

**Accounting rule.** Every instrument in the agenda is accounted for and visible, whatever channel it sits in. Channel revenues and outlays are counted **separately** from general-fund inflows and outflows — never netted against them, and never netted against each other. A channel that raises money and spends it on its own benefit still shows both halves at gross. Not touching the general fund is a reason to count an instrument in its own column, not a reason to leave it out of the agenda's accounts. The general-fund gap is computed from the general-fund column alone.

### REV-G11: full mark-to-market, in the base in place of G4

**Design.** The model is Wyden's Billionaires Income Tax (2021 draft, reintroduced in November 2023). It covers people with $100M or more of income, or more than $1B of assets, for three straight years.
- **Tradable assets:** stocks and other traded assets are taxed on each year's gain, and each year's loss is deducted, whether or not anything is sold.
- **Non-tradable assets:** real estate and private business stakes are taxed only when sold. The seller then pays a deferral charge, interest at the short-term federal rate plus 1 point, on the tax postponed while the asset was held.
- **Transition:** the first year's built-up gain can be paid over five years.
- **Founders:** up to $1B of one company's stock can be treated as non-tradable.

**Revenue.**

| Score | Window | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| JCT preliminary: $557B, with $346B of it in the first five years because of the transition | 2022-31 | sourced |
| About $0.55T to $0.8T, mid $0.65T. Enacted in 2029, the transition years fall inside the window, and billionaire wealth has grown since 2021 | 2030-39 | estimate |

**How it fits.** It **replaces G4**, not stacks on it, since both tax the same unrealized gains of the same households. It also shrinks G6 (gains at death) somewhat, since gains already taxed each year are no longer untaxed at death; that interaction is inside GX.

**Legal footing.** It is more defensible than a wealth tax, but not safe:
- **Wealth tax:** an annual tax on net worth is the hardest case. Most scholars read it as a "direct tax," which the Constitution requires to be apportioned among the states by population. Apportionment is unworkable for a wealth tax.
- **Mark-to-market:** it taxes income (gains), so it does not need apportionment if it falls under the Sixteenth Amendment. The open question is whether income must be *realized* to count. In *Moore* (2024) the Court upheld a tax on undistributed foreign earnings attributed to shareholders and expressly declined to decide the realization question. Two justices (Thomas, Gorsuch) said realization is required, and two more (Barrett, Alito) signaled doubt.
- **Precedent on the government's side:** the code has long taxed unrealized gains in narrower settings. Examples are futures contracts (section 1256), securities dealers (section 475) and the exit tax on people who give up citizenship (section 877A).
- **Severability:** only the yearly tax on tradable assets turns on realization. The deferral charge on non-tradables is collected at sale, which is ordinary realization. A severability clause that falls back to the deferral-charge method for all assets would keep most of the revenue if the yearly piece fell.

G4 has the same realization exposure, because its minimum tax also counts unrealized gains. G11's fallback structure arguably makes it the sturdier of the two. Its legal risk is **Medium-High**, versus G4's High.

**In the base (ledger C22).** G11 replaces G4. Design rule 7 already prefers mark-to-market to a wealth tax, and G11 carries a built-in fallback that G4 lacks. The pairing rule carries over: G11 never pays alone for a permanent program. G4 is listed under options not chosen.

**Stress test (Talarico scenario).** The scenario's mark-to-market line raises about $1.1T over ten years against G11's $0.65T, because it reaches further down ("above a high threshold") and is not haircut for overlap (scenario figure, not a score). Its own soft-spot note is the useful part: capital gains parity, mark-to-market and stepped-up basis repeal enacted together have never been tried, and their combined static scores "could be off by a third either way." The base already pairs G11 with G6 under a 15% haircut (GX); the scenario supports keeping that haircut rather than shrinking it, and supports a joint behavioral score before any further top-end row is stacked.

## The carbon border adjustment (REV-C2)

**Design.** A border adjustment on the embodied carbon of imports, set to the domestic fee's rate, with rebates on exports. Separately counted from REV-C1 so that its revenue has one use.

| Item | Ten-year effect | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Border adjustment | $0.15T to $0.25T (mid $0.20T) | estimate, scaled from the Thriving Economy map's $120-240B for the border adjustment and methane fees together |
| Methane fees | Unsized; outside REV-C1 either way | Thriving Economy; needs a figure |

**Use.** REV-C2 finances the Thriving Economy add-ons (CLI-5) directly. CLI-5's financing is therefore the 20% REV-C1 transition share (about $0.27T) plus REV-C2 (about $0.20T) plus methane fees, against add-ons of about $0.3T to $0.6T. CLI-5's capital lines (adaptation finance and minerals offtake, about $0.10T, estimate) are borrowed for inside the D4-B envelope (ledger C37, accepted in place of cut C29), so CLI-5 is kept whole. The add-ons are time-limited, which suits a base that shrinks as import carbon content falls — the same logic that keeps permanent programs off the carbon channel.

**Why this resolution.** The audit found the border adjustment counted twice: once inside REV-C1's gross and again as E4's "own revenue." The alternative was folding it into REV-C1 and trimming the add-ons to the 20% share alone, which would have added about $0.30T to the general-fund gap. Carving it out instead resolves the double count **with no effect on the general-fund gap**: the same dollars move from the dividend to the add-ons rather than onto the general fund. The cost is the $0.16T of dividend above, which is a distributional cost rather than a fiscal one.

**Open:** the $0.15-0.25T split between the border adjustment and methane fees is my estimate from a combined figure. Sizing the two separately would firm up both REV-C2 and CLI-5.

**Options (climate durability session).**
- **REV-C2B: a clean product standard.** Carbon-intensity limits on steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizer and glass sold in the United States, domestic and imported alike, with Buy Clean procurement. Trade-law compatible because it treats domestic and imported goods the same; it pairs with CLI-9's contracts for difference and builds the measurement machinery that later reaches power. Regulatory, so it needs the floor (◐) and raises no revenue. The session rates an industrial carbon-intensity standard with a border fee as the most plausible bipartisan emissions law of the 2030s, because U.S. goods are generally less carbon-intensive than Chinese ones.
- **Make the U.S. price count abroad.** Carbon-club agreements under which REV-C1 counts against the EU's border charge, so repeal would immediately cost U.S. exporters (a lock-in tool); the 2032 USMCA joint review is a natural opening for carbon-intensity terms.
- **Export rebates** on energy exports, including LNG, to avoid trade disputes (session). Already implied by "rebates on exports" above; LNG should be named.

## The household net test (D3)

D3 allows any instrument if, for every income group below the top, the agenda's combined taxes, fees and benefits leave households better off on net. New general-fund revenue must come mostly from the top 1% and corporations. In practice:

1. **The unit is the agenda, by income quintile, plus the top 10%, top 1% and top 0.1%.**
2. **Count every instrument that reaches the middle:** fuel-tax steps, the carbon fee's residual after the dividend, the paid-leave contribution, the public-safety channel's alcohol tax, any broad-based option chosen below, and the incidence of corporate taxes on workers (25% under the JCT/CBO convention).
3. **Count every benefit:** the Child Tax Credit, child care, health premium and cost-sharing savings, the carbon dividend **at its post-carve-out size**, paid leave, the Social Security pieces and Moving America's service gains (where quantifiable).
4. **Pass/fail:** each of the bottom four quintiles must come out positive on net, by year five (2034) and by year ten. The revenue mix passes if the top 1% and corporations bear more than half of new general-fund revenue.
5. **Report:** a published table, shown alongside every financing choice.

The accounting rule matters here: instruments netted out of the ledger are the ones most likely to be missed when the incidence model is built, and five of the ten channels reach the middle directly.

**Model status.** The test runs in index/revenue-household-incidence-model.md (generated). On the decided package (scenario Q: Package B, C38 and C39-C41, no trims) every quintile below the top comes out ahead; the fourth by about **+$1,268** a household a year on the ten-year average, and 72% of new general-fund revenue comes from the top 1% and corporations. The fourth quintile is still behind in 2030 (about −$1,539) and positive from 2031; that early dip is the remaining D3 exposure. REV-E1 (scenario H) moves no quintile below the top. REV-ED1 used as a closer (scenario G) passes the quintile test but leaves the fourth quintile slightly worse than scenario B. ### Combined top rates (stacking above $400K)

| Income above $400K | Rows that stack | Combined federal rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wages | 39.6% income (G3) + 12.4% Social Security (SS-1) + 5% Medicare (H1b) | About 57% |

- **Upper end:** that is near Diamond and Saez's revenue-maximizing 73%, which counts federal, state, payroll and sales taxes. It is well above Mankiw, Weinzierl and Yagan's 48% (revenue tab).
- **Revenue risk:** past the peak, avoidance can eat the scored revenue.
- **The haircut runs across channels.** REV-GX takes 15% off G11, G6, G9 and H1b, and ledger row OX takes 15% off SS-2 in the trust fund (about −$0.17T, or −0.2 to −0.24 points of payroll). The D8 baseline then closes about 65-72% of the 4.42% gap, or about 86-92% with SS-5.
- **Why the rates stay for now.** The usual escape from high rates on gains is holding assets until death. G6 (gains taxed at death) and G11 (yearly mark-to-market at the very top) largely close that, so a combined rate near 37-39% on gains is more defensible here than under current law. Revisit after the incidence and behavioral model.
- **Ways to ease the stacking (options, not decided):**
  - Set SS-2 at 6.2%, not 12.4%. SS-2 is the investment-income tax in domains/domain-social-security.md. This costs about half its revenue, roughly 0.65-0.8 points of payroll, which a closer would have to replace.
  - Drop H1b and keep H1a for HI. This costs the base $0.5T.
  - Phase SS-1 in as a donut that starts at $400K, not $250K.
  - Drop the +2 points on capital gains from C18 (about $0.13T), keeping the top rate on gains near 37%.
  - Score SS-2 and H1b jointly with behavior (replacing the flat 15% haircut).
  - SS-10's bipartisan design drops SS-2 entirely (domain-social-security.md), which would remove the investment-income stacking at a cost to the trust fund.
  
  Any of these needs re-scoring with behavior. The stacking is also the strongest argument the opposition will use, so the file should carry a published effective-rate table.

### Tariff note (after *Learning Resources*)

- **The ruling:** in *Learning Resources v. Trump* (Feb 20, 2026, 6-3) the Court held that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs. About $166B of IEEPA duties is subject to refund.
- **What replaced them:**
  - A 10% Section 122 surcharge ran from Feb 24 until it expired on July 24.
  - Section 301 tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on about 60 economies took effect July 24, plus 25% on Brazil.
  - Section 232 tariffs (steel and aluminum at 50%, autos at 25%, and others) stand.
- **Size:** the effective rate was about 7.1% in August. CRFB puts the new 301 and 338 tariffs at about $950B through FY2036, under 60% of the lost IEEPA revenue. Tax Foundation puts the 232 tariffs at about $635B over a decade.
- **What it means for the ledger:** current tariffs are in CBO's baseline, so keeping them raises **no new revenue**. A "keep current tariffs" row would be an avoided loss, not a pay-for, so none is counted. The live question runs the other way: repealing or narrowing the 232 and 301 tariffs (staging inventory row 5.12) would be a new general-fund claim of up to roughly $1.5-1.9T over 2030-39 (estimate, scaled from the two figures above). Any narrowing must be paid for under D4.
- **REV-C2 is not a tariff in this sense.** The border adjustment is priced off embodied carbon at the domestic fee's rate, is new revenue rather than baseline revenue, and is counted in its own channel.

## Further options (education, climate durability and Talarico reviews)

### REV-E1. The estate tax at 2009 parameters (ledger C35, accepted in Package B)

**Design.** Restore the estate tax to its 2009 parameters, a **$3.5M exemption and a 45% top rate**, with the exemption **frozen in nominal terms** rather than indexed. The education session's point about indexing: estates grow with asset values at roughly 5-6% a year, so a frozen threshold makes revenue grow about 5% a year (as fast as wage-indexed spending), price indexing about 3%, and wage indexing shrinks real revenue over time. Freezing is automatic and needs no future vote, but it is a quiet compounding tax increase and would be the first piece amended.

**Revenue: counted as the increment beyond G9.** About **$0.15T, range $0.05-0.25T** (estimate). The gross figure for 2009 parameters against current law is about $0.35T (range $0.25-0.45T); the education session sizes the estate component at $30-50B a year *including* an end to stepped-up basis, and the step-up piece is already REV-G6 ($0.70T, in the base).

**Why the increment, not the gross.** REV-G9 reverses the 2025 tax law's relief above $450K, sized as TPC's share of that relief going to the top. The 2025 law made a **$15M estate exemption permanent** (indexed from 2026), which is part of that relief. JCT scored the estate provision at about $20B in FY2027 against the old law's expiration ([ACTEC](https://www.actec.org/capital-letter/one-big-beautiful-bill-commentary)); carried over 2030-39 that is about $0.2-0.3T (scaled), already inside G9's $1.45T. G9 therefore takes the exemption back to roughly $7M. REV-E1 adds only the step from about $7M to a frozen $3.5M and the rate from 40% to 45%. **G9's own note says so**, and the ledger carries a conflict row so the two are never summed at their gross figures. If G9 is ever designed to exclude the estate tax, REV-E1 reverts to its gross figure.

**D3.** Falls on roughly the top 0.2% of estates; scenario H in the incidence model moves no quintile below the top. **D4.** As general-fund revenue it is unrestricted. Dedicating it to an education fund (EDU-9B's design) would be a pay-for placed in a fund, which D4 bars.

**Use.** General-fund revenue in Package B, about $0.15T at the increment. Legal risk low.

## Vehicle and floor time

Every general-fund revenue row can ride reconciliation; G8 is counted in the base but may not be credited by an official score on any given vehicle (see its note). The base splits across vehicles:
- **R1:** G1, G7 and G9, paired with health restoration and the Child Tax Credit.
- **R2:** G3, G11, G5, G6, G10 and the carbon fee (REV-C1), paired with Medicare upgrades, child care, paid leave, education and climate. REV-E1 (C35) belongs here with the education rows (and with G9's estate piece, which it extends).
- **R3:** REV-C2 with the Thriving Economy add-ons it finances, and whatever closes the gap.

REV-C2 rides R3 with CLI-5 so the revenue and the spending it covers move on the same bill, which the accounting rule and the legibility rule both favour. The revenue rows need no regular-order floor days. Social Security's revenue (REV-O1, REV-O2) is barred from reconciliation by section 310(g) and rides the regular-order Social Security bill.

## Dependencies

- **Durable revenue:** the pairing rule assumes R1 happens (D5). If the FY2029 slot is lost, G1, G7 and G9 move to R2 with their benefits.
- **The test needs a model:** the household net test runs in index/revenue-household-incidence-model.md; every middle-reaching option goes through it before it is final.
- **D7:** on the chosen path REV-C1 raises $1.20-1.50T and REV-C2 $0.15-0.25T, about $1.35-1.75T together. REV-C1D (convergence) stays open; REV-C1E departs from D7 and is not recommended.
- **D4 (D4-B):** the accepted borrowing lines (C21, C30-C32, C37, C42) sit inside the investment envelope; time-limited climate capital may be borrowed for (see the summary at the top).
- **HI:** solvency relies on REV-H1a and HI's share of the Medicare Advantage savings; see domains/domain-health.md. D4 is read on a 75-year window for HI, so the roughly $0.3T surplus is kept as reserve rather than swept.
- **Unsized channels:** methane fees need a figure.
- **G8 and CAP-7S/CAP-7:** the enforcement revenue here and the IRS share of the D9 agency-rebuild lines move together.
- **Education:** REV-E1 (accepted as C35) and REV-ED1 (held) are the two financing options the education file names; EDU-9B (the fund) needs D3 and D4 decisions.
- **Climate:** REV-E2 is the public-equity home for CLI-9 to CLI-11's equity pieces; REV-C2B pairs with CLI-9.
