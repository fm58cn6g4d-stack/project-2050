# Domain file: Public safety (SAF)

Canonical domain file. It converts *Safe Everywhere: A Democratic Public Safety Platform* (Oct 11, 2026; domains/public-safety-safe-everywhere.md) into IDs, survival tags and vehicles. Its nine planks become SAF-1 to SAF-9, and its delivery design becomes SAF-10. It folds in Bill Book 16 (universal background checks) and Bill Book 24 (cannabis, sentencing, policing, reentry) as the governing doc directs. It replaces the old ledger rows S3, S4 and S5 and staging rows 3.6, 5.10, 13.9 and 19.4-19.6 on substance.

**Safe Everywhere stays the evidence base.** Its research tables, coalition analysis, messaging and objections are not restated here. Read it for *why*; read this file for *what, how and how much*. It sits in domains/ as this file's evidence base; moving it to sources/ is proposed (open item 3).

All figures are ten-year totals for 2030-39 in trillions unless marked. Basis tags: **sourced**, **scaled**, **estimate** (mine). Safe Everywhere's own dollar figures are "proposals for debate, not sourced estimates" (its words), and are tagged **proposal** here. Survival at 51 (Dec. D1): ✔, ◐, ✖.

## Three choices on funding and revenue

1. **Funding is mandatory spending in R3, not annual appropriations.** Appropriations need 60 votes in the Senate (◐), get renegotiated every year, and fail design rule 2 and Safe Everywhere's own "Built to last" section. SAF-10A makes the Compact stream mandatory, a formula grant with a permanent authorization and five-year reviews, and rides R3 with its revenue package. Formula grants to states are direct spending and can be reconciled. The Judiciary Committee has received reconciliation instructions before (2021, my knowledge). **Byrd eligibility for each title is unchecked.** If a title fails the check, it falls back to appropriations (SAF-10B).
2. **The deployment-savings piece of the revenue package is probably not revenue.** D9 holds defense roughly flat in real terms. Ending domestic deployments frees money *inside* the defense budget. It moves nothing to public safety unless the defense topline is cut by the same amount. The savings sit inside the flat defense line (NSC-1), so counting them in PS1 as well would count them twice. **Recommendation:** leave the deployment piece out of PS1 (as the ledger does), or book it as a matching cut to NSC-1. The bill tab sizes it at about $1.1B a year (CBO: $93M a month for ongoing deployments) and itself rates it "low as a lasting source," since the savings last only while deployments would otherwise continue. Section 1207's bar on routine domestic deployments stays in the bill as policy. Only its revenue credit is dropped. The figures above already exclude it.
3. **The Crime Victims Fund receipts are a reallocation, and the trust fund conflicts with D4.**
   - *Section 1206* deposits unobligated False Claims Act recoveries (up to $0.75B a year) in the Crime Victims Fund. Those recoveries otherwise reach the Treasury, so the deposit moves general-fund money; it does not raise any. The bill tab itself calls the line volatile ($6.8B of recoveries in FY2025, under $3B the year before). The deposit can stay as a funding mechanism for SAF-7's victim-services floor. It is counted as a general-fund cost, not channel revenue.
   - *Section 1208* creates a Safe Communities Trust Fund to hold the Section 1201-1205 receipts. D4 bars a new trust fund "just to hold a pay-for." Design rule 2 allows one only where a permanent benefit has a *natural* revenue stream. An alcohol excise paying for clearance grants is a pay-for, not a natural stream. **Recommendation:** drop Section 1208. Fund the titles as mandatory spending (SAF-10A) and send the receipts to the general fund, attributed to the program in the statute (legibility). That is the same structure the revenue audit set for every other channel. It also answers the bill tab's own caution that dedicating these receipts to discretionary programs "would take a trust fund or mandatory funding."

## SAF-1 to SAF-9: designs

Each plank's chosen design is Safe Everywhere's text. Below are the IDs, the vehicle split, and the second versions folded in.

**SAF-1. Solve the crime.** The VICTIM Act (Booker-Kennedy) is the bipartisan base, funded well above its $60M authorization. Grants ride R3 through SAF-10. Quarterly clearance publication is a condition on the grants, so it rides with them. ✔ for the money, ◐ for anything binding agencies that take no grant.

**SAF-2. Police staffing and standards.**
- *Hiring grants:* R3, weighted to investigators and to patrol in high-violence areas, with the five-year federal-share step-down. ✔
- *Standards:* decertification registry, body cameras and use-of-force data, all as grant conditions. ✔ inside R3 where they are conditions on the money.
- *Liability:* agency liability without qualified immunity, the disciplinary-review trigger, and limits on arbitration. These need the floor. ◐
- *Domestic deployments:* one statute, shared with domain-executive-power.md (staging 5.10). Not restated.
- *Second version folded:* Bill Book 24 carries "qualified-immunity reform" and a registry from the George Floyd Act lineage. Safe Everywhere's employer-liability design (the agency pays, the officer answers through licensing) is chosen. The George Floyd Act's personal-liability version is **SAF-2B**.

**SAF-3. Focus on the few.** Restore the $811M in grants cancelled in April 2025 (Safe Everywhere, sourced). Violence intervention gets multi-year funding with independent evaluation and an end date for programs that fail. All of it is grant money and rides R3. ✔

**SAF-4. Fix dangerous places.** The Safe Blocks fund rides R3. ✔ Design note from Safe Everywhere's evidence: promise specific deployments and modest gains (pooled studies put greening at 5-6%).

**SAF-5. Keep guns from dangerous people.**
- *Universal background checks:* checks on all sales and transfers, including private sales, the Bipartisan Background Checks Act model.
- *Charleston loophole (folded in from Bill Book 16):* NICS review window extended from 3 to 10 business days, with stronger state reporting into NICS.
- *Incentives:* grants for states that adopt purchaser licensing and red-flag laws with due process. These ride R3. ✔
- *Enforcement:* trafficking and straw-purchase prosecution, conversion devices and untraceable guns.
- *Vehicle:* the checks and the NICS window need the floor. ◐
- *Write for this Court:* after *Bruen* (2022) and *Rahimi* (2024), the record should rest on historical analogues for disarming dangerous people, which *Rahimi* accepted (my knowledge). Background checks on commercial sales have not been held to violate *Bruen*. Private-transfer checks are the more exposed piece.
- *Options:* **SAF-5B**, checks on commercial and gun-show sales only (the narrower 2013 Manchin-Toomey shape, more votes).
- *Second version folded:* Bill Book 16 and old ledger S4 are this row; S4 rides the rules bill (the ledger's SAF-1 row) under C25.

**SAF-6. Public order with treatment.** Treatment beds, crisis teams and retail-theft task forces are funded through SAF-10. Enforcement policy belongs to localities, so the federal role is the money and its conditions. ✔ The coalition bargain is "no enforcement without an offer." Treatment and shelter capacity is funded first, and penalties rise only with repeat offending. That is written in as a grant condition.

**SAF-7. Swift courts and victims.**
- *Funding:* courts, prosecutors and defenders through SAF-10. ✔
- *Federal pretrial standard:* danger, not wealth, for federal cases, with a model for states. ◐
- *Crime Victims Fund:* stabilized by a deposit fix (◐ if it changes the deposit statute; ✔ if done as direct spending in R3, unchecked). Compensation is paid within 30 days.
- *Reentry:* ID, treatment and a job lead on release; restored benefits access is shared with SAF-11.

**SAF-8. Democracy's front line.**
- *This is the home for election-worker protection* (DEM-14 points here; staging 3.6).
- *Grants:* threat-assessment matching grants and home-security funding for officials, judges and election workers. R3. ✔
- *Floor items:* address privacy and the data-broker bar; threats prosecutors in every U.S. Attorney's office; a model threat statute fitted to *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023); armed-intimidation bans. ◐
- *Under G:* the election-worker pieces fit the democracy carve-out if they ride DEM-1. **Recommendation:** put the election-worker half of SAF-8 in DEM-1 as well as here, so it survives path G. The other officials stay in this bill.
- *Norms:* the no-pardons pledge and one standard for both sides are declared norms (X, ✔). A statutory pardon limit is EXE's question (staging 5.8).

**SAF-9. Fraud, cybercrime and identity theft.**
- *This is the home for staging 13.9* (AI-enabled fraud). domain-ai.md points here.
- *Floor items:* bank and payment-app duties to flag and hold high-risk first transfers, shared loss on missed warnings, carrier anti-spoofing duties, platform verification of financial advertisers, crypto-kiosk limits, a federal AI-impersonation offense, consent verification for voice cloning, and credit freezes by default. ◐
- *Funding:* state and regional cyber-fraud units through SAF-10. ✔
- *Revenue:* the fraud-carrier assessment (part of PS1) falls on institutions, not households.
- *Bipartisan base:* the Strategic Task Force on Scam Prevention Act and the Senate national-strategy and sanctions bills (Safe Everywhere, sourced).

## SAF-10. Safety Compacts (delivery)

**Design.** Safe Everywhere's "How the plan delivers" section, made a mandatory formula program:

- **Plans:** a locality writes its plan from local data, and a compact board signs off.
- **Funding:** one flexible stream per locality, weighted by violence burden and fiscal capacity, with a floor for every state and a rural regional track. Each compact runs five years.
- **Incentives:** 10% is reserved for evaluated new ideas, with improvement bonuses against each place's own baseline.
- **Failure:** help comes before a penalty.
- **Accountability:** the six-measure scorecard and the anti-gaming rules (real clearances only, audited data, no single-number bonus, fairness as a condition of funds, an independent evaluator, surveillance-technology policies).
- **Durability:** a five-year federal-share step-down for new hires, an automatic stabilizer when state revenue drops or shootings spike, and a supplement-not-supplant rule.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SAF-10A (chosen) | Mandatory formula funding in R3 with permanent authorization and five-year reviews | ✔; durable; needs a Byrd check title by title |
| SAF-10B | Annual appropriations (the old ledger S5 design) | ◐; renegotiated every year |
| SAF-10C | Mandatory core (SAF-1, SAF-3, SAF-8 grants) with the rest appropriated | Splits the difference if some titles fail the Byrd check |
| SAF-10D | The bill tab's lean version: Titles I, III, VIII and IX whole; II, VI and VII at about a third | About $8B a year (proposal); revenue then covers it without a general-fund residual. Ledger C34; **declined Oct 20 (Decision Brief 2)** |

## SAF-11. Federal sentencing, cannabis and reentry (Bill Book 24)

**Design.**

- **Sentencing:** retroactive crack-powder parity (EQUAL Act), a broader safety valve from mandatory minimums, and expanded compassionate release.
- **Cannabis:** descheduled, with automatic expungement of federal records, resentencing and reinvestment (the MORE Act lineage).
- **Reentry:** ban-the-box in federal hiring and contracting, and restored access to benefits, housing and student aid after release.

**World state.** Executive Order 14370 (December 2025) directed the Schedule III rulemaking. On April 22, 2026, DEA moved marijuana in FDA-approved products and state-licensed medical programs to Schedule III. A hearing on broader rescheduling ran June 29 to July 15, 2026, and the ALJ's recommendation is pending. All other marijuana stays in Schedule I (Morgan Lewis, Sept 24, 2026).

**Why it is here.** Safe Everywhere's organizing idea is certainty over severity. Added years at the back end deter little (its evidence table). Sentencing reform belongs in the same bill as clearance. It is the "no new mandatory minimums" half of the coalition bargain, made concrete.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SAF-11A (chosen) | Sentencing and reentry, plus cannabis descheduling | Bill Book 24; cannabis brings some libertarian-right votes (its war-game note) |
| SAF-11B | Sentencing and reentry; cannabis to Schedule III by statute with SAFE Banking and 280E relief, no descheduling | Matches where the executive is heading; less to defend in frontline seats |
| SAF-11C | Cannabis as a separate bill | Keeps the cannabis vote from carrying the sentencing provisions, or the reverse |

**Revenue note.** A federal cannabis excise (descheduling makes one possible) is the bill tab's optional Section 1209: $0.8-5.3B a year, about $2.0B in the middle case. That would close the public-safety residual. The tab's own advice is to pass legalization separately and let this bill name cannabis receipts as an eligible source. That is SAF-11C, and it pairs naturally with the tab. The excise is not in the base and would enter the D3 test.

## Survival at 51 (D1 stress test)

- **At 51 with no D2 path:** if SAF-10A's Byrd check holds, the R3 funding (most of SAF-1, SAF-2's grants, SAF-3, SAF-4, SAF-6, SAF-7's funding, SAF-8's grants and SAF-9's units) survives along with its revenue package. That is most of the money and none of the rules.
- **Under F (D2, decided):** the Safe Everywhere Act (6 days) can pass on Democratic votes alone. Background checks and liability are the defection risks.
- **Under G (the written fallback):** the rules bill fails, except the election-worker half of SAF-8 if it rides DEM-1. SAF-9's anti-scam strategy and SAF-1's VICTIM Act have bipartisan sponsors and could form a smaller 60-vote bill (my judgment).

## Revenue and the D3 test

- **Alcohol excise, $16 per proof gallon on all beverages (Section 1201).** About $7B a year, scaled from CBO's 2016 option ($70B over ten years); a current score would differ. It is regressive and reaches most households, so it is the largest public-safety item in the D3 test. It is counted once, here (ledger conflict row). The bill tab names the left's alternative: fund the bill from general revenue raised on high incomes and drop the excise taxes. That is option **SAF-R2**. It moves about $0.07T onto the top-end base and out of the D3 test.
- **Fraud-prevention assessment (Section 1202).** $1.2B a year, scaled to fraud losses on each firm's network. It falls on institutions and may be passed through to customers.
- **Firearms excise doubled (Section 1203) and a $10 background-check fee (Section 1204).** About $0.6B and $0.3B a year. Both fall on buyers, and the check-volume assumption is unverified. Legal exposure after *Bruen* is moderate. Excise taxes on firearms are long-standing (Pittman-Robertson). A per-check fee is the more exposed piece, since it charges for exercising the right itself (my judgment). The bill tab flags that both cost votes. The existing excise revenue stays with wildlife conservation.
- **Kiosk and data-broker fees (Section 1205).** About $0.1B, "rough guess" (the tab).
- **Deployment savings and Crime Victims Fund receipts.** Removed from the channel; see changes 2 and 3.
