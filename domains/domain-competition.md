## Where things stand (world state, Oct 2026)

- **The FTC after *Slaughter*.** *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 29, 2026) was itself about an FTC commissioner. FTC commissioners are now removable at will, so FTC rulemaking and enforcement priorities can change with each president. Anything that should last goes in statute, with enforcement that does not depend on the FTC alone (state AGs, private suits, DOJ).
- **Non-competes.** The FTC's 2024 rule never took effect. A Texas court blocked it in August 2024, and the FTC stopped defending it in 2025. The FTC now acts case by case: Gateway Services (about 1,800 workers), Rollins (over 18,000), warning letters to health-care employers, and a January 2026 workshop. Four states ban non-competes outright, and Washington becomes the fifth in 2027 (McDermott, 2026).
- **Junk fees.**
  - **Still in force:** the FTC's fee rule for live-event tickets and short-term lodging, in effect since May 2025. The FTC announced a $10M StubHub settlement under it in April 2026.
  - **Struck down:** the FTC's click-to-cancel rule, vacated by the Eighth Circuit on July 8, 2025 on procedural grounds (AGG).
  - **Gone at the CFPB:** the overdraft rule was repealed under the Congressional Review Act in 2025, and the credit-card late-fee rule was vacated the same year (per my knowledge; not re-checked this session).
  - **States:** California, Colorado, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Oregon and Virginia require all-in pricing across most consumer transactions (Troutman, Apr 2026).
- **CFPB.** On June 19, 2026 the en banc D.C. Circuit upheld the injunction against dismantling the bureau (*NTEU v. Vought*). The injunction bars firing more than 1,000 employees, and the trial court is reviewing a plan to cut about two-thirds of staff (Berkeley Center for Consumer Law, June 2026). The Justice Department has argued that the bureau cannot draw Federal Reserve funds when the Fed has no "combined earnings" (NOTUS, 2025).
- **Merger review.** About 2,000 HSR filings a year and 41 second requests in FY2025 (Skadden, Aug 2026).
  - **2026 fees:** $35,000 for deals under $189.6M up to $2.46M for deals of $5.869B or more (Akin, 2026).
  - **Fee retention:** DOJ Antitrust could not keep $49M of excess fees in FY2025 (GCR).

## COM-1. Merger law

**Design.** Modeled on CALERA (Bill Book 13):
- **Incipiency:** the Clayton Act standard becomes "an appreciable risk of materially lessening competition."
- **Burden shift:** for deals above about $5B (indexed), or by a dominant firm, the merging parties must show the deal does not harm competition.
- **Structural presumptions:** the 2023 Merger Guidelines' presumptions go into statute, so they no longer depend on which guidelines an administration keeps. (The FTC kept the 2023 Guidelines in force in February 2025; not re-checked since.)
- **Nascent rivals:** acquisitions of nascent competitors by dominant firms get closer review.

**Why statute.** After *Slaughter* the guidelines are one administration's choice. Statutory presumptions bind courts regardless of who runs the agencies.

**Options.** COM-1B: guidelines-level presumptions only, with no burden shift. It is the smaller change and has more Republican interest (Bill Book 13 calls antitrust "the one bill on the board with genuine populist-right reach").

## COM-2. Enforcers and the merger-review channel

**Design.**
- **Fees:** the HSR fee schedule rises about 2x across the tiers, and the FTC and DOJ Antitrust keep every dollar they collect. That ends the cap that cost Antitrust $49M in FY2025.
- **Monetary relief:** FTC Act section 13(b) is amended to authorize restitution and disgorgement in federal court. *AMG Capital* (2021) removed that power, the FTC's main tool for returning money to defrauded consumers.

**Channel.**
- **Current collections:** roughly $0.3-0.4B a year (estimate: about 2,000 filings across the 2026 fee tiers, weighted toward the lower tiers).
- **Doubling the schedule:** adds about $0.3-0.4B a year, or about **$0.004T over 2030-39** (estimate).
- **Spending:** that money funds an enforcement increase of the same size, so the channel nets to zero and the general fund carries nothing at the midpoint.

Ledger revenue row MF1 carries this figure.

**Vehicle.** Fees are offsetting collections and could ride R2 if a reconciled committee has jurisdiction (unchecked). The baseline keeps them in the floor bill with the rest of COM-1.

**Note.** The channel is small. "Funded by merger fees" pays for enforcers, not for anything else in the agenda.

## COM-3. Dominant platforms

**Design.** Modeled on the American Innovation and Choice Online Act (Bill Book 13):
- **Self-preferencing:** covered platforms (by user count and market capitalization) may not favor their own products, discriminate among business users, or block interoperability and data portability.
- **Enforcement:** by DOJ, the FTC and state AGs.

**Options.** COM-3B: interoperability and portability only, with no self-preferencing ban. Platform lobbying is the heaviest of any provision in this file.

## COM-4. Labor-market competition (the non-compete ban's one home)

**Design.**
- **Non-competes:** banned for all workers by statute, on the Workforce Mobility Act model, with a sale-of-business exception. Existing clauses become unenforceable on enactment. The design follows the vacated FTC rule but does not depend on FTC rulemaking authority, the issue the Texas court decided against it.
- **No-poach:** no-poach and wage-fixing agreements are per se violations.
- **Monopsony:** labor-market harm is an express merger-review factor and an enforcement priority.
- **Enforcement:** the FTC, DOJ, state AGs, and a private right of action for workers.

**Second version folded here.** The go-big health bill's Title V carried its own non-compete ban, and staging 11.2 and Bill Book 13 carried another. One home: COM-4. domain-health.md HLT-5 should reference it, not restate it.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| COM-4A (chosen) | Full ban, sale-of-business exception | Matches the four states with full bans |
| COM-4B | Ban below an income threshold (e.g., about $150,000, indexed) with notice rules above it | Illinois-style; easier votes; keeps executive non-competes |
| COM-4C | Health care first: ban for clinicians and health workers, everyone else later | The FTC's own current focus; strongest rural-access argument |

## COM-5. Junk fees and consumer protection

**Design.** Bill Book 14, in statute:
- **All-in pricing:** the advertised price must include every mandatory fee, across all consumer goods and services. This sets a federal floor; states may go further.
- **Click-to-cancel:** cancellation must be as easy as sign-up for any subscription.
- **Bank fees:** overdraft fees are limited to cost or capped, and credit-card late fees get a low safe harbor.
- **Other fees:** no airline family-seating fees, and telecom and cable bills must be itemized.
- **Enforcement:** FTC civil penalties for first violations, state AG enforcement, and a private right of action.

The statute names the protection in its title and requires the "all-in price" label at checkout, so people know where it came from (legibility rule).

**Why statute.** Every federal version of these protections except the ticket and lodging rule was undone in 2025, by court, by the CRA, or by abandonment. Rules made by an FTC whose commissioners are now removable at will will not outlast an administration.

**@51.** ◐ in the baseline. Junk fees have real cross-party support: the Trump FTC enforced the ticket rule against StubHub, and red and blue states have passed all-in pricing. COM-5 is the piece of this bill likeliest to clear 60 votes as a standalone if D2 fails. Option COM-5B splits it out for that purpose.

## COM-6. CFPB durability

**Design.**
- **Funding:** clarify that the CFPB draws its funding from the Federal Reserve System's operating expenses whether or not the Fed has "combined earnings." That answers the Justice Department's 2025 position. *CFPB v. CFSA* (2024) upheld the funding structure itself.
- **Staffing:** a statutory staffing and examination floor.
- **Restored rules:** the overdraft and late-fee protections are restored through COM-5 rather than left to rulemaking.
- **Director:** the single director stays, removable at will since *Seila Law* (2020).

**Cost.** About zero to $0.01T (estimate). Funding the CFPB from the Fed reduces Fed remittances to Treasury, which scores as a cost. Whether CBO's baseline already assumes full funding is unchecked.

**Options.** COM-6B: a five-member commission with partisan balance instead of a director, which slows swings in direction. After *Slaughter*, commissioners have no removal protection either.

## Survival at 51 (D1 stress test)

Everything is one floor bill and needs a D2 path. Under G (carve-out only) it fails as a whole. Two pieces could still find 60 votes as a smaller bill: COM-5 (junk fees) and possibly COM-4C (health-care non-competes). The fee increase in COM-2 could ride R2 if the jurisdiction check works.
