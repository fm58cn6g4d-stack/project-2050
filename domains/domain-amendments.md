# Domain file: Constitutional amendments and Article V (AMD)

Canonical. This file covers constitutional amendments in ratification order, the Article V convention, and the state-level ratification operation. On substance it replaces staging inventory section 17. Where an amendment constitutionalizes a statute that lives in another domain file, that file keeps the statute and this file keeps the amendment:

| Amendment | Statute it constitutionalizes |
| --- | --- |
| AMD-2 (term limits) | CRT's term-limits statute |
| AMD-3 (popular vote) | DEM-8 (compact) and DEM-9 (presidential method) |
| AMD-6 (23rd Amendment repeal) | DEM-5 (D.C. statehood) |

Survival at 51 (Dec. D1): every amendment is ✖ at any Senate size, because Article V needs two-thirds of both chambers and 38 states. The state-track rows are ✔. The convention-procedure statute is ◐ (it passes under D2's decided path, F). No amendment has a general-fund cost.

## The premise

Realistically this list holds about one plausible amendment in twenty years (staging inventory, from the old inventory). So the list is ordered by **coalition breadth**, not importance. The first ratification is a proof of concept: if it cannot reach 38 states, none can. That makes the first amendment a diagnostic as well as a goal.

## Where things stand (world state, Oct 2026)

- **Convention of States.** Twenty states have passed the Convention of States application. Kansas was the 20th, in early 2026. At 34, Article V says Congress "shall call" a convention (Washington Examiner, July 2, 2026). Counts for the separate balanced-budget campaign are disputed because of aggregation and rescission questions (my knowledge; not re-sourced).
- **Anti-corruption amendment.** American Promise counts 25 states that have formally called on Congress for an amendment on money in politics. Its For Our Freedom Amendment would let Congress and the states regulate campaign contributions and spending and distinguish natural persons from artificial entities (American Promise prospectus, 2026). *NRSC v. FEC* (June 30, 2026) struck party coordinated-expenditure limits (domain-integrity.md), which widens the gap an amendment would close.
- **Term limits.** 79% support (Marquette, Sept 2-9, 2026; via staging 17.2). Welch-Manchin (2024) and H.J.Res. 174 (119th Congress) are the drafting lineage.
- **Popular vote.** 19 jurisdictions with 222 electoral votes have joined the compact; 48 more are needed (DEM-8's sources).

## AMD-1. Anti-corruption and campaign finance (the pathfinder)

**Design.** The For Our Freedom Amendment text, or a close variant: Congress and the states may reasonably regulate and limit contributions and spending in elections, and may distinguish natural persons from artificial entities. Its first section names the interests (self-government, political equality, electoral integrity) so that courts read the regulatory power broadly.

**Why first.** It has the broadest cross-partisan support of any structural amendment (75-80% in the old inventory's sourcing). It has no small-state disadvantage, so the small-state veto that kills AMD-3 does not apply. And a quarter of the states are already on record.

**Pairing (design rule 4).** It travels with INT-5's small-dollar match and INT-2's disclosure (domain-integrity.md), the statutory half that works now.

**Option AMD-1B.** A first-Congress recorded vote, about 1 floor day. It will fail, but it puts every senator on record before 2030.

## AMD-2. Judicial term limits

**Design.** Eighteen-year staggered terms, a vacancy every two years, prospective for justices appointed after ratification, and Article III tenure and salary kept for service on lower courts after the term. This constitutionalizes the courts file's statute, removing the Article III objection that statute carries.

**Sequencing.** The statute comes first (first Congress, ◐). The amendment backs it up and moots the challenge. If the statute is struck down, the courts file's held response (expansion as a pre-committed deterrent) is the fallback, not this amendment, because ratification takes years.

## AMD-3. National popular vote with a majority method

**Design.** Bayh-Celler lineage: direct election of the president, with the presidential method designed in DEM-9 (national ranked-choice voting or a two-round runoff) written into the amendment. DEM-9 holds the method design; this file holds the amendment vehicle.

**Coalition note.** The real opposition is about seven swing states, not 25 small ones; small states are already ignored in presidential campaigns. The compact (DEM-8) is the faster route and is ✔ at 51. AMD-3 is the durable one.

**Tie to the second-term package (D1).** DEM-3, DEM-4 and DEM-9 move together in 2033. The package's weakest link is the amendment half. DEM-4C and DEM-4D are the fallbacks if AMD-3 stalls, and the compact re-enactment with ranked-choice operative language is the interim.

## AMD-4. Senate suspensive veto (held)

**Design.** Article V's equal-suffrage proviso stays intact: every state keeps two senators and an equal vote. What changes is what the Senate can do. The House may override a Senate rejection, or a failure to act, by a supermajority after a defined delay, on the model of the UK's Parliament Act 1911.

**Why held.** It is the largest structural gain available and the highest legal and political variance. The equal-suffrage proviso may be read to protect the Senate's *powers* as well as its seats (my judgment; contested). It is held as a long-horizon option and is not sequenced.

## AMD-5. An affirmative right to vote

**Design.** An individual right to vote in every public election, with Congress empowered to enforce it. *Bush v. Gore* (2000) said there is no such federal right for presidential electors. This is the floor under DEM-1's statutory protections. It appeared twice in the old inventory and appears once here.

## AMD-6. 23rd Amendment repeal

Hygiene. DEM-5's admission act handles the residual district's electors by statute (Instruments #7), so repeal is cleanup, not a precondition for D.C. statehood.

## AMD-7. Presidential immunity (contingent)

Only if *Trump v. United States* (2024) proves unworkable. Congress cannot overrule it by statute. EXE carries the statutory pieces (evidentiary procedures, IG strengthening, criminal statutes that expressly reach presidential conduct). Lineage: the 2024 "No One Is Above the Law" proposal.

## AMD-8. Legislative override (held)

A supermajority to override judicial invalidation of a statute, or a supermajority of the Court to invalidate one. The statutory version is likely unconstitutional and carries the highest escalation risk (Rebalancing #17). It exists only as a piece of a larger amendment package and is not sequenced.

## AMD-9. Convention procedure statute

**Design.** A statute setting the rules Article V leaves open, written so that we would accept them whoever calls the convention (design rule 1):

- **Counting:** which applications count, how subject-limited applications aggregate (only applications on the same subject count toward that subject's 34), and rescission (a state may rescind before the count is reached).
- **Delegates:** apportionment by state and selection by the state legislature, with one vote per state.
- **Scope:** the convention may consider only the subject the applications named, and Congress will not send a non-conforming proposal to the states.
- **Ratification:** Congress chooses ratifying conventions or legislatures under Article V, with a deadline.

**Why it is more urgent now.** The Convention of States campaign (a broad "fiscal restraints, federal power, term limits for Congress" call) reached 20 of 34 in 2026. Without a statute, the first fight over a convention call would be decided by whichever Congress holds the count, on rules written in the moment. A neutral statute is defensive: it protects against a runaway convention from either direction.

**Vehicle.** FL. ◐: passes under F (decided). Some Republicans have sponsored convention-procedure bills before (my knowledge), so this may be one of the few structural bills that could also pass at 60.

**Write for this Court.** Whether Congress can bind a convention is untested. *Coleman v. Miller* (1939) treats ratification procedure as a political question (my knowledge), which favors deference to a congressional statute.

## AMD-10. Ratification operations

**Design.** State-level and continuous:

- name the 38 target states for AMD-1 and sequence them by coalition, not geography;
- model legislation and an interstate legislator coordination body;
- bilateral candidate pledges extracted in primaries.

This is ✔: no federal vote. It runs alongside DEM-8's compact campaign, which targets some of the same legislatures.

## AMD-11. Convention applications as leverage (hold until AMD-9)

**The idea (old inventory).** The 17th Amendment moved through Congress partly because a convention call sat within a state or two of triggering. Subject-limited applications with rescission triggers keep that pressure without risking a runaway convention.

**The new risk.** With one campaign at 20 of 34 and aggregation rules unsettled, a Democratic application drafted as "subject-limited" could be counted by a hostile Congress toward a broader call. **Recommendation:** do not file new applications until AMD-9 sets aggregation and rescission rules by statute. Until then the leverage is the threat of applications, not the applications themselves.
