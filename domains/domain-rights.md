## Where things stand (world state, Oct 2026)

- **Abortion bans.** Nearly half the states ban or severely restrict abortion. Medication abortion is more than 60% of abortions nationwide (Reuters factbox, Jan 2026).
- **Shield laws.** About 20 states have laws blocking enforcement of other states' bans. Texas and Louisiana passed laws in 2025 letting private citizens sue anyone who mails abortion pills into or out of those states (Reuters).
- **Medication abortion.** Louisiana v. FDA (filed Oct 2025) challenges the 2023 change that allowed prescription by telehealth and mail. Per Bloomberg Law (June 23, 2026), the Supreme Court preserved mail access "for now." The FDA is reviewing the 2023 REMS changes, and in December 2025 it put the review off until after the November 2026 midterms (Reuters, citing Bloomberg). A revoked or modified REMS could restore in-person dispensing. So a 2029 Congress may inherit either access or its removal.
- **Preemption by FDA approval.** In July 2025 the Fourth Circuit rejected GenBioPro's argument that FDA approval preempts West Virginia's ban (Reuters). Any protection for medication abortion therefore has to be written into statute, expressly.
- **LGBTQ rights.**
  - *United States v. Skrmetti* (June 2025) upheld Tennessee's ban on gender-affirming care for minors (my knowledge).
  - *Little v. Hecox* and *West Virginia v. B.P.J.* (June 30, 2026, 6-3, Kavanaugh) held that neither the Equal Protection Clause nor Title IX requires schools to let students compete on teams matching their gender identity (JURIST).
  - *Bostock* (2020) still protects against employment discrimination under Title VII.
- **Disparate impact.** An April 2025 executive order directed agencies to stop enforcing disparate-impact liability (my knowledge; not re-checked this session). *Alexander v. Sandoval* (2001) already bars private suits to enforce Title VI disparate-impact rules. So the standard now depends on which administration is in office.

## RTS-1. Reproductive Freedom Act

**Design.** Modeled on the Women's Health Protection Act, with the Right to Contraception Act and Right to IVF Act folded in (Bill Book 22):

- **The right:** a statutory right for providers to provide, and patients to receive, abortion care before viability, and after viability where the patient's life or health is at risk. Medically unnecessary restrictions (waiting periods, TRAP laws, bans on telehealth) are preempted.
- **Medication abortion:** an express statement that a state may not ban or restrict a drug the FDA has approved for abortion, or its prescription by telehealth or delivery by mail, beyond the FDA's own conditions. The 2023 conditions are written into the statute as the floor, so a later REMS change cannot reach below them without an act of Congress.
- **Contraception and IVF:** statutory rights to both.
- **Travel and data:** interstate travel for care is protected; anyone who helps a patient travel is protected; reproductive-health data cannot be sold or turned over without a warrant.
- **Emergency care:** EMTALA expressly requires stabilizing abortion care in emergencies.
- **Clinics:** FACE Act enforcement funded.

**Write for this Court.** *Dobbs* returned the question "to the people and their elected representatives," and Congress is one. The constitutional basis is the commerce power, which rests on a real interstate market in services, drugs, travel and telehealth, plus Fourteenth Amendment section 5 findings on sex equality. *Gonzales v. Carhart* (2007) assumed that Congress's commerce power reached abortion procedures. The bill also needs aggressive severability, so that the medication, travel and contraception titles survive if the viability title falls.

**Reciprocity (design rule 1).** The same commerce power would let a Republican Congress pass a national ban. That power exists whether or not this bill passes, so the rule is satisfied: the bill creates no procedural tool the other side lacks today. It should be said plainly anyway, because opponents will say it.

## RTS-2. Coverage

**Design.** The Hyde Amendment and related riders are dropped from Labor-HHS and the other appropriations bills, so abortion is covered in Medicaid, the federal employee plan, TRICARE and the Indian Health Service. Title X funding is restored with its 2021 rule.

**Vehicle.** These are appropriations riders, so dropping them takes a regular appropriations bill. Appropriations bills need cloture, so this is ◐. It cannot ride reconciliation as a rider. A Medicaid coverage mandate might be Byrd-eligible as direct spending (unchecked).

**Cost.** About zero to $0.01T (estimate). Covering abortion in Medicaid is roughly cost-neutral in most analyses because of avoided pregnancy costs (my knowledge; not re-checked).

## RTS-3. Equality Act

**Design.** Bill Book 23, modeled on H.R. 15:

- **Coverage:** sexual orientation and gender identity are added to the Civil Rights Act's protections in employment, housing, credit, education, jury service, federally funded programs and public accommodations.
- **Public accommodations:** the definition is modernized to cover retail, transportation, online services and health care for **every** protected class, not only the new ones.
- **Bostock:** its employment holding is written into Title VII.

**After *B.P.J.*.** The Court held that the Constitution and Title IX *permit* states to restrict school sports teams by sex. A statute that bars gender-identity discrimination in education would require inclusion, overriding those state laws. That is the bill's hardest vote, and the polling there is worst for the bill (my judgment; not re-checked). The baseline keeps H.R. 15 as drafted, because the ledger carries it that way and this file does not change a policy silently. **Espen's call.**

**Religious liberty.** H.R. 15 bars use of RFRA as a defense. *303 Creative* (2023) and *Fulton* (2021) apply First Amendment protections regardless of what the statute says, so that provision changes less in practice than either side claims. The provision is kept in the baseline and carried as RTS-3C.

## RTS-4. Civil-rights enforcement

**Design.**

- **Disparate impact:** the disparate-impact standard is written into Title VI, the Fair Housing Act and ECOA, with the burden-shifting framework stated in statute, and a private right of action reverses *Sandoval*.
- **Enforcement offices:** the DOJ Civil Rights Division, the EEOC, HHS's Office for Civil Rights and Education's Office for Civil Rights get statutory staffing floors.
- **Data:** agencies collect civil-rights data, including the clearance-rate disparity data domain-public-safety.md requires.

**Why statute.** The standard currently depends on who holds office. It changed by executive order in 2025, and a future order could change it again. Written into statute, it holds for both parties' administrations (design rule 1).

**Vehicle.** Rides the RTS-3 bill. Both amend the Civil Rights Act and go through the same committees.

## Survival at 51 (D1 stress test)

- **Under F (decided):** both bills pass with every Democratic vote. Under C, the opening offer, they also pass, and RTS-1 is the most contested and the likeliest target of the minority's speech budget.
- **Under G (the written fallback):** both fail. Neither is democracy or elections law.
- **What survives at 51 without a rules change:** the executive pieces. Those are the FDA's REMS, which a Democratic FDA can restore to the 2023 conditions; EMTALA guidance; Title X by rule; agency disparate-impact rules; and enforcement priorities. All of them are reversible, and the REMS piece is exposed to the litigation above.

- **Democracy.** None, except that RTS-3's public-accommodations modernization covers every protected class.
