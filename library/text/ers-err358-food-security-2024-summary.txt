Summary

A report summary from the Economic Research Service

Household Food Security in the United
States in 2024
Matthew P. Rabbitt, Madeline Reed-Jones, Laura J. Hales,
Shellye Suttles, and Michael P. Burke
Key Points
• In 2024, 86.3 percent of U.S. households were food secure. The remaining 13.7 percent (18.3 million households) were food insecure. Food-insecure households (those with low and very low food security) had difficulty at
some time during the year providing enough food for all their members because of a lack of resources. The 2024
prevalence of food insecurity was not statistically significantly different from the 13.5 percent in 2023 nor the
12.8 percent in 2022.
• In 2024, 5.4 percent of households had very low food security, statistically similar to the 5.1 percent in both 2023
and 2022. In this more severe range of food insecurity, the food intake of some household members was reduced
and normal eating patterns were disrupted at times during the year because of limited resources.
• Children were food insecure at times during 2024 in 9.1 percent of U.S. households with children (3.3 million
households), statistically similar to the 8.9 percent in 2023 and 8.8 percent in 2022. These households with food
insecurity among children were unable at times to provide adequate, nutritious food for their children.
• Children are usually shielded from the conditions that characterize very low food security. However, in 2024,
children, along with adults, experienced instances of very low food security in 0.9 percent of households with
children, statistically similar to the 1.0 percent in both 2023 and 2022. These 318,000 households with very low
food security among children reported that, at times in 2024, children were hungry, skipped a meal, or did not
eat for a whole day because there was not enough money for food.

Why Does This Matter?
Most U.S. households have consistent, dependable access to
enough food for active, healthy living, meaning they are food
secure. However, some households experience food insecurity
at times during the year, meaning their ability to acquire adequate food is limited by a lack of money and other resources.
The U.S. Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) food and
nutrition assistance programs aim to increase food security
by providing low-income households access to food for a
healthful diet, as well as nutrition education. USDA monitors
the extent and severity of food insecurity in U.S. households
through an annual, nationally representative survey sponsored

This report presents statistics from the survey that cover

Federal, State, and local policies. This report does not provide
an analysis of the factors that determine the prevalence of or
trends in food insecurity.

A Few More Details
Data for the USDA, ERS food security reports come from an annual survey conducted by the U.S. Department of Commerce,
Bureau of the Census as the December supplement to the monthly Current Population Survey. USDA, ERS sponsors the annual Food Security Supplement (FSS) and compiles and analyzes the responses. The 2024 FSS included 32,719 households,
which comprised a representative sample of the U.S. civilian population of about 134 million households. The FSS asked one
adult respondent per household about experiences and behaviors that indicate food insecurity during calendar year 2024, such
as being unable to afford balanced meals, cutting the size of meals, or being hungry because of too little money for food. The
food security status of the household was assigned based on the number of food-insecure conditions reported.
