INTRODUCTION
The U.S. Census Bureau conducts the Current
Population Survey’s (CPS) Voting and Registration
Supplement following each national election in
November to ask eligible respondents about their
voting and registration behavior. The supplement
is fielded after presidential elections, when both
congressional seats and the presidency are decided,
and midterm elections, when congressional seats are
the highest offices decided. The CPS has surveyed
Americans eligible to vote in these elections since
1964, and estimates derived from this survey are
among the most consistently reliable and publicly
available estimates of the characteristics of American
voters.1, 2
The 2024 presidential election showed a decrease
in voter turnout from 2020, alongside an increase
in the citizen voting-age population; the number of
registered citizens also increased.3 Other notable
highlights include:
• Those ages 45–64 were the most predominant age
group among voters, constituting one-third of the
voting population in 2024 (33.3 percent).
• Those with some college or an associate’s degree,
and those with a bachelor’s degree or higher,

Comparing Current Population Survey (CPS) Voting Estimates to
Official Reports
Estimates in this report are based on responses to the November
Voting and Registration Supplement to the CPS, which surveys the
civilian, noninstitutionalized population in the United States. Voting
estimates from the CPS and other sample surveys have historically
differed from those based on administrative records, such as the
official reports from each state disseminated collectively by the
Clerk of the U.S. House of Representatives and the Federal Election
Commission. In general, sample surveys like the CPS tend to yield
higher voting rates than official results.1 Potential explanations for
these differences include question misreporting, problems with
memory or knowledge of others’ behavior, social desirability bias,
and methodological issues related to question wording, method of
survey administration, and survey nonresponse bias. Despite these
observed differences between CPS estimates and official tallies,
the CPS remains the most comprehensive data source available for
examining the social and demographic composition of American
voters in federal elections, particularly when examining broad
historical results.2

Voting-age population (VAP):
The population age 18 or older
on Election Day. This group
includes both noncitizens (who
are ineligible to vote) and citizens.
In 2024, there were 260.4 million
people in the VAP.
Citizen voting-age population
(CVAP): Those who are citizens
and in the VAP. This subset of
the VAP is the pool of eligible
voters, or those who may
register to vote and cast a ballot
in a national election. Much
of this report focuses on this
universe of respondents. Those
in this population are eligible
respondents of the Voting and
Registration Supplement. In 2024,
there were 236.1 million citizens of
voting age.
Noncitizens: Those who are
not U.S. citizens and, therefore,
ineligible to vote in national
elections. Respondents to the CPS
who indicate they are noncitizens
are not eligible to vote and are not
asked the questions in the Voting
and Registration Supplement.
There were 24.2 million noncitizens
in 2024.

Nonrespondents: Those in
the CVAP who do not answer
questions in the Voting and
Registration Supplement. These
respondents are determined to
be part of the CVAP in the CPS
but did not respond when asked
if they voted or registered to vote
in the latest national election.
Nonresponse may occur for a
variety of reasons, such as refusing
to answer a question or not
knowing the answer to a question.
Nonrespondents of the main
voting question were an estimated
14.1 percent of the CVAP in 2024.7
Voters: Those in the CVAP who
indicated they voted in the 2024
election. These respondents are
in the CVAP and are registered
to vote. There were an estimated
154.3 million voters in 2024.
Nonvoters: Those in the CVAP
who indicated they did not vote
in the 2024 election. Nonvoters
may or may not be registered to
vote. There were an estimated 48.5
million nonvoters in 2024.
Registered: Those who are in the
CVAP and registered to vote by
or on Election Day. Registered
respondents may or may not have
voted. There were an estimated
173.9 million citizens of voting age
who registered to vote in the 2024
election.
Registered nonvoters: Those who
are in the CVAP and registered
to vote but did not indicate they
voted in the 2024 election. There
were an estimated 18.2 million
registered nonvoters in the 2024
election.

AMERICAN VOTERS OVER
TIME: PRESIDENTIAL
ELECTIONS 1980–2024
Table 1 shows that since 1980,
the CVAP and the number of
registered citizens have increased
for each presidential election. For
the first time since 1988, however,
the number of voters did not
significantly change between
presidential elections. In 2024, the
CVAP increased by 2.0 percent,
the number of registered people
increased by 3.3 percent, and
the number of voters did not
significantly change, remaining at
approximately 154.3 million.
In addition to an increase in the
CVAP and the number of people
registered, the demographic
makeup of these populations have
shifted over time. Changes to
these voter shares may represent
changes in relative turnout across
demographics or may reflect
underlying demographic changes
in the CVAP. In other words,
an increase in voter share for a
particular demographic group
could be due to an increase in their
overall population, an increase in
their relative representation among
voters, or a combination of both.
This section primarily focuses on
demographic changes in the voter
distribution between 1980 and
2024.

OVER- AND
UNDERREPRESENTATION
AMONG VOTERS
Table 2 presents the number
and share of those who were
in the CVAP, who registered,
and who voted across various
demographic characteristics in the
2024 election. The final column
of Table 2 presents over- or
underrepresentation of each group
among the voting population. A
group is overrepresented when its
share of the voting population is
significantly larger than its share
of the CVAP; underrepresentation
is the opposite, with a smaller
share of people voting than their
presence in the CVAP would
suggest. This report explores
some of the reasons for these
differences in the final section.

In 2024, young adults ages 18-29
made up 15.3 percent of the voting
population and 19.6 percent of the
CVAP. As a result, 18- to 29-yearold voters were underrepresented
by 4.4 percentage points. Voters
ages 30-44 made up less than
one-quarter (23.4 percent) of
the voting population and nearly
one-quarter (24.7 percent) of
the CVAP, indicating that they
were underrepresented by 1.3
percentage points, a small but
statistically significant difference.
As younger age groups were
underrepresented among voters,
older age groups tended to be
overrepresented. Voters ages
45-64 were 33.3 percent of
the voting population and 31.1
percent of the CVAP in 2024,
an overrepresentation of 2.2
percentage points. Voters age
65 and older made up more

than one-quarter (28.1 percent)
of the voting population and
approximately one-quarter (24.6
percent) of the CVAP, resulting
in an overrepresentation of 3.5
percentage points.
Voters with higher educational
attainment levels were also
overrepresented in 2024. Those
with a bachelor’s degree were 27.8
percent of the voting population
in 2024 and 23.5 percent of the
CVAP, overrepresented by 4.3
percentage points. Voters with
advanced degrees contributed 17.1
percent of the voting population
and 13.5 percent of the CVAP,
overrepresented by 3.6 percentage
points. Conversely, voters who
with a high school credential
were 28.6 percent of the CVAP
and 23.0 percent of the voting
population, underrepresented by
5.6 percentage points.

roughly 9.3 percentage points

remains higher than any election

By comparing the voting methods
used in 2016 with those used
in 2020 and 2024, we can

Methods of Voting: State-Level Rules
According to the National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL),
states fall into the following categories of nontraditional voting
policies.1
Election Day Only. There were three states in which permanent
policies as of 2024 only permitted "traditional" methods of voting;
that is, early voting was not offered, and an excuse was required
to vote with an absentee ballot. These states were Alabama,
Mississippi, and New Hampshire.
Early In-Person Voting. In 47 states and the District of Columbia,
eligible voters during the 2024 election could cast a ballot during a
designated period prior to Election Day (this includes states with allmail elections). No excuse or justification was needed.
Absentee Voting. All states will mail an absentee ballot to certain
voters who request one. The voter may return the ballot by mail or
in person. In 14 states, an excuse was required as of 2024. States
that required an excuse typically included illness or disability as
a justification.2 Additionally, some states offered a permanent
absentee ballot list: once voters asked to be added to the list, they
automatically receive an absentee ballot for all future elections.3
All-Mail Voting. In some states, a ballot was automatically mailed
to every eligible voter—no request or application was necessary.
Permanent policies in California, Colorado, Hawaii, Nevada, Oregon,
Utah, Vermont, Washington, and the District of Columbia sanctioned
all-mail voting in all national elections as of 2024. These states and
the District of Columbia retain some early in-person voting options
for voters who need or prefer it, in addition to the option to vote in
person on Election Day.

the overrepresentation of voters with high family incomes and
bachelor’s degrees. The November
CPS Voting and Registration
Supplement offers a detailed look
at the demographics of who voted,
the methods voters used to vote,
and how these measures have

Every state’s board of elections

(in presidential election years)
or the total number of votes cast
for a House of Representatives
or Senate seat (in congressional
election years). In each election,
there are discrepancies between
the CPS Voting and Registration
Supplement estimates and these
official counts. The discrepancy
has varied in each election year,
with official tallies typically
presenting lower turnout than
the estimates in these types of

official counts and the November

combination of an understatement
of official numbers and an
overstatement of supplement
estimates.

Overstatement of Voting in the
CPS Voting and Registration
Supplement
Some of the error in estimating
turnout using the CPS core
questions and the CPS Voting
and Registration Supplement is
the result of population controls
and survey coverage. Respondent
misreporting is also a source of
error in the estimates. Previous
analyses based on reinterviews
presented respondents and proxy
respondents are consistent in their
reported answers, revealing that
misunderstanding the questions
does not fully account for the
difference between the official
counts and the survey estimates.
However, studies that have
matched survey responses with
voting records indicate that part
of the discrepancy between survey
estimates and official counts is the
result of respondent misreporting,
particularly vote overreporting
to appear to behave in a socially
desirable way.27

As discussed earlier, the issue of
vote overreporting is not unique
to the Voting and Registration
Supplement. Other surveys
consistently overstate voter
turnout as well, including highly
respected national-level surveys
like the American National Election
Studies and the General Social
Survey. Potential reasons why
respondents might incorrectly
report voting in an election are
myriad, including intentional
misreporting, legitimate confusion
over whether a vote was cast or
not, and methodological issues
related to question wording,
method of survey administration,
and specific question
nonresponses.
Voting Not Captured in the
CPS Voting and Registration
Supplement
The CPS only covers the civilian,
noninstitutionalized population
residing in the United States.
Therefore, the supplement does
not capture voting for citizens
living in institutions in the United
States or voting for citizens,
whether civilian or military,
residing outside of the United
States who cast absentee ballots.
