The version of the National Energy Modeling System (NEMS) used for our Annual Energy Outlook
2026 (AEO2026) generally represents legislation, environmental regulations, and international
protocols, including recent government regulations as of December 2025. Impacts of legislation,
executive actions, regulations, or judicial rulings occurring after this date are not reflected in
AEO2026 and will be addressed to the extent possible in subsequent EIA outlooks. The potential
effects of proposed federal and state legislation, regulations, or standards are not reflected in the
Counterfactual Baseline case. In addition, the Counterfactual Baseline case does not reflect sections
of legislation that have been enacted but have not been funded or lack implementation regulations.
A list of the federal and selected state legislation and regulations included in AEO2026, including how
we incorporated them, is provided in each module’s documentation. This document provides an
overview of all the relevant regulations and includes summary tables that represent both new and
existing legislation and regulations, as of December 2025, represented in NEMS.
In addition to the Counterfactual Baseline, AEO2026 features 10 side cases. The Alternative
Transportation case, the Alternative Electricity case, and the Alternative Electricity-Alternative
Transportation Combination case do make assumptions about certain regulations. For more details,
see the AEO2026 Case Descriptions.

Updated Laws and Regulations Reflected in the Annual Energy
Outlook 2026 (AEO2026) Counterfactual Baseline Case
Legislative and regulatory provisions that have been updated since AEO2025 are briefly described
below. For a list of all laws and regulations included in AEO2026, see the Summary: Existing and New
Federal and State Legislation and Regulations in the Annual Energy Outlook 2026 table, included in
the next section. All AEO2026 cases use the macroeconomic outlook from S&P Global Market
Intelligence as of August 2025.
The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) was passed on July 4, 2025. It modifies several provisions of
laws incorporated in AEO2023 and AEO2025, such as:
•
•
•

•

Changing the project online requirement for a production tax credit of up to $3.00 per
kilogram of hydrogen to producers of clean hydrogen (IRA Section 45V) from 2033 to 2027
Increasing the tax credit for carbon dioxide (CO2) used for enhanced oil recovery to $85 per
metric ton (IRA Section 45Q tax credit, a subsidy for carbon sequestration)
Terminating tax credits for qualified zero-carbon generators in Sections 45Y and 48E of the
IRA earlier (Under OBBBA, the deadline to be placed in service for wind and solar facilities is
now 2027, and for other technologies, phase out begins in 2033.)
Terminating several transportation-related tax credits earlier, including the Clean Vehicle
Credit (Section 30D), Qualified Commercial Clean Vehicles Credit (Section 45W), and
Alternative Fuel Vehicle Refueling Property Credit (Section 30C)

H.J. Res.87 became Public Law 119-15 on June 12, 2025. Congress disapproved the waivers
issued by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for several California regulations,
including the Advanced Clean Trucks (ACT) rule. Therefore, the ACT is not enforced in the
Counterfactual Baseline (CB) case or any of the side cases in AEO2026.
H.R.1 became Public Law 119-21 on July 4, 2025. Section 40006 set civil penalties for
noncompliance with National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) Corporate
Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards to $0. Therefore, light-duty vehicle CAFE standards
are not enforced in the CB case or any side cases in AEO2026. As discussed in the case
descriptions, EPA’s 2024 multi-pollutant emission standards, which cover similar portions of
the fleet, are modeled in all cases except the Alternative Transportation case and the
Alternative Electricity-Alternative Transportation Combination case.
In 2024, EPA finalized its Section 111 of the Clean Air Act (CAA) regulating CO2 emissions from
existing coal, oil, and natural gas-fired steam generating units and new natural gas-fired
combustion turbines in the Greenhouse Gas Standards and Guidelines for Fossil Fuel-Fired
Power Plants rule. This policy was first represented in AEO2025. Although there has been
a proposal to repeal parts of this policy, no final ruling had been made before the policy
cutoff date for AEO2026, and therefore, no changes were made to the core cases. The

The U.S. government introduced tariffs on nearly all trade partners beginning on April 2,
2025, with several subsequent modifications to tariffs occurring throughout the
year. Although some anticipated effects of these tariffs were captured through AEO’s
macroeconomic indicators, product-specific changes, such as tariff-related price increases to
U.S. solar panels or batteries, were not included in AEO2026.
Several Presidential Executive Orders were signed related to the energy sector in 2025:
o On January 20, 2025, President Donald J. Trump issued an order to halt permit and
leases for offshore wind projects, potentially conflicting with state offshore wind
mandates. To account for this in AEO2026, we delayed offshore wind mandate
deadlines and also constrained the required capacity for each target based on land
availability not including federal lands.
o Other Executive Orders, including those for nuclear and coal, were not included in
AEO2026 due to insufficient information regarding implementation details needed
for modeling.

Appendix A. Existing State-level Clean Energy Policies in the
AEO2026 Counterfactual Baseline Case
To the extent possible, the AEO2026 reflects state laws and regulations enacted as of December
2025, which include minimum requirements for clean energy generation or capacity for load-serving
entities operating in states. We do not include zero-emission credit policies within this group. The
AEO2026 projections do not include laws and regulations with voluntary goals. We include policies
that are enacted in legislation or set forth by state-level executive branch entities.
AEO2026 assumes that states will meet their clean energy standards and targets. We estimate
compliance constraints in most regions; however, because NEMS is not a state-level model, each
state generally represents a portion of one or more NEMS electricity regions. In general, we have
confirmed the requirements for each state through original legislative or regulatory documentation.
For clean energy generation requirements (Table 1), states meet required levels of renewable
generation based on qualified generation or purchases of renewable energy credits. 1 The modeled
requirements may differ from the written legislation based on the limitations in the model. For
example, policies designed to create an economy-wide carbon-neutral state are modeled as a 100%
clean electricity standard because our National Energy Modeling System (NEMS) is limited in
constraining carbon emissions. For AEO2026, a complete review of all states was implemented.
