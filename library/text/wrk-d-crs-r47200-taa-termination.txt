Trade Adjustment Assistance for Workers: Background and Current Status
rade Adjustment Assistance for Workers (TAA) provides federal assistance to workers who
involuntarily lose their jobs due to foreign competition.1 The primary benefits for TAA-
T eligible workers are funding for training and reemployment services as well as income
support while a worker is enrolled in training.
In accordance with the termination provisions of the Trade Adjustment Assistance
Reauthorization Act of 2015 (TAARA, Title IV of P.L. 114-27), the TAA program entered a
phased termination beginning July 1, 2022. After this date, no new petitions will be certified, but
previously participating workers can continue to receive benefits.2
This report discusses the TAA program in its current context. The first section provides brief
background on the authorization and operation of the program. The next section describes
TAARA and the implementation of its sunset provisions and termination provisions. The last
section describes congressional activity since the July 1, 2022, effective date of the termination
provisions, which has not included an enacted extension of the program.
Implementation of the TAA Termination Provisions
Beginning July 1, 2022, the TAA termination provisions took effect. Under these provisions, no new worker
groups may be certified, but workers who were receiving benefits prior to the termination date may continue to
receive benefits. The TAA program has continued to operate and receive appropriations to serve workers who
established eligibility for the program prior to the effective date of the termination provisions.
Background
This section provides a brief description of TAA. For more detail on program operation,
eligibility, and benefits, see archived CRS Report R44153, Trade Adjustment Assistance for
Workers and the TAA Reauthorization Act of 2015 .3
Program Rationale and Authorizing Statute
Reduced barriers to international trade are widely acknowledged to offer benefits to consumers in
the form of increased choices and lower prices. Expanded trade may also offer opportunities to
firms that produce goods or supply services for increased exports. Reduced barriers to trade may,
however, have concentrated negative effects on domestic industries and workers who face
increased competition.4 TAA is designed to provide readjustment assistance to workers who

TAA is authorized by Title II of the Trade Act of 1974, as amended.6 Historically, it has been

Financing and Administration
TAA is funded by federal appropriations. It is jointly administered by the U.S. Department of
Labor (DOL) and cooperating state agencies.
Financing and FY2023 Funding
TAA is a direct spending (also referred to as mandatory ) program, and thus is funded by
mandatory appropriations that are enacted in annual appropriations acts. Typically, appropriation
statutes appropriate a single sum that supports all TAA activities.8 DOL then allocates these funds
to various program functions. States may expend training and reemployment service funds in the
year of allotment or in either of the next two fiscal years.
While the termination provisions took effect in July 2022, the program has continued to receive
funding to support benefits to workers who established eligibility prior to the effective date of the
termination provisions.
In FY2023, the pre-sequestration appropriation for TAA was $494 million. TAA is subject to
mandatory spending sequestration under the Budget Control Act of 2011, as amended.9
In its FY2024 budget justification, DOL estimated that approximately $31 million for TAA would
be required to fund current-law reduced costs under the termination provisions.10

Major Components of the TAA Program
Eligibility for TAA is established by group petitions. Workers who are covered by a certified
petition pursue benefits through state agencies.
As noted above, in accordance with program termination provisions, no new petitions are being
certified after July 1, 2022. The eligibility procedures and benefit descriptions that follow pertain
to participants from prior certified petitions who are receiving benefits.
Eligibility
To establish eligibility, a group of workers who had been laid off or threatened with a layoff filed
a petition with DOL. A union, firm, or state workforce agency was also eligible to file a petition
on the workers’ behalf. After receiving a petition, DOL conducted an investigation to determine if
foreign competition “contributed importantly” to the workers’ job loss and if the circumstances of
the layoff met statutory eligibility criteria.13
Benefits
Individual benefits are funded by the federal government and administered by state agencies
through their workforce systems and unemployment insurance systems. Benefits available to
individual workers include the following:14
• Training and reemployment services are designed to assist workers in
preparing for and obtaining new employment. Training subsidies support workers
in developing skills for a new occupation. Workers may also receive case
management services and job search assistance. In some cases, workers who
pursue employment outside their local commuting area may be eligible for job
search or relocation allowances.
• Trade Readjustment Allowance (TRA) is a weekly income support payment
for TAA-certified workers who have exhausted unemployment insurance and are
enrolled in an eligible training program. Weekly TRA payments are equal to the

Trade Adjustment Assistance Reauthorization Act of
2015
TAA was most recently reauthorized by TAARA in June 2015.20 TAARA (Title IV of P.L. 114-27)
was part of a bill that extended other trade-related policies. TAARA was also passed in
conjunction with a separate bill that reauthorized the Trade Promotion Authority (TPA; Title I of
P.L. 114-26). TPA (also known as fast track authority ) grants the President authority to negotiate
trade agreements, which are then subject to an up or down vote in Congress.21

cap for “training and other activities” is $450 million. Under the Reversion 2021
provisions, the cap was limited to training and reduced to $220 million.26
The Reversion 2021 provisions were not retroactive. Workers from groups certified under the
2015 provisions would continue to be eligible under those provisions even if the eligibility
provisions that they were certified under are no longer in effect for new participants.
Termination Provisions under TAARA
On July 1, 2022, the termination provisions of TAARA took effect.27 These provisions specify
that DOL may not provide benefits under the TAA program after that date, with the exception that
previously participating workers may continue to receive benefits. New petitions may not be
investigated or certified after the termination date. TAARA does not have a hard sunset date for
workers who were receiving benefits prior to the July 1, 2022, termination date.
State systems continued to operate for workers who were receiving benefits prior to the
termination date. Prior to these termination provisions taking effect, DOL issued guidance to state
agencies on how to implement them.28 The guidance specified that workers would continue to
receive benefits under the version of the program that was in place at the time the workers’ group
petition was certified.

Selected Activity in the 117th Congress
While the 117th Congress did not enact an extension of TAA, there was some activity around the
program, and it was included in two broader pieces of House-passed legislation.
• In March 2021, the House Ways and Means Committee’s Trade Subcommittee
held a hearing on reauthorizing the TAA program.29
• In June 2021, the Trade Adjustment Assistance Modernization Act of 2021 (H.R.
3974) was introduced. The bill was sponsored by the chair of the House Trade
Subcommittee and was co-sponsored by most majority members of the
subcommittee. The bill was not marked up. H.R. 3974 would have extended the
authorization of TAA through FY2028 and made expansions to program
eligibility and benefits.
• In November 2021, the House passed the Build Back Better Act (H.R. 5376),
which included provisions that would extend the authorization of TAA through
FY2028 and included expansions similar to those in H.R. 3974. The TAA
provisions were not included in the enacted version of H.R. 5376.
• In February 2022, the House passed the America COMPETES Act (H.R. 4521),
which included language that would extend the authorization of TAA through
FY2028 and included program expansions similar to those in H.R. 3974. The
TAA provisions were not included in a subsequent Senate-passed version of H.R.
4521.
Selected Activity in the 118th Congress
In June 2023, the American Worker and Trade Competitiveness Act (H.R. 4276) was introduced.
The bill was sponsored by the ranking member of the House Trade Subcommittee. The bill would
extend the authorization of TAA through FY2030, make expansion to program eligibility and
benefits, and make other non-TAA changes to trade policy. This bill has not received committee
consideration as of the cover date of this report.
