## At a Glance
The federal government subsidizes health insurance for most Americans through various programs
and tax provisions. The Congressional Budget Office and the staff of the Joint Committee on
Taxation (JCT) regularly prepare projections of the federal costs of those subsidies and the number of
people with health insurance coverage through different sources. This report presents details underlying the projections released in February 2026, including details about health insurance coverage
by age and income, factors underlying growth in federal subsidies, and the effects of legislation and
administrative actions.
**Federal Subsidies.** In 2026, federal subsidies for health insurance, net of related payments to
the government, are projected to equal $2.4 trillion, or 7.4 percent of gross domestic product
(GDP). In CBO and JCT’s projections, those subsidies grow by 65 percent, reaching $3.9 trillion,
or 8.4 percent of GDP, in 2036. Subsidies for Medicare contribute most to that overall growth,
increasing by $900 billion.
Over the entire 2026–2036 period, federal subsidies for health insurance total $33.6 trillion,
distributed as follows:
• Medicare—$16.1 trillion (48 percent),
• Medicaid and the Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP)—$8.4 trillion (25 percent),
• Employment-based coverage—$7.2 trillion (21 percent),
• The premium tax credit and related spending—$1.2 trillion (4 percent), and
• Supplemental or partial benefits—$0.7 trillion (2 percent).
**Health Insurance Coverage.** An average of 318 million people are projected to have health insurance
in any given month from 2026 to 2036; nearly 50 percent of the total population would be enrolled
in employment-based coverage. An average of 36 million people—10 percent of the population—are
projected to be uninsured in any given month over that period.
The number of uninsured people in CBO’s projections grows from 30 million in 2026 to 37 million in 2036, largely because of provisions of the 2025 reconciliation act that are expected to reduce
enrollment in Medicaid and CHIP and, to a lesser extent, in the health insurance marketplaces
established by the Affordable Care Act and the Basic Health Program. About 60 percent of uninsured
people in 2036 would be eligible for subsidized coverage.

# Notes About This Report
The estimates presented in this report underlie the Congressional Budget Office’s February 2026
baseline budget projections. They are based on CBO’s February 2026 economic projections and
incorporate the effects of legislation enacted through January 14, 2026. The baseline projections,
which reflect the assumption that current laws governing taxes and spending would generally remain
unchanged, serve as a benchmark for assessing proposed policy changes.
The population referred to in this report is the Social Security area population, which includes all
residents of the 50 states and of the District of Columbia, as well as civilian residents of U.S. territories. It also includes federal civilian employees and members of the U.S. Armed Forces living
abroad and their dependents, U.S. citizens living abroad, and noncitizens living abroad who are
eligible for Social Security benefits.
Estimates of health insurance coverage reflect average monthly enrollment during a calendar year.
Those estimates include spouses and dependents covered under family policies and exclude people
with supplemental or partial coverage that, on its own, would not provide financial protection against
major medical expenses. Estimates of employment-based coverage include people enrolled in veterans’
health care programs, the Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB) program, and the military’s
TRICARE health care program.
Federal subsidies for health insurance include both outlays (also referred to as spending in this report)
and reductions in revenues. Estimates exclude discretionary outlays (such as spending on veterans’ health
care programs), outlays made by the federal government in its capacity as an employer (such as spending
on FEHB and TRICARE), and certain reductions in revenues (such as the medical expense deduction).
In 2026, discretionary outlays for veterans’ health care programs, FEHB, and TRICARE are estimated
to total $196 billion; mandatory outlays for those programs are estimated to total $91 billion.
Estimates of average subsidies per enrollee are for calendar years. All other estimates of federal
spending and revenues are for federal fiscal years, which run from October 1 to September 30 and are
designated by the calendar year in which they end.
Income in this report is measured as a percentage of the federal poverty guidelines, commonly known as
the federal poverty level (FPL). In most states, the FPL in 2026 begins at $15,960 of modified adjusted
gross income for a single person and increases by $5,680 for each additional person in a household.
Numbers may not add up to totals because of rounding.
All figures related to enrollment, spending on Medicare, and spending on Medicaid and the
Children’s Health Insurance Program are based on estimates by CBO. All other figures are based on
estimates by CBO and the staff of the Joint Committee on Taxation.

# Federal Subsidies for Health Insurance, 2026 to 2036
Most Americans’ health insurance is subsidized by the federal government, either through a program such as Medicare
or Medicaid or through tax provisions.
This report, the latest in a regular series, presents the Congressional Budget Office and the staff of the Joint Committee
on Taxation’s (JCT’s) projections of what health insurance coverage and the federal costs of that coverage would look
like over the next 10 years if current laws governing taxes and spending generally remained unchanged.
These projections underlie CBO’s baseline budget projections, which serve as a benchmark for assessing the budgetary effects of proposed policy changes. They reflect CBO’s economic and demographic projections as well as data
on health insurance enrollment and premiums; they also incorporate the effects of enacted legislation, including the
2025 reconciliation act (Public Law 119-21), which made significant changes to Medicaid and to the health insurance
marketplaces established under the Affordable Care Act (ACA).
For a list of CBO’s recent publications on policies that affect health insurance coverage, including provisions of the
2025 reconciliation act and a final rule related to the marketplaces that was published by the Department of Health
and Human Services in June 2025, see Appendix D.

### Overview of Sources of and Subsidies for Health Insurance
Eligibility to enroll in coverage and federal subsidies for that coverage differ among sources of
health insurance. The sources in the table below are listed in descending order by total federal
subsidies in 2026.
| | Eligibility | Federal subsidies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medicare | People typically become eligible for Medicare at age 65. About one-tenth of enrollees are people younger than 65 who receive benefits from the Social Security Disability Insurance program or have certain medical conditions. | The federal government pays for most of the cost of Medicare coverage through the Hospital Insurance Trust Fund, which is financed largely through payroll taxes, and the Supplementary Medical Insurance Trust Fund, which is financed through payments from the Treasury's general fund and covers outpatient services and prescription drugs. |
| Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) | Eligibility for both Medicaid and CHIP varies by state. Medicaid generally covers children, parents, pregnant women, older adults, people with disabilities, and some childless adults, particularly in states that expanded Medicaid eligibility under the ACA. CHIP primarily covers children in families with income above their state's Medicaid eligibility threshold but below a state-determined limit. States may operate their CHIP program as an expansion of Medicaid, a separate program, or a combination of those two approaches. | The federal government provides about two-thirds of all funding, and states finance the remainder. |
| Employment-based coverage | Full-time workers, workers employed by large employers, and workers with higher income are more likely to have an offer of employment-based coverage than part-time or seasonal workers, workers employed by small employers, and workers with lower income. Most full-time workers and their dependents are enrolled in employment-based coverage. | The federal government subsidizes employment-based coverage by generally excluding amounts paid by employers and employees for health insurance premiums from income and payroll taxes. That exclusion subsidizes about 30 percent of the cost of premiums, on average. People with higher income, who are taxed at higher rates on the additional dollars they earn, generally receive larger subsidies for a given premium. |
| Nongroup coverage and the Basic Health Program | Nongroup health insurance is private health coverage that is purchased by an individual through the marketplaces established by the ACA or outside the marketplaces, rather than being obtained through an employer. People purchasing coverage through the marketplaces may qualify for the premium tax credit, which subsidizes that coverage, if they are within certain income ranges, are ineligible for other sources of subsidized coverage, and meet other eligibility criteria. In addition, under the ACA, states have the option to establish a Basic Health Program intended primarily for people with income between 138 and 200 percent of the federal poverty level (FPL). Three states and the District of Columbia have established such programs. | For eligible enrollees in the marketplaces, the federal government offers a premium tax credit that reduces out-of-pocket costs for premiums. For states that modify their nongroup market through waivers under section 1332 of the ACA, the federal government provides funding equal to any reductions in the premium tax credit that result from the waiver. For states that establish a Basic Health Program, the federal government provides much of the cost of coverage. Altogether, the federal government subsidizes about 50 percent of the costs of nongroup coverage and the Basic Health Program. |
| Other coverage | Other sources of coverage include student health plans, state-sponsored health plans, correctional facilities, the Indian Health Service, and foreign sources of coverage. | Federal funding for correctional facilities and the Indian Health Service is excluded from this report because it mostly takes the form of discretionary outlays, which are subject to future appropriation action by lawmakers. |

### Composition of Federal Subsidies for and Enrollment in Health Insurance Coverage
Total federal subsidies are larger for Medicare than for other types of coverage. Although the
program does not cover the largest number of people, the federal government subsidizes a
larger share of health care costs for its enrollees, who use more health care services, on average,
than people with other types of coverage. Employment-based coverage has the largest number
of enrollees, but subsidies for that coverage tend to be smaller because the tax benefit amounts
to only a portion of the cost of the premium based on a person’s marginal tax rate. The federal
costs of subsidies are smallest for nongroup coverage and coverage through the Basic Health
Program because relatively few people receive those subsidies (which are provided through the
premium tax credit and related spending).
CBO’s estimates of enrollment and subsidies for a given source of coverage exclude supplemental
or partial coverage that, on its own, would not provide financial protection against major medical
expenses. In addition, to better align its estimates with underlying data on coverage, CBO does
not assign people reporting multiple sources of coverage to a primary source. As a result, the sum
of enrollment in each source and the number of uninsured people exceeds the total population.

### Growth in Federal Subsidies for Health Insurance
Total federal subsidies for health insurance are projected to grow faster than the overall economy over the next decade. In CBO’s projections, those subsidies grow by 65 percent between
2026 and 2036, from $2.4 trillion to $3.9 trillion. Gross domestic product (GDP), a measure of the size of the economy, increases by 46 percent over the same period. Because total
subsidies grow faster than the overall economy, subsidies as a percentage of GDP increase by
about 1 percentage point.

### Factors Underlying Growth in Federal Subsidies for Health Insurance
CBO attributed the projected growth in federal subsidies to three factors.
**Growth in prices for health care services** contributes to growth in subsidies for each source of
health insurance.
**Growth in the average real subsidy per beneficiary,** largely due to beneficiaries’ greater use of
medical care, also contributes to growth in subsidies for each source of health insurance. (Real
subsidies are nominal subsidies that have been adjusted to remove the effects of price growth.)
**Increases in enrollment** are expected to increase subsidies for Medicare and
employment-based coverage over the next decade.
For more information about the methods underlying CBO’s estimates, see Appendix B.

### Average Federal Subsidies for People With Subsidized Coverage
Average federal subsidies for health insurance per subsidized enrollee vary, both among the
sources of health insurance and within the group of people enrolled in coverage through a
given source. Among the sources of coverage, subsidies vary because of factors such as
enrollees’ demographic characteristics, the share of costs borne by the federal government,
and the prices paid to providers. Among people with the same source of coverage, subsidies
vary because of factors including age, health status, income, and disability status. For example, in CBO’s projections, the average subsidy per Medicare enrollee in 2036 is $26,030 for
those age 65 or older and $35,590 for those younger than 65 (who qualify because they have
a disability or certain medical conditions).

### Changes in Coverage Among Age Groups
To estimate federal subsidies for health insurance, CBO projects the number of people with
coverage through the different sources as well as the number of uninsured people. Those
estimates reflect both access to and take-up of coverage.
For insured people under age 65, employment-based coverage is the predominant source of
coverage, followed by Medicaid and CHIP. For those age 65 and older, the vast majority are
enrolled in Medicare.
Most uninsured people are estimated to be between ages 19 and 64. Children are less likely
to be uninsured, in part because they have more access to Medicaid and CHIP.
The uninsured rate is the lowest among adults age 65 or older because of their high levels of
access to and take-up of Medicare. (For details about CBO’s projections of health insurance
coverage, see Appendix B.)

### Differences in Coverage Among Income Groups for People Younger Than 65
For people younger than 65, sources of health insurance coverage are strongly associated
with income. Those with lower income are more likely to be uninsured, and when they have
insurance, it is often through Medicaid or CHIP. Those with higher income are predominantly insured through employment-based coverage. (To focus on how people’s sources of
health insurance coverage vary by income, this figure excludes people age 65 or older, nearly

Some Medicaid and CHIP enrollees in the underlying data have annual income that exceeds
the upper limit for their eligibility category. That can occur in any year when, for example,
people’s income and enrollment status changed during the year and their income exceeded
the eligibility thresholds when they were not enrolled in one of those programs.

### Medicare
Medicare has three components available to enrollees in the traditional fee-for-service (FFS) program:
Part A (Hospital Insurance), Part B (Medical Insurance, which covers doctors’ services, outpatient care, and
other medical services), and Part D (which covers outpatient prescription drugs). Medicare Part C, known
as Medicare Advantage (MA), allows people to enroll in private plans for their Part A and Part B coverage;
many MA plans also provide Part D benefits and offer supplemental benefits such as dental, vision, and
hearing coverage. More than 90 percent of Medicare enrollees participate in both Part A and Part B, either
directly or through MA. The remaining enrollees typically have only Part A coverage and often have an alternative source of primary coverage. Estimates of enrollment and total spending in this section include people
enrolled in Part A, Part B, or both; elsewhere in the report, only people who are enrolled in both Part A and
Part B are included.
The share of Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in MA rose from about 30 percent to about 50 percent between
2015 and 2024, but that growth slowed in 2025 and is projected to slow further. Nevertheless, CBO
projects that MA will account for an increasing share of spending for Part A and Part B benefits, from
54 percent in 2026 to 61 percent in 2036. Over that period, annual spending per MA enrollee is projected
to be 7 percent higher, on average, than spending per FFS enrollee with both Part A and Part B coverage.
(That estimate excludes categories of spending that are always paid through FFS, such as hospice, and
includes supplemental MA benefits that are federally financed through plan rebates.) On the basis of
historical trends, CBO projects that the gap in per-beneficiary spending between FFS and MA will grow
over time. For more details about MA and potential reasons for that spending differential, see Appendix C.

### Medicaid and CHIP
Together, Medicaid and CHIP provide coverage to a broad range of low-income populations, including
children, parents, pregnant women, older adults, and people with disabilities. The ACA allows states to
expand Medicaid eligibility to all nondisabled adults with income less than or equal to 138 percent of
the FPL. Children may qualify for Medicaid or CHIP at higher income levels than adults, with eligibility
thresholds that vary by state.
#### Policy Changes
The 2025 reconciliation act made major changes to Medicaid that, on net, are expected to decrease enrollment and reduce the federal deficit. This discussion focuses on the provisions of the law most relevant to
those effects.
Some provisions directly affect eligibility and enrollment:
• Community engagement requirements, which stipulate that certain adults made eligible for Medicaid
under the ACA expansion or under a similar waiver-based expansion must document their participation
in work or other qualifying activities (such as education or community service) to maintain coverage;
• Moratoria on certain regulations, which pause the implementation of rules finalized near the end of the
previous Administration that affect application, enrollment, and renewal procedures, including rules
related to returned mail and limits on the frequency of redeterminations for certain groups; and
• More frequent redeterminations for adults made eligible for Medicaid under the ACA expansion or
under a similar waiver-based expansion, which require states to reassess their eligibility every six months.
Other provisions modify federal financing arrangements:
• Changes to provider taxes, which limit states’ ability to use taxes on providers to finance their Medicaid
programs; and
• Changes to state directed payments, which limit states’ ability to direct Medicaid managed care plans to
pay providers at rates above Medicare levels.
Those modifications lead to lower enrollment indirectly, through states’ responses. For example, states may
respond to reduced resources for financing their share of Medicaid by tightening their eligibility requirements or scaling back optional coverage.

#### Changes in Enrollment and Spending
Medicaid enrollment is projected to decline over the coming decade, largely because of policy
changes under the 2025 reconciliation act. That decline also reflects reductions in enrollment among children, in part because lower fertility rates in CBO’s demographic projections
reduce the number of children eligible for Medicaid. At the same time, federal spending on

### The Premium Tax Credit and Related Spending
The premium tax credit was established under the ACA to lower the out-of-pocket cost of
premiums for eligible people who obtain health insurance through the marketplaces.
Related spending includes subsidies for health insurance that states provide through the Basic
Health Program; funding for states that modify the nongroup market through waivers under
section 1332 of the ACA; and net collections and payments for the risk adjustment program.
#### Policy Changes
Several provisions of the 2025 reconciliation act are projected to reduce enrollment in the
marketplaces and the Basic Health Program, including the following:
• Removal of eligibility for the premium tax credit from certain immigrants with income
below the FPL and several other groups of immigrants;
• Effective elimination of a special enrollment period that allowed people with income at or
below 150 percent of the FPL to enroll in coverage at any time in the year; and
• Modifications of enrollment processes, with the intent to reduce improper enrollments, and
limits on beneficiaries’ ability to receive the premium tax credit without filing a tax return.
In 2026, a final rule governing the marketplaces that was published by the Department of
Health and Human Services in June 2025 changed enrollees’ premium contributions, enrollment periods, verification procedures, and payment requirements for purchasing coverage.
That rule is projected to reduce enrollment about equally in all years over the next decade.

### Private Health Insurance Premiums
Federal subsidies for employment-based and nongroup coverage depend on the premiums
in those markets. Premiums for employment-based coverage are typically paid by both
the employer and the employee. The employer’s portion is excluded from federal income
and payroll taxes, and in most cases, the employee’s portion is as well. In general, people
with higher income, and thus higher marginal tax rates, receive a larger federal subsidy for
employment-based coverage. For nongroup coverage, federal subsidies take the form of the
premium tax credit. The amount of the credit is the difference between the benchmark premium (that is, the premium for the second-lowest-cost silver plan available in a region) and
the percentage of income that an enrollee is expected to pay toward premiums.
From 2026 to 2036, spending per enrollee in private health insurance is projected to grow by
an average of 5 percent per year. That growth generally reflects trends in the prices of medical
care and in personal income, which affects the amount people spend on medical care. CBO
uses the projected growth in private health insurers’ per-enrollee spending to forecast premiums for employment-based and nongroup health insurance. CBO’s projections of the average
marketplace benchmark premium are based on that per-enrollee spending in the broader
private market and on factors unique to the marketplaces, such as policies that are expected
to affect the relative health of enrollees.
The average benchmark premium increased sharply in 2026, largely driven by the disproportionate disenrollment of healthier marketplace enrollees after the expiration of the expanded
premium tax credit, higher-than-expected recent claims costs, and insurers’ uncertainty about
policy changes and their effects on average health spending among enrollees.

### The Uninsured Population
CBO counts people as uninsured if they are not covered by an insurance plan or enrolled in
a government program that provides financial protection against major medical expenses.
Many uninsured people receive some health care and often are not required to pay the full
cost of that care, but they have substantially less access to care and financial protection from
major medical expenses than insured people. Uninsured people who are eligible for Medicaid
have more financial protection than others because they can enroll throughout the year—in
some cases, as they are seeking care in hospitals or other settings—and may receive coverage
retroactively. People who are enrolled only in Medicare Part A or Part B, whom CBO counts
as uninsured, also have some financial protection because they receive coverage for inpatient
hospital services or for nonhospital services, such as physicians’ services, respectively.
#### Eligibility for Subsidized Coverage
CBO classified people who lack insurance into groups based on the most heavily subsidized
option available to them or the main reason they were ineligible for subsidized coverage.
Some uninsured people are eligible for one or more sources of coverage but have not
enrolled—for example, because they did not complete application processes or had difficulty
providing sufficient evidence that they met eligibility requirements.

### Uncertainty
The projections in this report are based on CBO’s best assessment of the available evidence.
Nevertheless, they are subject to uncertainty because they reflect complex interactions among
many entities, including federal and state policymakers, employers, households, and insurers.
They depend on economic conditions, which depend, in turn, on a wide variety of national
and global forces, and they reflect the assumption that current laws and regulations will
remain in place. Beyond those general sources of uncertainty, specific sources of uncertainty
apply to particular programs.
**Medicare.** Projections of Medicare enrollment and spending are inherently uncertain. Future
health care utilization, costs, and enrollment in Medicare Advantage may all diverge—
upward or downward—from CBO’s projections, driven by factors such as changes in administrative policy, shifts in demographics, and advances in technology.
**Medicaid.** Uncertainty about projections of Medicaid enrollment and spending primarily
relates to the implementation of provisions of the 2025 reconciliation act. To the extent that
the implementation of those provisions differs from CBO’s estimates, enrollment and spending will diverge from the baseline projections.
The changes in policy will also elicit reactions from states, employers, insurers, and individual Medicaid enrollees, and those responses are generally uncertain. For example, CBO’s
estimates incorporate the assumption that no additional states will expand Medicaid in the
future, mainly because, starting in 2028, the 2025 reconciliation act will limit the ability of
states that have expanded Medicaid to finance the program’s costs through taxes on providers. If additional states did expand Medicaid under the ACA, the federal savings associated
with that provision of the law would be smaller than estimated; if more states withdrew their
Medicaid expansions than CBO expects, those savings would be larger. In addition, considerable uncertainty surrounds responses by states and providers to provisions that modify
Medicaid’s federal financing arrangements and provisions that change the program’s eligibility and enrollment processes, such as community engagement requirements.
**Health Insurance Marketplaces.** CBO projects that average monthly enrollment in the
marketplaces will decline from 22 million in 2025 to 17 million in 2026. That projected
near-term decline is subject to considerable uncertainty because it reflects expected responses
by insurers and individuals to the expiration of the expanded premium tax credit and to the
implementation of provisions of the 2025 reconciliation act. From 2021 to 2025, when the
expanded premium tax credit was in place, marketplace enrollment increased significantly,
but administrative actions also contributed to increases in enrollment. Because those changes
overlapped, isolating their effects on enrollment is difficult. Estimating the pace at which
people will respond to policy changes is also difficult. If enrollment in 2026 is higher than
projected, the federal costs of the premium tax credit and related spending will be, too.
CBO projects that marketplace benchmark premiums will grow modestly in 2027, after a
double-digit percentage increase, following a pattern similar to that observed from 2017 to 2019.
Two factors make that projection uncertain. First, uncertainty about enrollment translates directly
to uncertainty about premiums because marketplace premiums depend on how many people
remain enrolled in the marketplaces and how their health care utilization compares with that
of people who have disenrolled. Second, insurers are developing and submitting premiums for
2027 on the basis of only a few months of 2026 data and amid regulatory uncertainty. Incomplete
data and continued uncertainty could place upward pressure on premiums in 2027, causing premiums—and the federal costs of the premium tax credit—to exceed CBO’s projections.

# Appendix C: Projections of Per-Beneficiary Spending in Medicare Advantage and the Medicare Fee-for-Service Program
The Congressional Budget Office projects that over the
next 10 years, average per-beneficiary spending will be
5 to 10 percent higher for Medicare Advantage (MA)
enrollees than for enrollees in the Medicare fee-forservice (FFS) program with both Part A and Part B
coverage. This appendix describes how CBO estimated
per-beneficiary spending in MA and Medicare FFS in
this report, how payments are determined in the two
programs, and the factors that contribute to differences
in their per-beneficiary spending.
### Per-Beneficiary Spending
CBO’s projections of per-beneficiary spending in this
report were developed from the total amounts the government spends on Medicare FFS and MA under current
law. To estimate per-person spending in Medicare FFS,
the agency used its baseline projections of per-person
spending for Medicare Parts A and B. Part A spending
was adjusted downward to account for three categories of
spending that benefit both MA and FFS enrollees but are
always paid for through Medicare Part A: hospice, indirect medical education spending, and graduate medical
education spending. Part A spending was then adjusted
upward to reflect the fact that some FFS beneficiaries are
enrolled only in Part A, and those beneficiaries typically
spend less than the average FFS beneficiary because they
have an alternative source of primary coverage, such as
an employment-based plan. That upward adjustment
made CBO’s estimates of FFS spending more representative of enrollees in both Parts A and B, and therefore
more comparable to spending for MA beneficiaries.
CBO’s projections of per-beneficiary spending in MA
reflect total spending on the program, divided by enrollment. Those spending totals are not adjusted to reflect
the relative health of MA beneficiaries compared with
FFS beneficiaries or differences in the benefits covered
by the two programs. As a result, to the extent that MA
beneficiaries are healthier than FFS beneficiaries, those
differences will contribute to differences in the programs’
total spending. Likewise, MA plans provide supplemental benefits that are not provided through Medicare FFS,
such as dental, vision, or hearing benefits, transportation
services, or reduced cost sharing. The federal payments
that support those benefits also contribute to differences
in total spending.
### Federal Payments to MA Plans
Federal subsidies are determined differently in Medicare
FFS and MA. In Medicare FFS, spending primarily
consists of payments to hospitals, physicians, and other
health care providers for services provided to beneficiaries. Spending is therefore driven by the quantity of services used and the prices paid for those services. In MA,
by contrast, the federal government pays private insurers
a fixed amount per enrollee, and insurers bear the cost of
covered services provided to beneficiaries.
The amount paid to insurers for a particular enrollee in
a given plan is determined by a formula. That formula is
based on three key factors:
• A benchmark set by the federal government. The
benchmark is the maximum amount that the
government will pay for an MA beneficiary with the
same health status as the average FFS beneficiary.
Benchmarks are adjusted on the basis of relative FFS
spending levels across counties and are increased
for MA plans whose quality metrics meet a certain
threshold under the Quality Bonus Program.
• A bid submitted by an insurer. A bid indicates the
amount that the insurer agrees to accept for providing
the benefits covered by Medicare Part A and Part
B for an MA beneficiary with the same expected
b health status as the average FFS beneficiary. The bid
incorporates the MA plan’s projected payments to
providers, administrative costs, and profits.
• A risk score assigned to the enrollee. Risk scores
are designed to capture how much more or less
covered medical spending a beneficiary is expected
to incur, compared with the average beneficiary in
Medicare FFS.
For each enrollee, the insurer receives a base payment
multiplied by that enrollee’s risk score. How the base
payment is set depends on whether the plan’s bid is
below or above the benchmark in the enrollee’s county of
residence. If the bid is below the benchmark, the insurer
receives the bid amount plus a “rebate”—a percentage
(between 50 and 70 percent) of the difference between
the bid and the benchmark. Plans are required to spend
those rebates on supplemental benefits for enrollees. If
the bid is above the benchmark, the insurer receives only
the benchmark amount and collects an additional premium from enrollees to cover the difference between the
bid and the benchmark. In 2026, nearly all MA plans
bid below their benchmarks.
### Factors Contributing to Differences Between MA and Medicare FFS in Per-Beneficiary Spending
Differences between MA and Medicare FFS in
per-beneficiary spending reflect differences between MA
and FFS beneficiaries’ risk scores, features of the MA
payment system, and differences in the benefits covered
by the two programs.
One factor contributing to higher per-beneficiary
spending in MA is higher average risk scores among MA
enrollees compared with Medicare FFS beneficiaries.
Those higher average risk scores among MA beneficiaries are partly due to different coding behaviors of MA
plans and FFS providers (that is, differences in how
MA plans and FFS providers document beneficiaries’
diagnoses). Because risk scores increase on the basis of
enrollees’ recorded diagnoses, and payments to plans
increase on the basis of enrollees’ risk scores, MA plans
have stronger incentives than FFS providers to document diagnoses completely. That difference in coding
behavior, referred to as coding intensity, can cause MA
beneficiaries to have higher risk scores than FFS beneficiaries with similar underlying health. 1 To account for
coding intensity, CMS applies a statutorily mandated
adjustment to risk scores—a decrease of 5.9 percent—in
its calculation of payments for MA plans. However, most
estimates suggest that, even after that adjustment, risk
scores are about 4 percent higher for a given MA enrollee
than they would be for an FFS enrollee with similar
health. Higher risk scores contribute to higher spending
per enrollee in MA than in Medicare FFS. 2
Although risk scores are higher in MA than in FFS
because of greater coding intensity, the difference is
partly offset by differences in health between enrollees in
the two programs. Many studies comparing the health
of FFS and MA enrollees suggest that those in MA are
healthier. To the degree that better health is reflected
in lower risk scores, Medicare will pay MA plans less
for covering healthier enrollees, reducing per-enrollee
spending in MA. 3
Differences between county-level MA benchmarks and
average spending in Medicare FFS also contribute to
differences between the two programs in per-beneficiary
spending. For example, benchmarks are set as a percentage of local Medicare FFS spending, ranging from 95 to
115 percent; higher percentages are assigned to counties
whose FFS spending is lower than the national average.
That geographic variation in benchmarks generally leads
plans to enter counties with higher benchmarks at higher
rates than they would otherwise, thereby increasing
spending. Benchmarks are also increased by 5 or 10 per- 4

### Implications for CBO’s Cost Estimates
For the purposes of this report, CBO reported per-person
spending amounts with minimal adjustments. In estimating the effects of policy proposals, CBO considers
how Medicare’s payments would change if beneficiaries
switched between MA and FFS. Those changes cannot
be determined from the projected differences in spending
for the programs in this report, which reflect payments
for different populations rather than payments for the
same beneficiaries under different sources of coverage.
Because beneficiaries are not observed in both MA and
FFS simultaneously, estimating the federal cost of their
coverage in each program requires statistical methods.
Currently, CBO estimates that if a beneficiary switched
from Medicare FFS to MA, federal spending on that
enrollee would increase by roughly 15 percent. That
increase would be driven primarily by coding intensity and favorable selection. Other factors, such as the
Quality Bonus Program and geographic differences in
benchmarks, would have smaller or offsetting effects.
CBO’s estimate is based on its assessment of the empirical literature. CBO regularly reviews the literature,
monitors regulatory changes made to risk adjustment,
and reassesses its methods in light of new evidence.

# About This Document
Each year, the Congressional Budget Office issues a series of publications describing its projections
of the federal budget. This report provides background information that helps explain some of the
projections in the most recent of those publications. In keeping with CBO’s mandate to provide
objective, impartial analysis, the report makes no recommendations.
Jessica Hale and Emily Vreeland prepared the report with contributions from Carolyn Burton,
Caroline Hanson, Claire Hou, Daria Pelech, Allison Percy, and Noah Zwiefel (formerly of CBO) and
with guidance from Berna Demiralp, Sean Dunbar, Sarah Masi, and Sarah Sajewski. Nianyi Hong,
Ben Hopkins, Sean Lyons, Eamon Molloy, Romain Parsad, Aaron Pervin (formerly of CBO),
Robert Stewart, Rajan Topiwala (formerly of CBO), Amy Zettle, and the staff of the Joint Committee
on Taxation also contributed to the analysis. Chad Chirico, Tamara Hayford, John McClelland,
Alexandra Minicozzi, Asha Saavoss, and Chapin White offered comments. Ravza Aykan,
Cyrus Ekland, Ruth Payne, and Youstiena Shafeek fact-checked the report.
Jeffrey Kling reviewed the report. Christine Browne edited it, and Jorge Salazar created the graphics
and prepared the text for publication. The report is available at www.cbo.gov/publication/62539.
CBO seeks feedback to make its work as useful as possible. Please send comments to
communications@cbo.gov.
