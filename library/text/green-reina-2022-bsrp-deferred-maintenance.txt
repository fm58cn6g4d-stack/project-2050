Abstract
Owner Occupied housing is generally assumed to be safe and of reasonably high quality in
the United States. Prior research on housing quality using the American Housing Survey
generally finds housing quality is good. Recent research critiquing housing quality measures
found in the AHS and offering alternative measures complicate this assumption by showing
housing quality is more variable than usually assumed and the costs of housing repair needs
are worth billions of dollars a year. In order to better understand the state of housing
quality in owner occupied housing we present information from Philadelphia’s Basic Systems
Repair Program (BSRP). BSRP is an emergency repair program that offers grants to low
income homeowners. From 2009-2019 the city has spect a little over $98 million in repairs
helping nearly 11,000 households. Comparing AHS derived estimates of housing quality to
the BSRP we find that housing quality for owner occupied homes in Philadelphia may be
more severe than is often assumed and that severe housing quality issues disproportionately
affect Black homeowners in the city. Planners concerned with stabilizing neighborhoods,
protecting property values and the racially disparate costs of homeownership should pay
closer to attention to housing quality issues and look at the feasibility of emergency home
repair programs in affected areas.

1

Introduction

The contemporary “equity” turn in planning focuses, in part, on the role that planning, as a field, has played
in exacerbating structural racism in the United States. On the question of housing, planners often focus on
questions concerning access to housing, particularly home ownership. These concerns often cover questions
examining the role of redlining and discrimination on racial wealth inequality and homeownership gaps (Hillier
2003; Gibson 2007). While the field has been principally concerned with expanding access to homeownership,
there is relatively little attention paid to the challenges of owning a home across demographic groups and low
income homeowners more generally. We generally assume that getting a family to own their home is the
primary goal, but the ability of families to remain in and to maintain their homes is equally important.
A major impediment to keeping homeowners in their homes is that of decreased housing quality due to
deferred maintenance. In economically distressed regions and neighborhoods there are potentially significant
numbers of lower income homeowners living in potentially unsafe and uncomfortable units. Substandard and
unsafe housing pose many risks to homeowners and their broader neighborhoods including, but not limited
to: negative mental health and chronic disease outcomes, decreased property taxes and links to violent crime

(South, MacDonald, and Reina 2021; Ruggiero, Rivera, and Cooney 2020; Mallach, n.d.). Even with these
recognized negative individual and community level effects the prevalence of substandard housing is an open
question.
Contemporary studies regarding housing quality run into one major issue– measurement. Studies focused
on measuring housing quality often use the American Housing Survey (AHS), which is limited by its poor
measures of housing quality and relatively small sample size (Eggers and Moumen 2013a, 2013b). The AHS
is the best data source researchers have access to, especially for national level studies, and researchers expend
a lot intellectual energy attempting to maximize what they can get from it. In addition to measurement
concerns, studies on housing quality often focus on rental housing as opposed to owner-occupied units and
often have difficulty disaggregating populations of interest by income levels or racial/ethnic differences either
due to a lack of data or poor sample size (Ruggiero, Rivera, and Cooney 2020; Divringi et al. 2019). These
issues suggest that researchers need to look at novel sources of data to gain a better understanding of the
state of housing quality.
In order to offer some estimates on the burden of poor housing quality on low income homeowners we
performed three analyses. First, using the AHS, we offer an alternative estimate of housing quality and
explore the differential rates of housing quality by race/ethnicity, income groupings and by geography, focusing
on the Philadelphia metropolitan area. Second, using city administrative data from Philadelphia’s Basic
Systems Repair program, we offer direct estimates of the need for emergency home repairs for low income
homeowners in the city. Finally, in an attempt to better explore what unit or individual level variables may
effect levels of deferred maintenance we performed a series of regressions on the BSRP dataset on household
repair expenses paid by the program. Our results provide a mixed picture.
Using the AHS, we find that reported poor housing quality is relatively rare among homeowners, overall,
though low income, Black and Hispanic homeowners report higher levels of poor quality housing than higher
income or White homeowners. Additionally, the AHS data for the Philadelphia metro area shows higher
levels of poor quality housing than the rest of the nation with Black homeowners, in particular, reporting
poor quality housing. Focusing on the city of Philadelphia, we take ten years of Basic Systems Repair
administrative data and find that of the approximately 11,000 households selected for repairs, the average
cost of repairs is approximately $8,900 while the city has spent nearly $100 million on repairs. Given the
thousands of homeowners on BSRP’s waitlist, it is possible that tens of thousands of low income homeowners
in Philadelphia not only have poorer quality housing, but have acute housing repair needs necessary for basic
survival. And, finally, the regressions do not highlight any one set of variables, either at the householder or
unit level, that explain housing repair costs.
While we cannot offer broader estimates on the prevalence of deferred maintenance or poor quality housing
due to the limitations of the data we do provide direct measures of the burden of poor quality housing for
low income homeowners in Philadelphia using city administrative data. These data are suggestive and should
encourage planners, and housing specialists, to invest in research programs to better estimate the prevalence
of deferred maintenance in their respective markets, and, in particular, the burden that deferred maintenance
places on non-White and low income households.

2

Literature Review

2.1

Planning’s Role (or lack thereof) In Housing

In a 2012 review, Bates (2012) highlights the relative lack of planning influence in housing in the UK and
the US. Planning generally indirectly dictates the provision of housing through land use policy with private
development interests largely responsible for the ultimate production and placement of housing. The result is
a vitally important system that lacks the benefits of a planning approach, namely, a comprehensive view
of systems, a future orientation and attention to geographic scale. The end result of this disjuncture is
that certain planning regulations, such as local zoning, have an outsized influence on the overall supply of
housing, but planners largely lack a comprehensive understanding of housing supply, quality or future growth
dynamics.
This indirect role planning plays with respect to housing is relatively recent. The rise, and fall, of public
housing and the legacies of urban renewal are a testament to the role planning, at multiple scales, played in the
provision of housing (Vale and Freemark 2012; Goetz 2012). The construction of public housing, in particular,
is notable not just for the public production of housing, but in the extensive surveys of housing quality and
the concern (however cynical it may have been at points) with the proliferation of slum housing conditions.

With the retreat of the federal government in funding public housing there have not been equivalent surveys
on the quality of housing, writ large, in cities. The result is that planners often have little idea of the state of
the quality of housing in their respective jurisdictions, and, therefore, cannot offer any potential guidance on
alleviating housing quality issues if they exist.
Of course, planners have much to say on planning and housing remains a core issue in all cities, especially
those in distressed markets. (2016) gives an extensive review of the literature on neighborhood decline
in legacy cities. While covering a wide array of issues, Mallach highlights the role of both vacant and
degraded properties in declining neighborhood fortunes and the necessity to design policies to arrest these
specific patterns in order to better stabilize neighborhoods. More recently, (2020) finds positive effects on
the demolition of vacant and degraded housing on the repair decisions of nearby homeowners. This body
of research highlights the central importance of housing quality to contemporary housing issues in weaker
markets and declining cities, but while this body of research explores specific cases in particular cities the
question of broader housing quality remains largely absent in the planning literature.
2.2

Housing Quality Is Hard to Estimate

While planning practice has largely turned away from studies of housing quality in a systematic sense over
the past decades, the issue of housing quality has remained an issue for local governments and in academic
research in related fields such as public administration, housing studies and public health. Topics include the
effect of housing repairs on local crime rates, the role that demolitions in degraded housing play in terms of
affecting property prices and the ability of older homeowners to effectively manage repairs in order to safely
age in place (South, MacDonald, and Reina 2021; Begley and Lambie-Hanson 2015; Kuhlmann 2020).
While such studies illuminate housing quality issues at the local level, studies at larger scales that attempt
to estimate the scale and scope of housing quality suffer from major measurement problems. The primary
survey source on housing quality for the US, as a whole, is the American Housing Survey (AHS), a biannual
survey conducted jointly by the Department and Housing and Urban Development and the Census Bureau.
In a 2013 piece, Eggers and Moumen (2013b), highlight issues with using the AHS to measure housing quality
and offer their own alternative measure. First, they note that researchers will often use an already provided
measure in the AHS called ZADEQ. ZADEQ is a three-point variable that purportedly measures housing
adequacy and not quality. Still, researchers will often use it because it is what the AHS provides even as it
is generally recognized to be insufficient for measuring quality. Second, they also found that measures of
adequacy were not stable across years and that some variables that would be used for tracking adequacy
were not present across all sample years further confusing the measure.
In a follow up report, Eggers and Moumen (2013a) offer a solution to the ZADEQ variable issue that many
other researchers have taken- providing their own measure. Their answer was to design the “Poor Quality
Index” (PQI), a 35-variable weighted measure of housing quality that uses many of the variables that go into
the construction of ZADEQ variable, but applies individual weights instead of a large aggregation approach.
For each individual variable, the authors applied a weight to individual answers to questions. They generally
find the PQI as preferable as it allows for more variation across units to be explored and they also find general
stability over time across surveys. We use a modified version of the PQI in this study due to it being based
on the AHS, the basic calculations are publicly documented and one of the authors was willing to provide
their SAS script for us to make sure we could properly reproduce the measure.
Even with all of the work that went into the development of the PQI, Eggers and Moumen (2013a) still find
rather low rates of poor housing quality in the US, and, in fact, find that housing quality, on the whole, had
largely improved over time at the time of printing. This is a relatively common finding, especially in studies
using the AHS, that housing quality issues are generally rare and that housing quality is largely improving.
That basic conclusion may be correct, but using the AHS still remains an issue. First, while it is nationally
representative, the AHS has a relatively small sample size that make estimating housing quality in particular
metropolitan areas difficult and makes comparing social differences in exposure to housing quality at finer
scales a tricky proposition. Second, while the PQI, or other housing quality measures, offer better construct
validity than the ZADEQ variable, the indices remain relatively hard to interpret with respect to what they
mean for the daily experiences of residents. And, finally, many studies look at all occupied housing in the
AHS mixing rental and owner-occupied properties making it difficult to isolate housing quality issues for
homeowners.
One approach to the problem of construct validity regarding housing quality is to estimate the financial
cost of home repairs as opposed to listing them individually. A recent study from the Philadelphia Federal
Reserve does just this: first, they modify their PQI type measure by developing a cost-based index on the

estimated cost of needed home repairs; and, second, they make use of a hierarchical clustering approach in
order to develop a typology of households in order to gain a better understanding of the variation in housing
quality needs across different household types (Divringi et al. 2019). The report from the Fed estimates that
in 2018 there were approximately $126.9 billion in needed home repairs. Additionally, they find higher cost
burdens for non-White households, particularly Hispanic/Latinx, Black and Native American households.
The Fed report offers an alternative measure of home repair that gives a better view of the actual burden poor
quality housing though it still suffers from the weaknesses of using the AHS with respect to sample size and
representativeness. In examining the Philadelphia MSA, the Fed report estimated a $2.7 billion repair need
largely concentrated on low income housing occupants and older homeowners. Beyond the topline value, the
Fed estimated that nearly 20% of needed repairs in the Philadelphia MSA would cost between $1,000-5,000.
Additionally, nearly half of Black and Hispanic/Latinx households reported repair needs compared to a 1/3
of White or Asian households.

3

Data and Methodology

In order to offer some alternative measures on the prevalence of substandard housing in Philadelphia, we
make use of two datasets: the 2019 American Housing Survey and ten years of Basic Systems Repair Program
administrative data. The AHS is the principal source of information on residents and structural attributes
for researchers.
3.1

American Housing Survey

Performed in conjunction with the Census Bureau, the AHS offers hundreds of variables on individual
borrowers, households and structural attributes for housing units. It is a biannual nationally representative
survey.
Following Eggers and Moumen (2013a) we calculated a modified Poor Quality Index (PQI) and provide
descriptive statistics of the prevalence and burden of poor housing quality by sociodemographic factors,
income as well as disability. The PQI is a composite measure of housing quality based on 35 variables found
in the AHS. We modified the PQI by removing the question on elevators as we limited our sample to owner
occupied single family attached and detached homes. The theoretical maximum score with this modified PQI
is 162. Eggers and Moumen (2013a) go into detail on the calculation of the PQI in detail, but it is important
to have a basic idea of the measure. The PQI is a composite measure based on the weighted responses to
certain questions regarding housing quality in their unit. Eggers and Moumen (2013a) decided on the weights
on individual questions. For example, if a homeowner reports a unit does not have electricity that question
receives a score of 10, whereas the question regarding the number of the unit’s heating equipment broke down
for 6 hours or more would have a maximum score of 16 if a respondent answered 8 times or more.
Additionally, we recoded race ethnicity variables to include Non-Hispanic White, Black, American Indian,
Asian, Hawaiian and Pacific Islander, Other and Hispanic/Latinx. AHS offers a richer array of racial/ethnic
categories, but cell sizes become prohibitively small requiring aggregation.
3.2

Basic Systems Repair Program

BSRP is an emergency home repair program for qualified low income homeowners in the city of Philadelphia.
It is one of multiple programs designed to assist low income homeowners and to encourage neighborhood
stability through the Philadelphia Housing Development Corporation. BSRP provides grants of up to $20,000
for structural emergency repairs. These repairs may range from unsafe electric systems to leaky roofs or
compromised plumbing systems. Recent research finds BSRP repairs were linked with reductions in violent
crime highlighting additional benefits to the program (South, MacDonald, and Reina 2021).
BSRP is designed to serve the most extreme cases of deferred maintenance and vulnerable homeowners. While
applications are open, the program has strict income requirements, for example, a household of four may
have not have a total gross income exceeding $63,250. Beyond meeting the income requirements, applicants
may not have a current lien on their property, own any other property and must have proof of need of an
emergency repair. While some households that apply are turned away, the vast majority of applicants that
are not served are due to a lack of funds, not because they do not meet program standards.
Preparing the BSRP data involved a few steps. Client data and repair data are in two separate tables.
We matched the primary client table, which includes all households that applied to the program, with the
contractor table that includes information on the repairs performed for accepted households. In addition to

Results

4.1

American Housing Survey

Echoing earlier works, the 2019 AHS shows relatively modest values of the PQI, overall, signaling that the
majority of housing in the United States is of good quality (Divringi et al. 2019; Eggers and Moumen 2013a).
Philadelphia, while having a higher average PQI than the rest of nation, as well as more extreme values, still
has relatively modest PQI scores (Table 1).
While the prevalence and severity of poorer quality housing is relatively low in the AHS, low income homeowners
are still disproportionately affected by poorer quality housing both nationally and in Philadelphia. PQI
by household income quartiles illustrates the pattern that lower income households have poorer quality
housing than higher income households at both the national level and the Philadelphia metropolitan region.
Homeowners in Philadelphia generally report larger PQI scores at every income level except for the lowest
income quartile. The greatest reported difference in housing quality is found in the second income quartile.
When examining racial and ethnic difference, Black and Hispanic/Latinx respondents report poorer quality
housing than other groups in both the Philadelphia metro area as well as nationally. American Indians
report the worst quality housing, overall, nationally, but the AHS lacks any such respondents in the
Philadelphia metro. A striking feature of this disproportionate exposure is that Black and Hispanic/Latinx
homeowners report significantly poorer quality housing in the Philadelphia metro compared to other Black
or Hispanic/Latinx owners, nationally. This speaks to the more degraded housing stock in Philadelphia,
generally, but it should be noted that the differences in average PQI scores for Philadelphia versus the rest of
the country for these groups are much larger compared to others.
A more detailed breakdown of the regional and racial/ethnic differences in PQI illustrates the intra-racial
distribution of poorer quality housing. Black-headed households have higher PQI scores across all income
quartiles than other racial groups, but the most extreme differences in PQI between the Phialdelphia metro
area and the rest of the country lies in the difference of homeowners within the second income quartile.
Conversely, Hispanic/Latinx have more even PQI scores between Philadelphia and the rest of the nation
except at the lowest income quartile where the average PQI is more than double that of lower income
Hispanic/Latinx homeowners in the rest of the country.
4.2

BSRP

The BSRP data allow us to take a more granular view of housing quality issues for low income homeowners
as well as to offer analysts estimates of the burden of deferred maintenance. While the BSRP is not a
representative sample of all homeowners in the city, the program from 2009-2019 had over 45,000 applicants
and nearly a 1/3 applications have been approved. Of those nearly 15,000 approved repairs we were able to
link 10,908 of BSRP repairs to city parcel data. Of those 10,908, 9,893, or 90%, were of distinct owners. The
remaining difference were owners who were able to receive multiple repairs.
A review of the participants compared to 2019 five-year American Community Survey data show that BSRP
homeowners are significantly poorer, older and much more Black than the city of Philadelphia, as a whole.

Discussion

A review of the AHS with a modified PQI score echoes earlier work in finding a generally low prevalence
and severity of home repair needs in owner occupied single family residences at the national level. But as
recent work has highlighted, current estimates of home quality are flawed and call into question the construct
validity of AHS-based housing quality measures. Issues regarding small sample size, appropriate questions
and a lack of standardization, such as through estimated repair costs, obscures the burden of housing quality
issues (Divringi et al. 2019). While the AHS is the best available source we have with a rich array of variables,
it is largely not usable at any geography finer than the MSA level, and even at the MSA level cell counts
become prohibitively small. This makes exploring housing issues by race/ethnicity or income levels impossible
except at a much larger scale.
Even recognizing the limits of the AHS in estimating housing quality issues, the work of the Federal Reserve
highlights how burdensome deferred maintenance truly is. Taking the aggregated calculations from Divringi
et al (2019), nationally, they identified average costs to homeowners at $3,842. Their estimates for the
Philadelphia MSA report an average repair cost of $3,176 dollars with over a 1/3 of homeowners reporting
some form of repair need. Even at lower median costs, the estimated burden on low income households is
non-trivial. For example, BSRP participants in our sample reported a median annual income of $12,000. The
average repair cost for the MSA is over a 1/4 of their annual income, and from the administrative data we
found that average repair costs were over $8,000, or 3/4 of reported annual income.
The BSRP data offer an alternative and detailed examination of the extreme repair needs in Philadelphia. Of
the 11,087 approved repairs from 2009-2019, the average cost of repairs at $8,908 dollars represents not just
the severity of degraded housing, but the immense burden such repairs pose to households. Additionally,
the racially disparate participation rates of the BSRP highlight the relatively unique burden that deferred
maintenance plays in the experiences of low income Black homeowners. In an already poor city, severely
degraded housing is primarily a Black homeowner problem. While the BSRP represents a self-selected sample,

the older housing stock and greater prevalence of low income homeowners in the city signal that unaddressed
home repair needs may be in the hundreds of millions of dollars.
While we cannot offer estimates of the overall prevalence of severe deferred maintenance as we find in the
BSRP data we can offer some nominal estimates to get a better idea of what the current burden on low
income households in Philadelphia is. We pulled five year ACS 2019 data for owner-occupied housing income
for households that make up to $35,000 a year. From the ACS, there were 97,848 households in Philadelphia
that reported up to $35,000 of income. We then took all unique BSRP addresses that had repairs from
2015-2019 and reported, at most, $35,000 in income. Of the 5,677 unique addresses that received repairs from
2015-2019, 5,587 or 98% made less than or equal to $35,000, thus BSRP households make up approximately
5.7% of households making up to $35,000 in Philadelphia. This is not a stringent estimate of the prevalence of
deferred maintenance but to highlight the relatively large share of low income households who not only require
emergency repairs, but received funding. It is likely that the demand for such repairs, and the prevalence of
home repair needs, for lower income households is non-trivial.
Regression models on the BSRP data show the difficulty in predicting the burden of deferred maintenance
using cross sectional data. Common unit and individual level variables are relatively weak predictors of
the severity of deferred maintenance, though the livable area and market value of the home are consistent
strong predictors across both specifications. These results potentially highlight the fact that estimating the
burden of deferred maintenance is dependent upon a more dynamic, temporally based set of processes such
as neighborhood or economic decline and may not be possible to isolate well in a cross sectional approach.

6

Conclusion

The prevalence and severity of poor housing quality is unclear in the US. National level estimates often
make use of the AHS, a national level survey with well recognized limitations with respect to the variables
collected and sample size. The result is that studies that do attempt to estimate housing quality using the
AHS generally find modest to minuscule rates of inadequate/poor quality housing. Due to the limitations of
the AHS and the pressing policy need in cities like Philadelphia that need to maintain and upgrade their
housing stock, alternative measures and datasets for measuring housing quality are necessary.
We made use of a decade Philadelphia’s Basic Systems Repair Program data in order to to gauge the burden
poor housing quality places on low income homeowners in the city. Within that decade, the city performed
over 11,000 repairs with nearly 40,000 homeowners applying, and being rejected, or placed on the program’s
waitlist. Over that decade the city has spent $100 million with an average repair cost of $8,900. While this
is a self-selected sample the number of program applicants, the high average cost of repairs and growth in
repairs performed over time imply that housing quality in Philadelphia, at least at the lower ends of the
housing market, may be especially acute.
Taking these results into account as well as the recognized limitations of the AHS, cities/counties should
seriously consider collecting their own data on local housing quality. This need may be especially urgent in
larger, older and lower income urban areas such as Philadelphia, Baltimore or Cleveland. Additionally, the
challenges facing low income homeowners in these cities should encourage planners to revisit assumptions
they may hold regarding homeowners. Homeownership is generally the stated goal of many policies as well
as a normative push in US society. As such, the question of whether homeowners can adequately maintain,
or keep, their homes are often secondary concerns, but become urgent questions in regions with relative
high homeownership but larger proportions of low income homeowners and weaker housing markets overall.
Pennsylvania recently passed the Whole Home Repair Program to provide funding for counties to address
single and multifamily deferred maintenance as well as energy efficiency programs. In Pennsylvania, at least,
the issue of deferred maintenance is now officially a statewide concern.
Relatedly, Black homeowners disproportionately suffer from degraded housing more than other racial/ethnic
groups (American Indians also report high levels of degraded housing, but insufficient sample size makes
comparisons difficult). The causes of this disproportionate racial exposure is unclear and further research
should focus on whether Black homeowners are steered into lower quality housing due to Black borrowers
lower incomes and it should call into the question the value of homeownership as a primary goal for Black
people. Or homeownership programs towards Black Americans should potentially include information and
additional funding for housing upkeep and repair.
Housing quality remains a black box across the country. Rental housing must often meet safety requirements
in many areas, but inspection regimes are generally uneven and under resourced. Owner-occupied units are

largely unknown to inspection offices unless an owner wants to make some change to their unit or to sell
their house. As such, we have very little to zero knowledge of the physical state of owner occupied housing in

prolonged disinvestment. Given the importance of safe and well maintained housing for personal health and
safety, as well as neighborhood stabilization, cities should investigate ways to gain a better understanding of
housing quality issues, and, if necessary, design programs to help property owners to repair and upgrade their
properties.
