PREFACE
This publication summarizes the fiscal year (FY) 2027 Budget for the U.S. Department of Agriculture
(USDA). Throughout this publication any reference to the “Budget” is in regard to the 2027 Budget,
unless otherwise noted. All references to years refer to fiscal year, except where specifically noted.
The budgetary tables throughout this document show actual amounts for 2024 and 2025; Working
Families Tax Cut Act; Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related
Agency Appropriations Act, 2026; and the President’s Budget request for 2027. Amounts for 2026
estimated levels include: non-enacted amounts such as Full-Time Equivalent levels, fleet levels,
information technology investment levels, recovery levels, transfers in and out, balances available end
of year, and obligation levels.
Throughout this publication, the Working Families Tax Cut Act is used to refer to the Public Law 11921.
Pursuant to the Balanced Budget and Emergency Deficit Control Act of 1985, sequestration is included
in the numbers for mandatory programs in 2024, 2025, 2026 and 2027.
In tables throughout this document, amounts equal to zero (0) are displayed as dashes (-). Amounts
less than 0.5 and greater than zero are rounded and shown as a zero (0). This display treatment is
used to prevent the masking of small non-zero amounts that do not round up to one (1). Due to
rounding, some tables may not sum exactly.

AGENCY-WIDE
PURPOSE STATEMENT
The USDA Rural Utilities Service (RUS) enhances the quality of life and improves economic
opportunity in rural communities by providing financing for the basic infrastructure of modern life.
Electricity, telecommunications, and water and waste systems are essential services for individuals
and businesses alike that assist communities with creating wealth, supporting self-sustaining and
repopulating communities, and helping to improve rural economies.
Electric Programs:
Direct Loans — Authorized by section 4 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as amended, (7 U.S.C.
904) and bear interest at the government’s cost of money (or the current Treasury rate). The
program’s purpose is to finance electric distribution, transmission, and generation systems, and for
demand side management, energy efficiency and conservation programs, and renewable energy
systems to serve rural areas.
Hardship Rate Direct Loans — Authorized by section 4 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as
amended, (7 U.S.C. 904) to finance electric system improvements for qualified borrowers that meet
thresholds for retail rate disparity and for customer per capita and household incomes, or that have
suffered a severe, unavoidable hardship, such as a natural disaster. No private financing is required.
Hardship rate loans at a fixed rate of five percent are also offered under section 305 of the Rural
Electrification Act of 1936 (7 U.S.C. 935).
Municipal Rate Direct Loans — Authorized by section 305 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as
amended, (7 U.S.C. 935) to finance the electric system improvements of eligible distribution
borrowers. Interest rates are set quarterly based on municipal bond market rates for similar
maturities and determined at time of each loan advance. Private financing of 30 percent of the total
loan is required for most projects.
Guaranteed Loans — Authorized by section 306 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as amended,
(7 U.S.C. 936) to finance electric distribution, transmission, and generation systems (including
renewable energy), headquarters facilities, and programs for energy efficiency, conservation, and
demand side management. The Federal Financing Bank (FFB) is the primary source of guaranteed

loans and fixes interest rates to the prevailing cost of money to the Treasury, plus an administrative
fee of one eighth of one percent. USDA-guaranteed private loans issued through National Rural
Utilities Cooperative Financing Corporation, Co Bank or other private lenders carry an interest rate
negotiated between the lender and the borrower.
Guarantees for Bonds and Notes Issued for Utility Infrastructure Loans — Authorized by section 313A
of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 940c-1), to guarantee payments on
certain bonds or notes issued by qualified cooperative or other non-profit lenders. The note proceeds
must be used for financing eligible utility infrastructure and must be of investment grade. Notes have
been obtained through the FFB with interest rates equal to the Treasury rate for notes of similar
maturities plus an annual fee of 30 basis points on any unpaid principal balance.
Energy Efficiency and Conservation Loan Program — Authorized by section 317 of the Rural
Electrification Act of 1936, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 940g) to finance facilities that generate electricity
from solar, wind, hydropower, biomass, or geothermal source for resale to rural and nonrural
residents. Annual appropriations acts have included a provision to exempt the interest rate
requirement for this program. USDA administers the program under the Direct Loan FFB authority
under Section 306 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 936).
Rural Energy Savings Program — Authorized by section 6407 of the Farm Security and Rural
Investment Act of 2002 (7 U.S.C. 8107), as amended, to offer zero-interest loans to eligible
borrowers for relending to qualified consumers to implement durable cost-effective energy efficiency
measures.
High Energy Cost Grants — Authorized by section 19 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 (7 U.S.C.
918a) to fund energy generation, transmission, and distribution facilities, including energy efficiency
and conservation programs and renewable energy systems serving rural communities with average
home energy costs exceeding 275 percent of the national average. Program funds are also used for
RUS grants to the Denali Commission to carry out eligible projects in small remote villages in Alaska
and to eligible State entities to establish revolving funds for bulk fuel purchases for certain
communities where fuel deliveries by surface transportation is not available year-round.
Empowering Rural America (New ERA) Program – Authorized by Section 22004 of the Inflation
Reduction Act of 2022 (P.L. 117-169) to assist eligible entities in achieving the greatest reduction in
Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions while advancing the long-term resiliency, reliability, and
affordability of rural electric systems. The program provides loans, grants and loan and grant
combinations to member-owned rural electric cooperatives to help rural Americans transition to clean,
affordable, and reliable energy. By reducing air and water pollution, New ERA funding improves health
outcomes and lowers energy costs for people in rural communities.
Powering Affordable Clean Energy (PACE) Program – Authorized by Section 22001 of the Inflation
Reduction Act of 2022 (P.L. 117-169) to finance projects that store electricity generated from eligible
renewable energy sources listed under Section 317 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936. This
program provides loans for wind, solar, hydropower, geothermal, or biomass renewable energy
projects. PACE allocates funding across three categories of applicants that can qualify for up to 60
percent total loan forgiveness for designated energy communities or Tribal populations.
Telecommunications Programs:
Hardship (direct loans) — Authorized by section 305(d)(1) of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as
amended, (7 U.S.C. 935(d)(1)) and bear interest at a fixed rate of five percent per year to furnish and
improve telecommunications services, including a wide array of telecommunication related services, in
rural areas. The program is intended only for borrowers with extremely high investment costs in
terms of per subscriber service, has a very low number of subscribers for each mile of
telecommunications line constructed and cannot typically afford higher interest rate loans. This low
subscriber “density” inherently increases the cost to serve the most sparsely populated rural areas.

Direct Loans (or Treasury rate loans) — Authorized by section 305(d)(2) of the Rural Electrification Act
of 1936, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 935(d)(2)) and bear interest at the government’s cost of money (or
the current Treasury rate). The program’s purpose is to furnish and improve telecommunications
services, including a wide array of telecommunication related services, in rural areas.
Guaranteed Loans — Authorized by section 306 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as amended,
(7 U.S.C. 936) and are provided to borrowers of a non-government lender or from the Federal
Financing Bank (FFB) to furnish and improve telecommunications services, including a wide array of
telecommunication related services, in rural areas. The interest rate charged on FFB loans is the
Treasury rate plus an administrative fee of one-eighth of one percent. The terms of these loans may
vary significantly and allow borrowers more flexibility in meeting their financing needs.
Broadband Loans — Authorized by section 601 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, as amended, (7
U.S.C. 950bb). This program provides funding for the cost of constructing, improving, and acquiring
facilities and equipment for broadband service in rural communities of 20,000 inhabitants or less.
Direct loans are made at the cost of money to the Treasury for the life of the facilities financed.
Community Connect Broadband Grants — Authorized by section 306(a)(20)(E) of the Consolidated
Farm and Rural Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926(a)(20)(E)) to fund eligible
entities to provide public access to broadband in otherwise un-served communities. Funds must be
used to deploy broadband service and offer free broadband service to critical community facilities for
two years.
Distance Learning and Telemedicine Loans and Grants — Authorized by sections 2331 to 2335A of the
Food, Agriculture, Conservation, and Trade Act of 1990 (7 U.S.C. 950aaa et seq.), as amended, to
assist rural schools and learning centers to gain access to improved educational resources and assist
rural hospitals and health care centers to gain access to improved medical resources. Telemedicine
projects are providing new and improved health care services and benefits to rural residents, many in
medically underserved areas, by linking to urban medical centers for clinical interactive video
consultation, distance training of rural health care providers, and access to medical expertise and
library resources. Distance learning projects provide funding for Internet-based educational services in
schools and libraries and promote confidence in, and understanding of, the Internet and its benefits to
students and young entrepreneurs.
Broadband Pilot Loans and Grants (ReConnect) Program — Established by section 779 of the
Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018 (P.L. 115-141) and funded in subsequent years. The program’s
purpose is to conduct a new broadband loan and grant pilot program under the Rural Electrification
Act of 1936, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 901 et seq.) to fund broadband capable infrastructure in rural
communities where at least 90 percent of the households to be served are without sufficient access to
broadband, defined for this pilot program as 10 Megabits per second (Mbps) downstream, and 1 Mbps
upstream, provided that funds shall not be used to overbuild or duplicate broadband expansion efforts
of any entity that has received a broadband loan from RUS.
Water and Environmental Programs:
Direct Water and Waste Disposal Loans — Authorized by section 306 of the Consolidated Farm and
Rural Development Act of 1972, as amended (7 U.S.C. 1926). The program’s purpose is to develop
water and wastewater systems, including solid waste disposal and storm drainage, in rural areas and
in cities and towns with a population of 10,000 or less. Priority is given to communities with
populations of 5,500 or less.
Guaranteed Water and Waste Disposal Loans — Authorized by section 306 of the Consolidated Farm
and Rural Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926) for the same purpose as direct
loans. The loans are made and serviced by private lenders, such as banks, and savings and loan
associations with the guarantee for up to 90 percent of an eligible loss incurred by the lender. The

Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 (P.L. 115-334) authorized the program to service eligible rural
populations up to 50,000 and prioritize communities of 10,000 or less.
Water and Waste Disposal Grants — Authorized by section 306A of the Consolidated Farm and Rural
Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926). The program’s purpose is to reduce water
and waste disposal costs to a reasonable level for users of the system. Grants may be made, in some
instances, up to 75 percent of eligible project costs.
Water and Waste Facility Loans and Grants to alleviate health risks — Authorized by section 306C of
the Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926) to assist
low-income rural communities whose residents face significant health risks because they do not have
access to water supply systems or waste disposal facilities. The grants are available to provide water
or waste services to designated Colonias in Arizona, California, New Mexico, and Texas, and are also
available to Native American tribes with the funds specifically intended to be used for eligible projects
benefiting members of federally recognized Native American tribes. Grants may be made up to 100
percent of the eligible project cost and be used in conjunction with loan monies.
Water and Waste System Grants for Alaskan Villages (including technical assistance programs) —
Authorized by section 306D of the Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act of 1972 (7 U.S.C.
1926). The program’s purpose is to fund water and waste disposal systems in rural Alaskan Villages.
Funds must be used for development and construction of water and wastewater systems to correct
dire health and sanitation conditions in those villages.
Special Evaluation Assistance for Rural Communities and Households Program grants (SEARCH) —
Authorized by section 306 of the Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act of 1972, as
amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926). The program’s purpose is to fund feasibility studies, design assistance,
and technical assistance to financially distressed communities in rural areas with populations of 2,500
or fewer inhabitants for water and waste disposal projects.
Grants for the construction, refurbishment and servicing of low- or moderate-income individual
household water well systems — Authorized by section 306E of the Consolidated Farm and Rural
Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926). The program’s purpose is to provide funds
to non-profit organizations to assist them in establishing loan programs from which homeowners may
borrow money to construct or repair household water well systems.
Revolving Loan Fund Grants — Authorized by section 306 of the Consolidated Farm and Rural
Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926) to enable qualified state and local
government entities, and nonprofits to create a revolving loan fund. The funds can enable applicants
to finance certain pre-development costs and short-term capital projects. Loans may be made by the
intermediary for up to $100,000 and a term of 10 years.
Household Water Well System Grants — Authorized by section 306E of the Consolidated Farm and
Rural Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926(e)) to help qualified nonprofits and
tribes create a revolving loan fund to increase access to clean, reliable water for households in eligible
rural areas who own and occupy a home. The 2018 Farm Bill changed program eligibility to include
individually owned household decentralized wastewater systems and changed income eligibility from
100 percent median non-metropolitan household income of the State to 60 percent.
Solid Waste Management Grants — Authorized by section 310B of the Consolidated Farm and Rural
Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1932) to assist eligible organizations to address
solid waste management issues and threats to water resources from solid waste in rural communities.
Assistance may be provided to enhance operator skills in operations and maintenance, identify threats
to water resources, reduce or eliminate pollution of water resources, improve planning and
management of solid waste disposal facilities in rural areas, and reduce the solid waste stream.
Technical Assistance and Training Grants — Authorized by section 306 of the Consolidated Farm and
Rural Development Act of 1972, as amended, (7 U.S.C. 1926). The program’s purpose is to assist

private non-profit organizations to provide technical assistance and training to communities and utility
systems on issues relating to delivery of water and waste disposal service. For water and waste
disposal facilities, eligible non-profit organizations help identify and evaluate solutions, improve the
operation and maintenance of existing facilities, and prepare loan and grant applications.
All RUS loans are based on extensive feasibility studies that determine a borrower’s ability to repay
the loan. Rural Development (RD) staff monitor loan repayments that are secured through covenants
in loan contracts and mortgage documents with RD. The government obtains a priority position on the
assets of the borrower.
RD is comprised of three agencies: Rural Housing Service, Rural Utilities Service, and Business
Cooperative Service. RD’s headquarters is located in Washington, DC. As of September 30, 2025,
there were 4,450 permanent full-time employees, including 245 in the headquarters office and 4,205
across 461 field offices.
RD activities contribute to the success of USDA’s overall mission to provide leadership on food,
agriculture, natural resources, rural development, nutrition, and related issues based on public policy,
the best available science, and effective management. USDA is currently developing the FY 2026 –
2030 Strategic Plan. A detailed FY 2027 performance plan, including Key Performance Indicators, can
be found at https://www.usda.gov/our-agency/about-usda/performance.

Change Description
The first change (lines 7 and 8 of paragraph 2) deletes language on 306Aand 306E; the budget is not
requesting funding for these purposes.
The second change (line 11 to 20 of paragraph 2) deletes language that provides funding for water
and waste direct one percent loans for distressed communities, water well system grants, and
emergency community water assistance grants. The budget is not requesting funding for this
purpose.
The third change (line 22 of paragraph 2) eliminates language for Colonias. The set-aside is not
requested in the budget.
The fourth change (line 24 of paragraph 2) eliminates language “and the Department of Hawaiian
Home Lands (of the State of Hawaii)” for water and waste loans and grants.
The fourth change (line 25 to 28 of paragraph 2) adds language allowing transfers without written
notification to Congress.
The fifth change (line 35 to 44 of paragraph 2) deletes language that provides funding for technical
assistance grants for rural water and waste systems. The budget is not requesting funding for this
purpose.
The sixth change (line 46 to 55 of paragraph 2) deletes language that provides funding for solid waste
management grants and community project funding grants. It also removes authorization for High
Energy Cost Grants transfer. The budget is not requesting funding for this purpose.
The seventh change (line 56 of paragraph 2) eliminates language “under this heading” for direct loan
subsidy costs.
The eighth change (line 58 to 66 of paragraph 2) deletes language allowing unobligated balances from
prior year to cover the subsidy costs for direct loans, guaranteed loans, and grants.

JUSTIFICATION OF CHANGES
Rural Utilities Service (RUS) programs support a healthy community and environment with loans and
grants to make sure people, kids, and families have clean water and safe sewer systems that prevent
pollution and runoff. RUS programs also keep the people of rural America connected with reliable,
high-speed internet that brings new and innovative ideas to the rest of our country.
RUS provides much-needed infrastructure and infrastructure improvements to rural communities.
These include water and waste treatment, electric power, and telecommunications services. All these
services help to expand economic opportunities and improve the quality of life for rural residents.
The numbers and letters of the following listing relates to values in the Change (Chg) Key column of
the Project Statement:
1. A decrease of $183,128,000 for the Water and Waste Disposal loan program ($1,000,000,000
available in 2026).
The Water and Waste Direct Loan program provides low-interest extended term loans to support
clean and reliable drinking water systems, sanitary sewage disposal, sanitary solid waste
disposal, and storm water drainage facilities to households and businesses in eligible rural areas
of a population of 10,000 or less. Often combined with grant funding, the Water and Waste
Disposal Direct Loans enable communities to maintain reasonable user costs for rural residences,
businesses, and other users.
Adequate and reliable water and waste disposal capacity is critical for supporting quality of life
issues, environmental sustainability, and the economic vitality of rural communities. Rural water
and waste disposal systems face many unique challenges, including a lack of economies of scale,
access to qualified workers, and more. This program helps very small, financially distressed rural
communities extend and improve water and waste disposal facilities that serve local households
and businesses. The availability of these services can save tax dollars through a reduction in
health care costs, improve the natural environment, and help manufacturers and businesses
locate or expand operations in rural areas. As the only federal water and waste disposal program
solely focused on rural America, this funding is crucial to ensure that rural communities have
access to the clean water and waste disposal services they need and deserve.
Applications for this program are accepted throughout the year. Borrowers must have the legal
authority to construct, operate and maintain the proposed services or facilities. All facilities
receiving federal financing must be used for public purposes. Partnerships with other federal,
state, local, private, and nonprofit entities that offer financial assistance are encouraged and the
project must be financially sustainable. The loan term is up to a 40-year payback period, based
on the useful life of the facilities financed. The interest rates are fixed based on the need for the
project and the median household income of the area to be served. The funds may be used to
finance the acquisition, construction or improvement of drinking water sourcing, treatment,
storage, and distribution; sewer collection, transmission, treatment, and disposal; solid waste
collection, transmission, and closure; and storm water collection, transmission and disposal. In
some cases, funding may also be available for related activities including, but not limited to, legal
and engineering fees, land acquisition, water land rights, and permits and equipment.
In 2024, this program experienced increased interest rates, supply and demand challenges,
supply chain issues that drove up material costs. Also, the agency implemented the Build
America Buy American Act requirements, so WEP is working to maintain affordable user rates for
the programs financed. Similar strains are experienced in engineering and other professional
industries that support project development.
The agency has obligated approximately $889 million in the Water and Waste Direct loan
portfolio in 2025. From this total, $40 million of funding went towards persistent poverty areas
and just over $1 million went towards Empowerment Zones and Enterprise Communities loans.
The Water and Waste Direct loan program has funding invested in most states, but the five

states who have received the largest investment from the agency are Iowa, Oklahoma, Arizona,
Michigan, and Idaho.
RUS has awarded nearly $47 million in direct loans in Michigan for several projects completing
sewer system improvements, upgrading drinking water system transmission lines, installing
water meters, replacing public lead service lines, and reducing iron infiltration into the drinking
water just to name a few ways this Administration is making real and positive improvements in
rural America. Work completed as a part of this portfolio benefit residents and communities by
taking proactive actions such as a $1.7 million project in Michigan that will replace undersized
watermains to improve the flow for fire prevention and reduce watermain breaks as well as
meeting the maximum day and peak hour demands for the city. This investment will benefit
1,956 residents, increasing the city’s capability to keep the community safe from wildfires and

With the funding requested in 2027, WEP will continue focusing on smaller rural communities.
With the 2027 funding request of $817 million, RD is expected to support approximately 344
loans that will assist rural residents in obtaining decent, safe, and sanitary water and wastewater
services.
2. A decrease of $15,000,000 in program level for the Water and Waste Direct 1 percent interest
loan program ($15,000,000 available in 2026).
No funding is requested for this program. The budget includes funding for water and waste direct
and guaranteed loans that can continue supporting small rural communities without sufficient
financial resources. In addition, other Federal agencies like the Environmental Protection Agency
can provide funding for rural water infrastructure.
3. A decrease of $242,389,000 in the Water and Waste Disposal grant program ($386,389,000
available in 2026).
Clean, safe, and reliable water and waste services are fundamental building blocks to any
community and critical to the vitality of both residents and businesses alike.
Beyond poverty and aging infrastructure, rural communities face numerous unique obstacles
because systems cover large geographical areas, have reduced economies of scale, and
experience high operator turnover. Delivered in coordination with the RD network of team
members providing hands-on assistance, WEP addresses several of the Administration’s
priorities. Applying a comprehensive approach to developing water and waste infrastructure, WEP
offers a variety of construction and technical assistance programs to ensure that communities
receive the support they need.
Most projects require a loan and grant component to the funding package to enable communities
to provide quality service and reasonable rates. The increased water and waste direct loan
amount will be matched with grant funds to allow for an overall loan to grant ratio approximately
of 70/30 and for the agency to continue focusing on underserved and economically challenged
communities. Set asides under this program provide grant funds for targeted groups to develop
and construct water and waste systems in disadvantaged communities, including programs that
address emergencies, alleviate health risks, and provide vital technical assistance. This funding
enables rural communities to build and maintain decent, safe, and sanitary water and waste
services.
Looking at 2025, RUS has obligated over $385 million in the water and waste disposal regular
grants program with an additional $41.9 million going towards persistent poverty. To highlight
a few of the ongoing work in the grant portfolio, the agency obligated over $35.2 million in
Technical Assistance Grants as well as just over $9.7 million in programs in the portfolios
working to alleviate health risks for Native Americans and the Colonias. The states with the
portfolio’s largest investments are California, Texas, and Arizona.

These grants fund a variety of projects that improve the livelihood and safety of thousands of

wastewater collection systems, in addition to funding water system, wastewater, and storm
water improvements. Furthermore, parts of the system are undersized for the current
population. These efforts will not only help them meet code requirements but provide more
efficient services for their customers.
As the only federal program solely focused on rural water and waste needs, the funding level

Environmental Compliance: Wetlands Conversion
Administrative/Appropriation/Authorizing: Authorizing
Funding/Language/Both: Language
Program: Water and Waste Disposal Program.
Current Legislative Authority to be Amended: Farm Bill
Proposal: This change is needed as many water and waste disposal projects are located near bodies of
water which usually include wetlands. Cost of projects are driven up due to having to design projects
around wetlands rather than the agency being able to mediate the wetlands as available under the
National Environmental Protection Act.
Rationale: Expands exemptions from the prohibition regarding wetlands for water and waste disposal
projects covered under sections 306, 306A, 306C and 306D. This change is needed as many water
and waste disposal projects are located near bodies of water which usually include wetlands. The cost
of projects is driven up due to having to design projects around wetlands rather than the agency
being able to mediate the wetlands as available under the National Environmental Protection Act.
Projects would still maintain compliance with all established statutes and Executive Orders while
providing greater flexibility with design alternatives. This is a Farm Bill proposal. Additionally, this will
update the verbiage to the correct citation for “wetlands” in the U.S.C.
Goal: This is a cost neutral proposal that will provide greater flexibility with design alternatives for
those water and waste disposal projects in the wetlands.

Environmental Compliance: National Historic Preservation Act “eligible for inclusion”
Administrative/Appropriation/Authorizing: Authorizing
Funding/Language/Both: Language
Program: Water and Waste Disposal Program.
Current Legislative Authority to be Amended: National Historic Preservation Act
Proposal: Current implementation of these sections put an undue burden on Federal Agencies to
undergo research, field survey and often dissertation level analysis of area of potential effects to
determine the potential presence of a property that may meet the criteria. This level of effort is not
commensurate with the potential risk posed on impacting these yet to be discovered properties.
Rationale: As in other environmental regulatory processes the responsibility should be put on the
regulator to identify the protected resource. If these resources are so precious as to be protected and
curated, then why have they not been identified, evaluated and initiated the process to be placed on
the Register by the Tribes, State Historic Preservation Offices, local preservation groups, stakeholders
and other interested parties.
Goal: This is a cost neutral proposal that will balance the cost and risk levels associated with
implementing the associated regulatory processes.

Decentralized Water System Grant Thresholds
Administrative/Appropriation/Authorizing: Authorizing
Funding/Language/Both: Language
Program: Water and Waste Disposal Program.
Current Legislative Authority to be Amended: Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act, as
amended.
Proposal: To provide language modifications for 7 USC 1926(e) by creating separate and revised
income standards for revolving loan funds and sub-grants.
Rationale: Prior to the 2018 Farm Bill, the Decentralized Water System (DWS) Grant program was
previously called the Household Water Well Systems (HWWS) Grant program. The HWWS program
allowed for grants to non-profits that could then establish revolving loan funds to support household
water well systems. Eligible households could have an income of up to 100 percent of the state nonmetro median household income (SNMHI). During the 2018 Farm Bill, the program name was
changed and expanded to allow for decentralized wastewater systems and to allow nonprofits to
establish revolving loan funds and to issue sub-grants. The expanded programmatic authority was
countered through a reduction of the household income requirement down to 60 percent of the
SNMHI. This is a significant decrease that causes challenges for the nonprofit organizations to provide
assistance to the individual households.
Goal: This is a cost neutral proposal that will address the concerns of stakeholders in implementing
the program and remove barriers for the rural community to access this program.
Legislative Update: Modify section 7 USC 1926e(a) as follows:
[In] For the purposes of loans made with grant funds under this section, the term “eligible individual”
means an individual who is a member of a household the members of which have a combined income
(for the most recent 12-month period for which the information is available) that is not more than 120
percent of the median nonmetropolitan household income for the State or territory in which the
individual resides, according to the most recent decennial census of the United States. For the
purposes of subgrants made with grant funds under this section, the term “eligible individual” means
an individual who is a member of a household the members of which have a combined income (for
the most recent 12-month period for which the information is available) that is not more than 80
percent of the median nonmetropolitan household income for the State or territory in which the
individual resides, according to the most recent decennial census of the United States.

Administrative/Appropriation/Authorizing: Authorizing
Funding/Language/Both: Language
Program: Water and Waste Disposal Program.
Current Legislative Authority to be Amended: Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act, as

Proposal: The requirements set forth within 7 USC 1926c mandate that funding only be provided to
support areas that have a per capita income of not more than 70 percent of the national average, as
directed by the Department of Commerce. Also, the area must have an unemployment rate of not
less than 125 percent of the national average, as dictated by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
Rationale: Expands the exemption within Section 306(a)(2)(B) to include at least Tribal customers.

requirements set forth within 7 USC 1926c mandate that funding only be provided to support areas
that have a per capita income of not more than 70 percent of the national average, as directed by the
Department of Commerce. Also, the area must have an unemployment rate of not less than 125
percent of the national average, as dictated by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Currently Section 306
C exempts Colonias for this requirement. RD is requesting to include tribe borrowers to be exempted
from this requirement.
Goal: This is a cost neutral proposal that will expand exemption requirements from including only
Colonias to adding Native American Tribes to expand access for the program.

financing for increased energy production and storage.

JUSTIFICATION OF CHANGES
Rural Utilities Service (RUS) programs support a healthy community and environment with loans and
grants to make sure people, kids, and families have clean water and safe sewer systems that prevent
pollution and runoff. RUS programs also keep the people of rural America connected with reliable,
high-speed internet that brings new and innovative ideas to the rest of our country.
RUS provides much-needed infrastructure or infrastructure improvements to rural communities.
These include water and waste treatment, electric power, and telecommunications services. All these
services help to expand economic opportunities and improve the quality of life for rural residents.
The numbers and letters of the following listing relates to values in the Change (Chg) Key column of
the Project Statement:
1. Increase of $200,000,000 for the Federal Financing Bank (FFB) Electric Loans
($2,667,000,000 available in 2026).
Eligible borrowers will have access to direct financing for rural electrification at a lower interest
rate, the proposal will not require budget authority. The request continues promoting
bifurcation of the funding between FFB and Direct Treasury rate loans. In alignment with the
Direct Electric Treasury Rate Loan program, the FFBs program also will contribute to advancing
EO 14541: Unleashing American Energy.
Providing reliable, affordable electricity is essential to the economic well-being and quality of
life for the Nation's rural residents. The electric programs provide leadership and capital to
upgrade, expand, maintain, or replace America's vast rural electric infrastructure.
In 2025, the agency obligated over $2.7 billion across 23 loans with $217.4 million aligned in
persistent poverty funding. In Missouri, the agency made major utility investments
obligating more than $0.7 billion to multiple cooperatives in the state with loans ranging
from just over $500 million down to $28 million. In alignment with the Administration’s
determination that a national emergency exists for the Nation’s electric system, the agency
awarded a loan to Ralls County Electric Cooperative (RCEC) to help them expand and
improve service across their territory, which serves part of five counties in northeast
Missouri, including connecting new customers via new distribution lines, upgrading existing
lines, and deploying advanced smart grid equipment and fiber infrastructure. The project in
Missouri is part of the national effort to improve grid resilience and update rural electric
infrastructure. Enhancing the reliability of service can both encourage new growth and
attract new businesses to those rural communities, bringing additional economic benefits.
The Electric Program continues its effort to improve customer service and experience by
streamlining procedures and regulations that impose burdensome requirements on
borrowers and applicants. A funding request of $2.7 billion in 2027 will support 19 loans
that invest in the infrastructure needs of America’s farmers, ranchers, and producers.
2. Increase of $200,000,000 for program level for the Direct Electric Treasury Rate Loan program
($4,333,000,000 available in 2026).
This program provides electric financing loans at the government’s cost of money (or the
current Treasury rate) under section 4 of the Rural Electrification Act (RE Act) as amended in
the Budget to reduce borrower’s interest costs while maintaining a sustainable program level.
These loans support improvements and additions to rural electric distribution, transmission, and
generating facilities, and the implementation demand side management to service rural areas.
The long-term, low-interest rate financing helps non-profit electric borrowers provide reliable,
affordable electric service for rural homes and businesses, while creating good paying jobs. This
program is one of the ways the Administration is continuing to invest resources supporting the
success of rural America and its hardworking people.

In 2025, the agency obligated over $5.4 billion across 73 loans. From this total, $1.3 billion was
aligned to persistent poverty. The RUS has made over $1.8 billion in loans across five states,
bolstering the electric grid in Georgia, Texas, Florida, Michigan, and North Carolina. But this
funding can be used for more than enhancing smart grid technologies. Cooperatives can serve
more than just one state, so the investment the agency made in North Dakota will be used to
finance transmission system improvements, headquarters facility, improvements for the
replacement of a UPS battery system, and low impact internal network security monitoring. The
recipient of the funding is an electric generation and transmission cooperative who serves
member electric service needs across nine states including North Dakota, South Dakota,
Montana, Wyoming, New Mexico, Colorado, Nebraska, Minnesota and Iowa- serving a total of
140 members.
Funding from this program support’s the Administration’s priorities as identified in Executive
Order 14154: Unleashing American Energy. The funding request of $4.5 million will support
84 loans that will benefit approximately 182,000 customers.
3. A decrease of $910,000,000 in program level for Guaranteed Electric Underwriting for Bonds
and Notes ($910,000,000 available in 2026).
Funding for this program is not being requested in 2027. The reduction in funding requested
for the guaranteed underwriting program can be offset by continued access by most of the
lenders’ eligible borrowers to direct financing for rural electrification and telecommunications
under the Direct Treasury Rate Electric Loans, Electric FFB loans, and Telecommunications
Direct Treasury Loans.
4. A decrease of $350,000,000 in program level for the Telecommunications Direct Treasury
Loan program ($350,000,000 available in 2026).
The Budget is not requesting funding for this program. The budget is requesting funding in
the Direct Telecommunications Direct FFB program that can serve the borrowers of the
regular Telecommunications Direct Treasury Loan program.
5. No change in program level for the Telecommunications Direct FFB program ($200,000,000
available in 2026).
Direct FFB loans are authorized by section 306 of the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 (7 U.S.C.
936) and are provided to borrowers of a non-government lender or from the Federal Financing
Bank (FFB) to furnish and improve telecommunications services, including a wide array of
telecommunication related services, in rural areas. The interest rate charged on FFB loans is the
Treasury rate plus an administrative fee of one-eighth of 1 percent. The terms of these loans may
vary significantly and allow borrowers more flexibility in meeting their financing needs.
The budget requests funding for this program to support the same borrowers that will
be requesting funding from the Telecommunication Direct Treasury rate.
The agency did not receive this funding in 2024; therefore, there is no historical execution data
associated with this program in 2024 or in 2025. With the funding requested, RD would be able
to provide 35 loans.

6. A decrease of $25,000,000 in program level for the Rural Energy Savings program ($25,000,000
available in 2026).
The Budget is not requesting funding for this program. RESP projects, if aligned with the current
Administration’s priorities, can compete for funding through the regular FFB program since both
programs served similar constituencies; therefore, no funding is requested for RESP. The budget
is not requesting this program in 2027.
7. A decrease of $3,000,000 in budget authority for the Energy Circuit Rider Grants ($3,000,000 in
available 2026).
The Budget is not requesting funding for this program.
8. A decrease of $10,770,000 in budget authority for the Rural Telecommunications program and
Rural Energy Savings program ($10,770,000 available in 2026).
These programs are not requested in the budget.

APPROPRIATIONS LANGUAGE
The appropriations language follows:
Distance Learning, Telemedicine, and Broadband Program
For grants for telemedicine and distance learning services in rural areas, as authorized by 7 U.S.C.
950aaa et seq., [$40,767,000]$30,000,000, to remain available until expended[, of which
$10,767,000 shall be for the purposes, and in the amounts, specified for this account in the table
titled "Community Project Funding/ Congressionally Directed Spending" in the explanatory statement
described in section 4 (in the matter preceding division A of this consolidated Act)]: Provided, That
$3,000,000 shall be made available for grants authorized by section 379G of the Consolidated Farm
and Rural Development Act: Provided further, That funding provided under this heading for grants
under section 379G of the Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act may only be provided to
entities that meet all of the eligibility criteria for a consortium as established by this section.
[For the cost to continue a broadband loan and grant pilot program established by section 779 of
division A of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018 (Public Law 115–141) under the Rural
Electrification Act of 1936, as amended (7 U.S.C. 901 et seq.), $50,750,000, to remain available until
expended, of which $750,000 shall be for the purposes, and in the amounts, specified for this account
in the table titled "Community Project Funding/Congressionally Directed Spending" in the explanatory
statement described in section 4 (in the matter preceding division A of this consolidated Act):
Provided, That the Secretary may award grants described in section 601(a) of the Rural Electrification
Act of 1936, as amended (7 U.S.C. 950bb(a)) for the purposes of carrying out such pilot program:
Provided further, That the cost of direct loans shall be defined in section 502 of the Congressional
Budget Act of 1974: Provided further, That at least 90 percent of the households to be served by a
project receiving a loan or grant under the pilot program shall be in a rural area without sufficient
access to broadband: Provided further, That for purposes of such pilot program, a rural area without
sufficient access to broadband shall be defined as twenty-five megabits per second downstream and
three megabits per second upstream: Provided further, That to the extent possible, projects receiving
funds provided under the pilot program must build out service to at least one hundred megabits per
second downstream, and twenty megabits per second upstream: Provided further, That an entity to
which a loan or grant is made under the pilot program shall not use the loan or grant to overbuild or
duplicate broadband service in a service area by any entity that has received a broadband loan from
the Rural Utilities Service unless such service is not provided sufficient access to broadband at the
minimum service threshold: Provided further, That not more than four percent of the funds made
available in this paragraph can be used for administrative costs to carry out the pilot program and up
to three percent of funds made available in this paragraph may be available for technical assistance
and pre-development planning activities to support the most rural communities: Provided further,
That the Rural Utilities Service is directed to expedite program delivery methods that would implement
this paragraph: Provided further, That for purposes of this paragraph, the Secretary shall adhere to
the notice, reporting and service area assessment requirements set forth in section 701 of the Rural
Electrification Act (7 U.S.C. 950cc).]

JUSTIFICATION OF CHANGES
Rural Utilities Service (RUS) programs support a healthy community and environment with loans and
grants to make sure people, kids, and families have clean water and safe sewer systems that prevent
pollution and runoff. RUS programs also keep the people of rural America connected with reliable,
high-speed internet that brings new and innovative ideas to the rest of our country.
RUS provides much-needed infrastructure or infrastructure improvements to rural communities.
These include water and waste treatment, electric power, and telecommunications services. All these
services help to expand economic opportunities and improve the quality of life for rural residents.
The numbers and letters of the following listing relate to values in the Change (Chg) Key column of
the Project Statement:
1. No change for Distance Learning and Telemedicine (DLT) Grants ($30,000,000 available in
2026).
The DLT Program supports rural schools and learning centers gaining access to improved
educational resources. Distance learning projects provide funding for Internet-based educational
services in schools and libraries and promote confidence in, and understanding of, the Internet
and its benefits to students and young entrepreneurs. Grants through this program are also
being used to address the nation’s need for additional science, technology, engineering,
and mathematics (STEM) educational resources. This program is designed specifically to
meet the educational and health care needs of rural America by providing grants for advanced
telecommunications technologies that provide enhanced learning and health care opportunities
for rural residents.
Telemedicine can help hospitals meet operational and financial challenges by helping to increase
volume, improve quality, and save on staffing costs. Even small rural hospitals can expand to
provide highly specialized health services and expertise without the tremendous costs of
incentivizing and relocating specialty physicians.
Millions of Americans in rural areas have limited access to healthcare and educational services and
very few private sector providers would likely consider working in the rural sector. For many small
communities that are currently underserved, lower-income and extremely rural, RUS represents
the primary financial assistance available. If the program was not funded, the ability of rural
Americans to access critical telemedicine and distance learning services would diminish.
In 2025, the program obligated $52.4 million through 81 grants. The agency awarded 4 grants
totaling $1.6 million for programs addressing the Opioid Epidemic and 14 grants totaling $10.3
million for Substance Use Disorders. Kentucky, Georgia, and Tennessee received over 30 percent
of funding. One of these investments, awarded over $800,000 in Pennsylvania to equip one hub
site and 35 end-user sites across multiple counties with distance learning equipment to include
laptops and Webex-equipped conference rooms. This project will benefit 11,638 rural residents.
Funding for DLT grants helps rural communities use the unique capabilities of telecommunications
to connect to each other and to the world, overcoming the effects of remoteness, low population
density, and persistent poverty. The agency sets aside funding for projects that seek to reduce the
morbidity and mortality associated with substance use disorder, including opioid misuse, in rural
communities by strengthening the capacity to address prevention, treatment and/or recovery at
the community level.
The agency published a document in the Federal Register (90 FR 13580) on March 25, 2025, to
adjust a multitude of RD funding notices. This notice corrected the prior funding announcement to
align it with the current Administration's priorities and the Executive Order: Ending Radical and
Wasteful Government DEI Programs and Preferencing.

This program provides grant funds for developing health care services, health care cooperatives,

STATUS OF PROGRAM
The Rural Utilities Service (RUS) provides much-needed infrastructure or infrastructure improvements
to rural communities, including water and waste treatment, electric power, and telecommunications
services. These services help to expand economic opportunities and improve the quality of life for rural
residents.
Water and Environmental Programs
The Water and Environmental Programs (WEP) provides rural communities with construction and
technical assistance funding to develop and support modern, affordable water and waste disposal
(WWD) services. WEP provides direct financing for clean and reliable drinking water systems, sanitary
sewage disposal, sanitary solid waste disposal, and storm water drainage to serve households and
businesses in eligible rural areas with populations of 10,000 or less. Guaranteed construction financing
is available to support communities of up to 50,000 or less in population. With a focus on underserved
communities, WEP directs its technical and construction financing resources to rural communities with
the greatest need, including rural water and waste systems in areas that are poverty-stricken and
face health or sanitary issues, or have other obstacles hindering their ability to provide safe and
reliable services. These communities may also qualify based on stringent regulatory compliance
responsibilities, local changes in economic factors, or even natural disasters; with support, they can
sustain economic development and improve the quality of life for their residents by providing
dependable water and waste disposal services. This program prioritizes subsidies for communities
using a three-tier approach with product offerings at poverty, intermediary and market rates.
WEP is the only federal Agency solely dedicated to supporting rural water and waste disposal systems.
In the 2026 President’s Budget it was identified as a program that has “demonstrated efficient results”
while playing “an appropriate Federal role.” In 2025, WEP obligated over $900 million in direct and
guaranteed loans and $624 million in grants, including both Community Project
Funding/Congressionally Directed Spending (CDS) and Disaster Assistance, but excluding High Energy
Cost Grants. The program obligated current year and carryover funds resulting in a total larger than
the amount of new funding received in 2025. From these totals, 22.3 percent of obligations occurred
in counties with persistent poverty for 105 customers across 76 projects. Also, the obligated funding
benefits more than 1.65 million rural residents, with 61 percent of projects addressing a health or
sanitary issue.
Current Activities
In 2025, WEP funded 233 direct loans with 121 at the poverty rate (52 percent), 95 at the
intermediate rate (41 percent), and 17 at the market rate (7 percent). There were 3 direct loans
obligated through the 1 percent Persistent Poverty Direct Loan program.

Selected Examples of Recent Progress
Safe drinking water and sanitary waste disposal systems are vital not only to public health, but also to
the economic vitality of rural America. WEP works with communities to replace and repair damaged
water systems to ensure safe water is available for rural residents. In Tennessee, for example, the
Copper Basin Utility District received more than $1.25 million in funding to construct a booster pump
station, add approximately 4,000 linear feet of 6-inch water line to form a loop in the eastern end of
the system, and a tie-in to the proposed tank to the system. This project will mainly serve the
residents of Ducktown and Turtletown through the Isabella Water System, which is run by the Copper
Basin Utility District. The proposed water system improvements will strengthen the continuity of
service and improve pressure and flow. The resulting improvements will also eliminate any potential
for health concerns from low pressure and flow at the customer's meter. In Montgomery County,
Tennessee, funds will be used to extend water lines to serve 70 households that currently rely on
private wells as their water source. The project is a response by Cunningham Utility District to provide
safe and reliable drinking water within their service area within the county. However, the largest
investment went to expand and upgrade the 40-year-old Water Treatment Plant within the Town of
Jonesborough's Water System. Receiving more than $40.4 million in funding, upgrades and
expansions are necessary due to population growth within the area and extension of water lines into
new housing subdivisions that are planned within the area. This project will increase the water
system's capacity to better serve the residents of Jonesborough.

Focusing on waste disposal, in Iowa, an $8 million loan will be used to help the City of Emmetsburg
renovate its wastewater treatment facility which was built in the 1970s. This project will replace the
rotating biological contactor treatment with an activated sludge treatment. The new treatment
method will be resilient to slug loading and peak weather events. It will provide ease of operations and
ensure regulatory compliance to the benefit of the Palo Alto community. The project will also replace
other aging and obsolete infrastructure. In Alabama, a $725,000 loan will provide financing to
rehabilitate the collection system and construct a new centralized wastewater treatment plant for the
Town of Kinsey. The proposed plant will be capable of managing an average daily flow of
approximately 360,000 gallons; with a peak capacity of 680,000 gallons. Improvements to reduce
inflow and infiltration in the gravity sanitary sewer collection system will also be completed. This
system will serve a population of more than 1,200 people and eliminate health and safety issues in
the Town.
The City of Grygla, MN was awarded RUS funding through a low-interest loan of $3,274,000 loan and
$308,000 grant for a water and sewer improvements project. The proposed funding will be used to
construct a new water treatment plant, and rehabilitate water storage, wastewater lift station and
sanitary sewer ponds. The project will also replace portions of the water distribution system and
sanitary sewer collection system. There has been no major construction completed on the lift station
since its original construction, and there has been noticeable deterioration. This project will provide
assistance to the 221 residents of Grygla in northern Minnesota.
In west-central Michigan, the Village of Sheridan is upgrading their water system to address several
overdue issues. WEP awarded the Village $5,018,000 direct loan and $118,000 grant funding to
replace infrastructure that was originally constructed in the 1950’s to serve residential, commercial,
and industrial users. A significant amount of the infrastructure and mechanical equipment in Sheridan
is over 70 years old. Project funding will be used to replace undersized water mains, loop dead ends,
install residential water meters, replace commercial water meters, replace lead service lines, and
connect 10 new users. The program will ensure the everyday needs of the community and that their
water system is compliance with safety standards.
Elsewhere, there are several other health and sanitary issues rural Americans face with their
infrastructure. In Arkansas, the Town of Holly Grove will soon start construction on a project that will
provide several upgrades to an elevated storage tank, many of which will bring them into compliance
from issues caused by lead paint and leaks. The project will also replace water mains and meters.
With a service area of just over 1,600, the RUS assistance of $3,059,000 loan and grant of
$1,670,000 will resolve its OSHA and Arkansas Department of Health violations.
Water and Environmental Grant Programs
There are many grants products and set asides appropriated in the WEP grants portfolio, which
include but are not limited to: regular Water and Waste Disposal Grants, Emergency Community
Water Assistance Grants, Special Evaluation Assistance for Rural Communities and Households, Rural
Decentralized Water Systems Grant Program, Solid Waste Management, Disaster Assistance,
Congressionally Directed Spending, as well as grants for Native American Tribes, Rural Alaskan
Villages, and Hawaiian Homeland. Examples from just a few of the many projects are included below.
Through the Emergency Community Water Assistance Grants (ECWAG) program, communities can
receive up to $1 million in assistance to support water intake and treatment needs and up to
$150,000 for storage and distribution issues stemming from emergency events. Emergency and
disaster events often leave communities the most vulnerable, so this is their biggest time of need. In
Illinois, there are two ECWAG projects totaling $137,000. One of these investments will be used to
support the Jersey County Rural Water Company’s emergency watermain repairs following flash
flooding in July 2024, which destroyed waterlines and exposed buried main. Water service for more
than 750 users throughout Jersey County was compromised. Timely repairs to replace 200 linear feet
of 4-inch watermain and appurtenances were needed to maintain the system and provide clean
drinking water to residents. A second project will fund the repair of exposed water mains for Atkin
Water District. The District experienced severe weather and heavy rainfall during the spring of 2025,

which resulted in the exposure of over 70 feet of water main. If further flooding occurs, it could
damage the exposed additional infrastructure, jeopardizing water quality for the community and leave
residents without access to clean water. The project includes reburying the existing water main four
feet deeper than its current location and replacing approximately 180 linear feet of water main and
appurtenances. This project will serve more than 460 people in Franklin County, Illinois.
The agency recently announced Special Evaluation Assistance for Rural Communities and Households
(SEARCH) Grant projects across nine states with a median value of $30,000. While small grants, the
biggest barrier for many rural communities is often finding funding to complete technical documents,
like engineer reports, and finding assistance with application development. In Utah, a $30,000
SEARCH grant will be used to assist the small, census-designated community of La Sal to secure
professional services to develop engineering and environmental documents needed to construct a
reliable and adequate municipal water system. La Sal is a remote community within San Juan County
and has no access to municipal water. With 154 existing residential connections, and many lots for
new construction, La Sal does not have enough individual well permits to provide adequate water for
all properties. One of the smaller grants, a $11,000 investment in Oklahoma will be used to complete
an engineering report for improvements to their water system. This report will evaluate the best
option available to Rural Water District #17 and their customers to reduce water loss, increase
production and clear up water quality issues. Removing these health and safety items will provide safe
drinking water for their residential and wholesale customers.
Here is an example of a RUS WEP 306C-NATIVE AMERICAN-WASTE ONLY grant. In 2025, RD
awarded approximately $4.7 million in grant funds in New Mexico that will be used to extend the
existing sanitary sewer systems of the Villages of Seama and Paguate and replace the existing sewer
lines in the Villages of Laguna, Old Laguna and Mesita Village. The Pueblo of Laguna has been
experiencing significant issues with their wastewater and sewer system. Aging wastewater facilities at
the Pueblo of Laguna present severe health, sanitation, and security risks to community members.
This improvement project will address the deficiencies in the sanitary sewer systems for the Villages of
Laguna, Old Laguna, Mesita, Paguate, and Seama and provide the Pueblo with necessary upgrades to
accommodate growth.
WEP’s Rural Decentralized Water Systems program awards grants to both nation-wide and local
nonprofit organizations that then provide loans and sub-grants to individual homeowners. On the
larger scale, a $2,000,000 grant is enabling the Water Well Trust Inc. to increase potable water
availability and safe wastewater systems to low-income households in eligible high-need rural counties
across America.
More local in scale, a grant of just over $108,000 will enable The Foundation for Healthy Communities
of Northeastern Utah to establish a revolving loan fund for eligible individual homeowners primarily in
three counties (Daggett, Duchesne, and Uintah), to address failed wastewater systems. One of the
most searched programs on the Agency’s website, DWS grants are one of the few programs that
support decentralized solutions in rural America. Due to geography or other barriers, it is not always
possible to tie into a centralized system. This program builds upon the US Environmental Protection
Agency and USDA initiative titled, Closing America’s Wastewater Access Gap, which has shown
significant success.

Direct and Guaranteed Loans
Current Activities
The rural electric direct loan and loan guarantee programs provide financing for electric generation,
transmission, and distribution facilities, and for energy efficiency and conservation projects to improve
service in rural areas. Loans are made to corporations, States, territories, local government
subdivisions, municipalities, utility districts, and to other eligible entities, including cooperatives, tribal
utilities, nonprofits, and limited-dividend or mutual associations that provide retail electric service
needs to rural areas or power supply needs of distribution borrowers in rural areas.
USDA’s Electric Loan Program helps finance new generation from all sources, including natural gas,
improvements to coal facilities, as well as wind and solar. Local utilities also use the loans to invest in
infrastructure to deliver affordable power to millions of residential, commercial, and agricultural
consumers.
Selected Examples of Recent Progress
RD invested $9.1 billion in loans to 107 projects in 30 through the Electric Infrastructure Loan and
Loan Guarantee program to help rural utility providers and electric cooperatives build and improve
electric infrastructure, including smart-grid technologies. Smart grid technology can be a catalyst for
broadband and other telecommunication services in underserved and rural areas. Smart gird
technologies help companies and corporations modernize and expand the rural electric grid and
increase grid security. Strengthening Americas electric grid is vital to the long-term health of rural
America. The projects will ensure more than two million people in rural areas have access to reliable
electricity, increasing their opportunities for economic growth and enhancing their overall quality of
life. Below are examples of how this funding is being utilized:

The Rural Energy Savings Program (RESP)
RESP provides loans to entities that agree to make affordable loans to help consumers implement
cost-effective, energy efficiency measures. RESP will help lower energy bills for rural families and
businesses and will reduce barriers to investment in energy efficiency projects or activities. In 2025,
the agency made one award in New York for $10 million that will finance a relending program where
the Borrower provides energy efficiency loans to qualified consumers to implement durable costeffective energy efficiency measures.
Inflation Reduction Act Programs
Current Activities
In 2022, the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) provided $10.7 billion in loan and grant budget authority to
support the implementation of two new renewables in the Electric program: the Empowering Rural
America (New ERA) Program and the Powering Affordable Clean Energy (PACE) program. In 2025 the
agency obligated $890.9 million through 47 loans for the PACE program and just over $5.6 billion
through 98 loans and grants for New ERA program. Both programs experienced an increase in
obligations. The PACE program saw over $280 million increase in obligations while the New ERA
program experienced a more than $12 billion increase in obligations in 2025. In 2026 and 2027, New
Era funds will be used in round 2 of investing in projects that increase energy capacity. Such
investments include, but are not limited to, reconductoring and new generation capacity.

High Energy Cost Grants
RUS-financed electrical systems provide service to more than 90 percent of the Nation’s counties that
are identified as suffering from persistent poverty, out-migration, or other economic hardships. The
Electric Program also provides financial assistance through High Energy Cost Grants (HECG) to rural
communities with extremely high energy costs to acquire, construct, extend, upgrade, and otherwise
improve energy generation, transmission, or distribution facilities. In 2025, HECG obligated just over
$20.6 million supporting ten grants in Alaska.
These grants will be used for a variety of projects, including a $3 million grant to fund the replacement
of the failing electric distribution system with construction which meets USDA Rural Utilities Service
and National Electric Safety Code construction standards in the Village of Kipnuk, Alaska. In another
project, a $800,000 grant will be used to fund part of the costs to complete the conduit distribution
line from the diesel generating station to the airport. The existing conduit was built in 1988.

IT Development, Enhancement, and Modernization
In addition to facilitating loan and grant programs, RUS Telecom Program staff have
continued to lead the IT development and automation efforts to streamline and modernize
operations across the grant/loan lifecycle. Beginning with the introduction of the ReConnect
Program in 2018, the Telecom Program developed the RUS Program Services (RPS)
Platform. The new system created a single, scalable, and easily maintainable platform for
application Intake. Since 2018, the Program has worked to develop, modernize, and
enhance the system, improving upon its configurability, flexibility, scalability, security, and
efficiency. Today, RUS has developed five modules within the RPS system for each phase of
the loan and grant lifecycle: Application Intake, Mapping and Public Notify, Review and
Award, Advancing Award Funds, and Reporting and Compliance.
All Telecommunications programs leverage the RPS platform to streamline their operations
across the grant/loan lifecycle. Additionally, several Electric Programs are currently in the
process of moving to RPS systems. Through upgrades to the IT system, RD has successfully
retired or is on the way to retiring seven different legacy systems that handled various parts
of the loan/grant lifecycle for various programs. The IT platform includes robust security
features, ensuring that sensitive applicant data is protected in compliance with federal
regulations and industry best practices. The efficiency of the system reduces administrative
burdens and accelerates the processing of applications, leading to faster disbursement of
funds and improved service delivery.

Telecommunications Infrastructure
Current Activities
The Telecommunications Infrastructure Loan and Loan Guarantees Program provides financing for the
construction, maintenance, improvement and expansion of telephone service and broadband in rural
areas. Funding is used to finance broadband capable telecommunications service through
improvements, expansions, construction and in some cases acquisitions and refinancing. Funding is
available through Cost-of-Money Loans, Loan Guarantees of up to 80 percent and Hardship Loans. By
helping private and non-profit providers secure the necessary financing, this program helps maintain a
seamless nationwide telecommunications network that also provides access to broadband for
education, healthcare, public safety and jobs for all Americans regardless of where they live.

Distance Learning and Telemedicine (DLT) Program
Current Activities
The DLT program helps rural communities connect to each other and to the world, overcoming the
effects of remoteness and low population density. The DLT Program assists rural schools and learning
centers to gain access to improved educational resources. Distance learning projects provide funding
for Internet-based educational services in schools and libraries and promote confidence in and
understanding of the Internet and its benefits to students and young entrepreneurs. Grants through
this program are also being used to address the nation’s need for additional science, technology,
engineering, and mathematics (STEM) educational resources.
