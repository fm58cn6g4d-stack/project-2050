Appendix. Additional Detail on USDA Appropriations
Summary
The Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies Appropriations Act (Agriculture appropriations) funds the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA)—except for the U.S. Forest Service—and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA, in the Department of Health and Human Services). It also carries the funding for the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) in alternating fiscal years; jurisdiction for CFTC appropriations is with Agriculture appropriations in the House and a different subcommittee in the Senate.
Agriculture appropriations acts include both discretionary and mandatory funding, which differentiates Agriculture appropriations from some other appropriations acts. Congressional debate on appropriations is primarily about discretionary spending. Appropriations for mandatory spending programs, although not usually debated, are necessary to technically make funds available for those programs (e.g., entitlement programs, such as farm safety net and nutrition assistance programs, that were previously enacted under separate budget enforcement procedures).
The primary discretionary spending accounts are for the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC); agricultural research; rural development loans and grants; the FDA; foreign food assistance and trade promotion; farm agency salaries and loans; food safety inspection; animal and plant health programs; and conservation program technical assistance.
Below is an overview of the legislative actions and amounts in FY2027 Agriculture appropriations compared with prior years.
The Trump Administration released its budget request on April 3, 2026. The Administration proposed $22.055 billion for discretionary appropriations in the jurisdiction of Agriculture appropriations, a reduction of $4.923 billion from FY2026 (-18.2%). The largest reductions would eliminate appropriations for Foreign Agricultural Service Food for Peace (FFP) Title II international food aid grants (-$1.2 billion, -100.0%), the Rural Housing Service (RHS) Community Facilities Program (-$664 million, -100%), the McGovern-Dole Food for Education Program (-$240 million, -100.0%), formula funding for university agricultural research under the Hatch Act (-$265 million, -100.0%), and the Rural Business-Cooperative Service (RBCS, -$81 million, -100.0%) and would reduce Conservation Operations in the Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS, -$739 million, -87%), Food and Nutrition Administration Commodity Assistance Programs (-$460 million, -83.5%), Rural Utilities Service (RUS) Rural Water and Waste Disposal Program (-$243 million, -54.6%), and WIC (-$200 million, -2.4%). Accounts proposed for increases include the RHS Rental Assistance Program (+$80 million (+4.7%) and several USDA administrative accounts (+$55 million, +12.5%).
The House Appropriations Subcommittee on Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies (House Appropriations Agriculture Subcommittee) voted to send a markup to the full committee on April 23, 2026. The House Committee on Appropriations reported a bill ( H.R. 8646 , H.Rept. 119-632 ) on April 29. The House passed the bill on June 4. The bill would provide $26.265 billion in discretionary spending, a decrease of about $713 million from FY2026 (-2.6%) on a comparable basis, including the CFTC in FY2026 levels and $4.209 billion more than the Trump Administration's request. The largest reduction would be from FFP (-$300 million, -25.0%), followed by RHS Community Facilities Grants (-$254 million, -38.3%), WIC (-$200 million, -2.4%), RUS Rural Water and Waste Disposal Program (-$59 million, -13.1%), and NRCS (-$63 million, -7.0%). Accounts proposed for increases include the RHS Rental Assistance Program (+$80 million, +4.7%), RBCS Guaranteed Business and Industry Loans (+$23 million; +148%), Food Safety and Inspection Service (+$11 million, +0.9%), and several USDA administrative accounts.
The Senate Appropriations Committee has not marked up a bill for FY2027 Agriculture appropriations. A continuing resolution ( P.L. 119-103 ) was enacted on September 2, 2026, that funds government operations at FY2026 levels until December 11, 2026.
Description of Agriculture Appropriations
The Agriculture Appropriations Act—formally known as the Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies Appropriations Act (referred to as Agriculture appropriations in this report)—funds the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), except for the U.S. Forest Service. 1 It also funds the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the Department of Health and Human Services and, in certain cases based on committee jurisdiction, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC, an independent agency). 2
Congressional consideration of appropriations bills primarily concerns discretionary spending. However, Agriculture appropriations include both discretionary and mandatory funding, which differentiates them from some other appropriations acts. For example, while the level of mandatory spending for farm commodity and nutrition assistance programs is set in the farm bill, appropriations are technically required for funding to be available. 3 Specifically, the Commodity Credit Corporation—a line of credit with the U.S. Treasury—needs a reimbursement in appropriations to continue operating. 4 The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) needs an annual appropriation to fulfill its authorization. 5 This report focuses mostly on discretionary appropriations but includes tables for the mandatory appropriations included in Agriculture appropriations.
Mandatory and Discretionary Spending
Federal spending is divided into two main categories: discretionary and mandatory (CRS In Focus IF13124, Distinguishing Between Discretionary and Mandatory Spending ). Congressional jurisdiction for discretionary spending rests primarily with the House and Senate A ppropriations C ommittees , including the House and Senate Appropriations Subcommittees on Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies. Congressional jurisdiction for mandatory spending rests primarily with the authorizing committees , including the House and Senate Agriculture Committees that write the farm bill (CRS In Focus IF12233, Farm Bill Primer: Budget Dynamics ). Congressional actions on specific programs may overlap these jurisdictions. Authorizing committees set parameters in law for how discretionary programs are to operate (e.g., eligibility criteria for rural development loan and grant programs) and authorize them for funding that is subsequently determined in the appropriations process. Appropriations committees may decide whether to appropriate funding for discretionary programs and may include changes to some program terms, typically for one year at a time, in reported bills. Appropriations committees provide appropriations for some mandatory programs, which technically makes the funds available for those programs (e.g., entitlement programs, such as farm safety net and nutrition assistance programs, enacted by authorizing committees under separate budget enforcement procedures).
Summary of FY2027 Action
The Trump Administration released its full FY2027 budget request on April 3, 2026. 6
On April 23, 2026, the House Appropriations Subcommittee on Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies (House Appropriations Agriculture Subcommittee) voted 10-7 to send a markup to the full committee. On April 29, the House Committee on Appropriations reported the bill ( H.R. 8646 , H.Rept. 119-632 ) by a vote of 35-25. Sixteen amendments were offered: five were adopted, nine were not adopted, and two were withdrawn. 7 On June 4, the bill was passed by the House by a vote of 213-210. Under a structured rule ( H.Res. 1333 ), 26 amendments were made in order, of which 23 were adopted by voice vote in an en bloc amendment.
The Senate Appropriations Committee has not marked up a bill for FY2027 Agriculture appropriations. A continuing resolution ( P.L. 119-103 ) was enacted on September 2, 2026, that funds government operations at FY2026 levels until December 11, 2026.

Key Budget Terms
Budget authority is the amount of funding provided by an act of Congress for an agency to enter into financial obligations on behalf of the United States that will result in immediate or future outlays . It is the amount allocated by Congress for program grants, loan subsidies, and salaries and expenses. Most funding amounts in this report are budget authority.
Obligations are commitments using budget authority that create a legal liability of the government to pay for goods and services ordered or received. Obligations occur when a federal agency awards a grant or agrees to make a loan. The Antideficiency Act (31 U.S.C. §§1341 et seq.) prohibits agencies from obligating more budget authority than is provided in law, such as during a government shutdown.
Outlays are a federal agency distribution of funds for grants, loans, or services to recipients to satisfy a valid obligation. The timing of outlays in fiscal years may differ from budget authority or obligations because payments from an agency may not occur until services are fulfilled, goods are delivered, or construction is completed.
Loan subsidy is the cost to the government of making loans, including interest rate subsidies and anticipated losses from delinquencies. The loan subsidy is part of the discretionary budget authority. The relationship between loan authority and loan subsidy is explained for each loan program in the annual budget request (Office of Management and Budget, Federal Credit Supplement , for each fiscal year).
Loan authority is the amount of loans that can be made or guaranteed with a loan subsidy. Loan authority is not added in budget authority totals. A loan authority is several times the amount of its corresponding loan subsidy.
Program level is the total funding dedicated to a program, as calculated by the agency, including both grants and loan authority. A program level may be higher than the appropriated budget authority if the program (1) receives user fees , (2) includes loan authority (loan authority exceeds the loan subsidy), or (3) receives transfers from other agencies.
Rescissions are permanent reductions in budget authority after enactment that score as budgetary savings.
CHIMPs (changes in mandatory program spending) are adjustments via an appropriations act that can change available funding for mandatory programs. CHIMPs usually change spending for one year and may score as an increase or decrease to outlays. They do not change the underlying authority of the program in law.
For background, see CRS Report 98-721, Introduction to the Federal Budget Process ; and CRS Report R46417, Congress's Power Over Appropriations: Constitutional and Statutory Provisions .

Administration's Request
The Trump Administration FY2027 proposal for discretionary appropriations that is in the jurisdiction of Agriculture appropriations is $22.055 billion, which would be a reduction of $4.923 billion from the FY2026 levels in P.L. 119-37 (-18.2%).
The largest reduction would be from the elimination of Foreign Agricultural Service (FAS) Food for Peace Title II international food aid grants (FFP, -$1.2 billion, -100.0%), followed by reductions of the Conservation Operations account to the Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS, -$739 million, -86.9%), research and extension grants by the National Institute of Food and Agriculture (-$655 million, -32.3%), grants and loans in the RHS (-$648 million, -20.9%), FNA Commodity Assistance Programs (-$460 million, -83.5%), grants and loans in the Rural Utilities Service (RUS, -$330 million, -55.4%; including to the Rural Water and Waste Disposal Program, -$243 million, -54.6%), Farm Service Agency salaries, expenses and programs (-$266 million, -22.7%), the FAS McGovern-Dole Food for Education Program (McGovern-Dole, -$240 million, -100.0%), and WIC (-$200 million, -2.4%).
In percentage terms of reductions, the Administration's request would eliminate appropriations for FFP (-$1.2 billion, -100.0%), formula funding for university agricultural research under the Hatch Act (-$265 million, -100.0%), McGovern-Dole (-$240 million, -100.0%), and the Rural Business-Cooperative Service (RBCS, -$81 million, -100.0%).
Accounts proposed for increases include the RHS Rental Assistance Program (+$80 million (+4.7%), the Agricultural Census in the National Agricultural Statistics Service (+$2.5 million, +5.4%), and several USDA administrative accounts, including Buildings and Facilities (+$55 million, +366%), Office of the Chief Information Officer (+$50 million, +59%), Office of Homeland Security (+$14 million, +798%), and Departmental Administration (+$10 million, +59%). A reduction in the amount of rescissions in the bill compared with FY2026 would increase the net appropriation by $345 million (because of less offsetting reductions).
Subcommittee Allocations
The total amount allowed for discretionary appropriations is controlled by congressional budget enforcement rules. 10 Typically, each chamber's appropriations committee would receive a top-line limit on discretionary budget authority from the chamber's budget committee via an annual budget resolution, referred to as a "302(a)" allocation. The appropriations committees would subdivide the allocation among the 12 appropriations subcommittees, referred to as "302(b)" allocations. 11
For FY2027, the House and Senate Budget Committees did not approve a budget resolution with discretionary spending limits. The appropriations committees may set (deem) spending levels during meetings to mark up separate subcommittee bills. 12
On April 22, 2026, the House Committee on Appropriations ordered reported interim subcommittee allocations for six subcommittees. 13 The allocation for the Agriculture subcommittee is $26.270 billion. The Senate Committee on Appropriations has not set limits for any of its appropriations subcommittees.
House-Passed Bill, H.R. 8646
The House-passed bill, H.R. 8646 , would provide $26.265 billion, a decrease of about $713 million from FY2026 (-2.6%) on a comparable basis including the CFTC in FY2026 levels. The House-passed bill would provide $4.209 billion more than the Trump Administration's request and does not follow most of the Administration's largest proposed reductions.
The largest reduction would be from FFP (-$300 million, -25.0%), followed by WIC (-$200 million, -2.4%), RHS Community Facilities Grants (-$254 million, -38.3%), RUS (-$79 million, -13.3% [including the Rural Water and Waste Disposal Program, -$59 million, -13.1%]), and NRCS (-$63 million, -7.0%).
The largest increases would be for the RHS Rental Assistance Program (+$80 million, +4.7%), RBCS Guaranteed Business and Industry Loans (+$23 million; +148%), Food Safety and Inspection Service (+11 million, +0.9%), and several USDA administrative accounts, including the Office of the Chief Information Officer (+$39 million, +46%) and the Office of Homeland Security (+$14 million, +798%). A reduction in the amount of rescissions in the bill compared with FY2026 would increase the net appropriation by $365 million (because of less offsetting reductions).

Other Appropriations Issues
Policy-Related Provisions
Appropriations acts set spending authority and may serve as vehicles for policy-related limitations and so-called "riders" (language extraneous to the subject of the bill) that direct executive branch actions. 14 These provisions may have the force of law if they are included in an act's text, but their effect is generally limited to the current fiscal year unless they amend the U.S. Code , which is not typical in appropriations acts. Report language may also provide policy directions for agencies but does not carry the force of law.
Congress has directed that committee reports and the joint explanatory statement are to be read together to capture congressional intent.
The joint explanatory statement accompanying this division is approved and indicates congressional intent. Unless otherwise noted, the language set forth in H.Rept. 119–172 and S.Rept. 119–37 carries the same weight as language included in this joint explanatory statement and should be complied with unless specifically addressed to the contrary in this joint explanatory statement. While some language is repeated for emphasis, it is not intended to negate the language referred to above unless expressly provided herein. 15
Congressionally Directed Spending (Earmarks)
Congressional e armarks are defined in the 119 th Congress as a type of congressional spending action that noncompetitively benefits a specific entity or locality. 16 The House Committee on Appropriations refers to these as Community Project Funding, and the Senate Committee on Appropriations refers to these as Congressionally Directed Spending.
Beginning in FY2022 and continuing to FY2024, after about a decade of no earmarks, the House and Senate allowed earmarks in annual appropriations acts, including in Agriculture appropriations. 17 The FY2025 yearlong CR did not include earmarks and reduced funding commensurately for accounts that had been earmarked in FY2024. In FY2026, earmarks resumed; 6 accounts in the enacted appropriation contained 509 earmarks totaling $643 million.
For FY2027, 5 USDA accounts have Community Project Funding in the House-passed bill: ARS Buildings and Facilities; NRCS Conservation Operations; RUS Water and Waste Disposal Program grants; and RUS Distance Learning and Telemedicine Program grants. There were 592 earmark projects totaling $604 million ( Table 5 ). Earmarks were requested by 196 Representatives or Delegates from 45 states, the District of Columbia, and 4 territories. 18
The Community Facilities account comprises 73% of the number of earmarks and 66% of the dollar amount of earmarks in Agriculture appropriations. Nearly all of this account is earmarked (97%), leaving little for USDA to allocate competitively. The Rural Water and Waste Disposal account is the next largest earmark recipient, with 21% of the number of earmarks and 22% of the amount. More of this account's total remains available for competitive allocation; 35% is earmarked. Six infrastructure projects in the Community Facilities, Rural Water and Waste Disposal, and ARS Buildings and Facilities accounts are the largest earmarks, ranging from $5 million to $8.5 million.

Jurisdiction began differing between the chambers after FY2008. The CFTC is always carried in House Appropriations Agriculture Subcommittee markup, never in the Senate Agriculture Subcommittee markup, and always in the Senate Appropriations Financial Services and General Government Subcommittee markup. The CFTC is normally carried in enacted Agriculture Appropriations Acts in alternating fiscal years—in even-numbered fiscal years from FY2008 to FY2024 and in odd-numbered fiscal years beginning in FY2025. The FY2025 year-long continuing resolution (CR) for Agriculture appropriations repeated carrying the CFTC as in FY2024 and caused the alternating cycle to switch.
