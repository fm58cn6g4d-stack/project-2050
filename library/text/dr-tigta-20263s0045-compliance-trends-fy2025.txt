Impact on Tax Administration
This report is a compilation of
statistical information reported by
the IRS. The data presented in this
report provide taxpayers and
stakeholders with information
about how the IRS focuses its
compliance resources and the
impact of those resources on
revenue and compliance over time.

While enforcement revenue comprises only a small portion of the
total tax revenue paid to the IRS, the IRS also achieved a historic high
in enforcement revenue of $98.7 billion in FY 2024. However,
enforcement revenue decreased in FY 2025 to $93.8 billion, mainly
attributable to a 35 percent decline in examination-related revenue
from FY 2024 to FY 2025.
Although the IRS hired a substantial number of employees in
FYs 2023 and 2024, in FY 2025 the federal government began efforts
to reduce the size of government. As a result, the IRS lost
approximately 27 percent of its Examination and Collection staff from
FY 2024 to FY 2025. While the workforce reductions influenced
metrics in FY 2025, the downstream effects of these reductions are
likely to become more apparent over time.
The IRS’s overall annual appropriation remained flat at $12.3 billion
from FY 2023 through FY 2025, with an Enforcement Operations
allocation between approximately $5.2 and $5.4 billion per fiscal year.
The enforcement budget was supplemented in recent years by
$3.8 billion in Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) funding. As of
December 31, 2025, the IRS has exhausted this supplemental
enforcement funding.
Revenue from Collection functions increased by 17 percent from
FY 2023 to FY 2025, which offset declines across all other
enforcement revenue categories. These increases were primarily due
to increases in automated collection notices, which had been
intermittently paused during the pandemic. For example, the IRS sent
approximately 3.2 million notices to individual nonfilers in FY 2025
after sending no notices in FY 2023.
Revenue from the examination functions increased by 41 percent
from FY 2023 to FY 2024, then decreased by 35 percent from FY 2024
to FY 2025. The increase and subsequent decrease in examination
revenue coincided with IRS investments in the Examination function
staffing resources using IRA funding in FY 2024 and the workforce
reductions in FY 2025.
The number of examinations of individual tax returns started
decreased from FY 2024 to FY 2025 by 30 percent, with examinations
of individual taxpayers examined with incomes over $400,000
declining by 27 percent.
This report provides information only, so we made no
recommendations.

Final Report – Trends in Compliance Activities Through Fiscal Year 2025
(Review No.: 20253S0001)

This report presents the results of our review to provide various statistical information regarding
Collection and Examination function activities as they relate to the Internal Revenue Service’s
(IRS) efforts to bring taxpayers into compliance with their tax obligations. We performed this
review during the period May 2025 through May 2026. We are issuing this report to highlight
trends in IRS compliance activities during Fiscal Years 2024 and 2025. This review is part of our
Fiscal Year 2026 Annual Program Plan and addresses the major management and performance
challenges of Managing a Reduced Workforce and Budget and Ensuring Tax Compliance.
We made no recommendations as a result of the work performed during this review. However,
IRS officials reviewed this report prior to its issuance and agreed with the facts and conclusions
presented.
If you have any questions, please contact me or Matthew A. Weir, Assistant Inspector General for
Audit (Compliance and Enforcement Operations).

Background
Given the responsibility for administering the nation’s tax code, the Internal Revenue Service
(IRS) must consider its mission in planning and executing various legislative changes, as well as
in evaluating its performance and the results of its programs. The IRS’s mission is to:
Provide America’s taxpayers top quality service by helping them understand and meet
their tax responsibilities and enforce the law with integrity and fairness to all.
We conduct an annual review of the IRS’s fiscal year-end nationwide compliance statistics for
the civil enforcement functions: Examination and Collection.1 This year’s review focused on
analyzing enforcement activities for Fiscal Years (FY) 2023 through 2025.
Taxpayers who do not meet their tax filing, reporting, and payment obligations contribute to the
Tax Gap. The IRS projected a $696 billion gross Tax Gap for Tax Year (TY) 2022. The Tax Gap
estimate is comprised of three components: nonfiling ($63 billion), underreporting ($539 billion),
2
and underpayment ($94 billion).
According to research hosted on the IRS Statistics of Income website, many factors influence the
amount of enforcement revenue collected each year, including changes in the budget, staff, as
well as overall economic trends, e.g., enforcement revenue tends to rise as the economy grows.
Some of the factors influencing enforcement revenue also tend to have a delayed effect. For
example, examinations started in the current fiscal year can take years to close. While the impact
of economic trends on enforcement revenue is beyond the scope of this review, we address
changes to the IRS’s staffing later in the report. The IRS’s annual appropriation remained flat at
$12.3 billion for FYs 2023 through 2025, however, IRS resources were augmented by funds from
the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 (IRA).3 The IRS initially received $79.4 billion from the IRA;
however, as of March 2025, Congress subsequently reduced IRS funding to $37.6 billion,
reducing the enforcement allocation to $3.8 billion.4 Figure 1 shows allocated IRS enforcement
budget for FYs 2023 through 2026, and a breakdown of the $3.8 billion IRA funds spent. As of
December 31, 2025, the IRS has exhausted this supplemental enforcement funding.

Results of Review
Total Tax Revenue Continued to Increase in Fiscal Years 2023 through 2025
In FY 2025, total federal tax revenue paid by taxpayers to the IRS was approximately $5.3 trillion,
which was roughly $213 billion (4.2 percent) more than in FY 2024 and $619 billion
(13.2 percent) more than in FY 2023. The tax revenue paid in FY 2025 was the most ever paid by
taxpayers, without adjusting for inflation. Tax revenue from business income taxes decreased by
approximately $79 billion (14 percent) from FY 2024 to FY 2025. However, this decrease was
offset by a larger increase of $232 billion (8.6 percent) in revenue from individual income taxes.
From FY 2023 to FY 2025, individual income taxes paid increased by 17 percent. Figure 2 shows
federal tax revenue by type of tax for FYs 2023 through 2025 and the gradually increasing share
of individual income taxes.

Although Overall Enforcement Revenue Increased from FY 2023 Through
FY 2025, Revenue from Examination Declined in FY 2025
The total revenue collected from enforcement activities, as shown in Figure 3, increased from
$86.1 billion in FY 2023 to a historic high of $98.7 billion in FY 2024, before decreasing to
$93.8 billion in FY 2025. While revenues from enforcement activities have been generally
trending upwards, this revenue accounts for a relatively small portion of total federal tax

revenue. For example, enforcement revenue in FY 2025 ($93.8 billion) made up just 1.7 percent
of the $5.3 trillion paid by taxpayers. Enforcement revenues flow from various IRS enforcement
activities in the different business divisions and functions of the IRS. The IRS attributes
enforcement revenue collected to one of four functional areas:
·

Examination – The IRS has four business operating divisions with examination functions
including the Small Business/Self-Employed (SB/SE) Division, the Large Business and
International (LB&I) Division, the Taxpayer Services (TS) Division, and the Tax-Exempt and
Government Entities (TE/GE) Division. Each examination function within these divisions
has the authority to examine taxpayers’ books and records to ensure tax compliance.

·

Appeals – Taxpayers have the right to appeal the IRS’s examination decision. The
Appeals function attempts to resolve unagreed federal tax controversies without
litigation on a basis which is fair and impartial to both the government and the taxpayer.

·

Collection – The IRS Collection function collects revenue from delinquent taxpayers and
balance due accounts.

·

Automated Underreporter (AUR) – The AUR function identifies discrepancies between
amounts reported on income tax returns with information supplied to the IRS from
third-party sources, e.g., employers, banks, and credit unions.

As shown in Figure 4, the SB/SE Division accounted for the largest portion of enforcement
revenue from FY 2023 to FY 2025. Enforcement revenue generated by the SB/SE Division
increased by 11 percent from FY 2023 to FY 2024, then by another 4 percent in FY 2025. The
LB&I’s Division enforcement revenue increased by 49 percent from FY 2023 to FY 2024,
coinciding with increased IRA enforcement spending. Revenue subsequently decreased by
19 percent in FY 2025. Revenue from the TS and TE/GE Divisions both decreased in FY 2025 as
well.
Enforcement revenue is collected through the following revenue collection streams:
·

Collection Notices – The IRS issues a series of automated collection notices, beginning
with the initial notice and demand for payment letter followed by several reminder
notices, referred to in the IRS as the “notice stream.”

·

Automated Collection System (ACS) – After the notice stream, delinquent accounts
can be assigned to the ACS, which assists taxpayers through telephone-based
operations. ACS employees also work cases that may generate tax revenue from a variety
of systemic actions, such as the issuance of notices of intent to levy to prompt payments
and bring taxpayers who do not respond or cooperate into compliance.

·

Collection Queue – The Collection Queue (hereafter referred to as the Queue) is an
electronic holding file for collection cases awaiting assignment to Field Collection, the
ACS, or to private debt collectors. Taxpayers whose cases are assigned to the Queue are
sent the annually required collection notice, but no other action is taken on the cases
until they are reassigned.6

·

Field Collection – Field Collection cases are worked by revenue officers who are
assigned the most complex collection matters. These cases are resolved through direct
contact with taxpayers and their representatives.

As Figure 5 shows, collection revenue from automated notices in the notice stream (Collection

Collection function compliance activities
The mission of the IRS’s Collection function is to provide taxpayers with top quality pre- and
post-filing services by helping them understand and comply with all applicable tax laws and by
applying the tax laws with integrity and fairness. Enforcement revenues attributed to the
Collection function include those from the following revenue streams: Collection Notice, ACS,
Queue, Field Collections and Private Debt Collection, as shown in Figure 5 above.
According to the IRS, enforcement revenue in the Collection function increased between
FY 2023 and FY 2025 due to the following factors:
·

Notice Restarts – In February 2024, the IRS restarted several notice programs that had
been subject to intermittent pauses since the pandemic. These notices included certain
balance due notices, notices of intent to levy, and nonfiler programs, and included new
language intended to empower taxpayers to leverage self-service options such as Quick
Response codes and online payment agreements. The restart of these notice programs
has increased the volume of work across several programs.

·

Staffing from IRA funding – Using IRA funding, Campus collection hired 820 tax
examiners in Compliance Services Collection Operations and Automated Collection
System Support, and 1,170 Contact Representatives in ACS through September 2024.
These increased resources expanded the amount of work processed within those
functions, as well as the level of service provided to taxpayers by the ACS.

ACS Collection
Collection inventory is generally routed to the ACS when taxpayers do not respond to notices
issued via the notice stream. ACS staff generally work cases requiring telephone contact for
resolution. They also work cases using a variety of systemic actions to prompt payments and
bring taxpayers into compliance.
In FY 2024, with 4,828 employees, ACS collected approximately $12.7 billion in enforcement
revenue, with average collections exceeding $2.6 million per employee. The following year, in
FY 2025, ACS collected approximately $14.1 billion in enforcement revenue with
2,857 employees, averaging over $4.9 million collected per employee. This represents an
increase of 88 percent in the average amount collected per employee from FY 2024 to FY 2025.
While this increase in collection revenue per employee appears to be significant, we believe the
increase may be due to a combination of increased collection revenues from restarted notices
and reduced end-of-year staffing. This is a trend we will continue to analyze in future reviews
and we expect the impacts of these reductions will be clearer in the future.

Field Collection
Field Collection works collection cases that are complex and high risk. Employees (revenue
officers) receive legal and financial training to work on these cases. Revenue officers work with
taxpayers to bring them into compliance by obtaining delinquent returns and payments on past
due tax delinquencies or establishing payment plans. When necessary, revenue officers take
enforcement actions such as levies, Notice of Federal Tax Lien filings, or seizures of property.
In FY 2024, with 3,418 revenue officers, Field Collection collected approximately $5 billion in
enforcement revenue, which is an average of approximately $1.5 million per employee. In
FY 2025, with 2,544 revenue officers, Field Collection collected approximately $8.2 billion in
enforcement revenue, which is an average of approximately $3.2 million per employee. As with
the ACS calculation above, the increase in enforcement revenue and related increase in average
collection per employee is likely to be due to a combination of increased revenue from the
restart of collection notices and reduced end-of-year staffing.

Nonfiler ASFR Program
ASFR case volume increased in FY 2024 with the restart of return delinquency notices.
Authorized by Internal Revenue Code (I.R.C.) Section (§) 6020, the ASFR Program is an important
program that enforces filing compliance on taxpayers who have not filed individual income tax
returns but owe a significant income tax liability. The ASFR Program determines and assesses tax
liability by securing valid income tax returns from taxpayers or by computing tax, penalties, and
interest, based upon information returns from third parties, for example Form W-2, Wage and
Tax Statement, and Form 1099-MISC, Miscellaneous Information. ASFR determinations are
based only on income reported to the IRS and do not incorporate all deductions and credits to
which taxpayers may be entitled. In response to a proposed ASFR assessment, taxpayers may file
their own tax returns to claim deductions and credits to potentially reduce their liability.

As noted in our prior report, IRS processing centers were shut down in FY 2020 due to the
pandemic and many nonfiler initiatives were paused.8 This included the ASFR Program, which
has been paused intermittently since April 2020 and was not fully restarted until May 2024. From
FY 2023 to FY 2024, additional tax assessments decreased 93 percent. The IRS explained that the
large decrease in cases “closed with contact” in FY 2024 was due to the IRS having worked
through most of the remaining inventory from before the FY 2020 pause in issuing notices and
from FY 2022 when the notices were briefly restarted before being paused again. While the
program resumed in May 2024, ASFR cases typically take approximately seven to nine months to
close; therefore, the cases started in FY 2024 did not affect the FY 2024 totals for cases closed
with contact. Cases closed without contact are closed automatically and were directly impacted
by the restart of the ASFR Program in FY 2024, as shown by the 112 percent increase from
FY 2023 to FY 2024.
From FY 2024 to FY 2025, the number of cases closed with contact increased from 1,169 to
161,908 as new cases worked after the restart of the program began to close while cases closed
without contact stayed at similar levels to the prior year. Overall additional assessments
increased to $2.9 billion in FY 2025 from $1.1 billion in FY 2023 and $82 million in FY 2024.

Private Debt Collection
In December 2015, legislation was enacted that requires the IRS to use designated contractors
to assist in collecting tax delinquencies. The Fixing America’s Surface Transportation Act includes
a provision requiring the IRS to enter into qualified tax collection contracts with private
collection agencies to work “inactive tax receivables."9 Retained earnings from this program are
also used to fund the hiring of Special Compliance Personnel that perform ACS and Field
Collection related tasks and fall under the umbrella of Private Debt Collection.
During FYs 2023 through 2025, the Private Debt Collection program brought in $6.1 billion in
revenue with collections increasing 119 percent from FY 2023 to FY 2025. Of this total, almost
$2 billion was collected by private collection agencies, with the remaining amount collected by
Special Compliance Personnel.

of voluntary compliance. According to the most recent Tax Gap projections provided by the IRS,

IRS examinations are intended to detect and correct noncompliance of audited taxpayers, as
well as create an environment to encourage non-audited taxpayers to comply voluntarily. These
efforts have both a direct and indirect impact on the Tax Gap. In addition to the direct impact on
the taxpayers examined, the IRS Research function has determined that examinations have a
substantial indirect multiplier effect on tax compliance, meaning when a taxpayer is audited
there could be a change in compliance by members of the taxpayer’s social network and could
potentially be a deterrence for noncompliance. Examinations can range from the issuance of an
IRS notice asking for clarification of a single tax return item that appears to be incorrect
(correspondence examination) to a face-to-face interview and review of the taxpayer’s records
(field examination).

Large corporate and partnership examinations
One of the IRS’s goals for the use of IRA enforcement spending was to improve the tax
compliance of large corporations and large partnerships. The LB&I Division is primarily
responsible for compliance of these large entities and high-wealth taxpayers. The examination
rate of partnerships over the years has decreased as the number of partnership returns has
increased. In March 2026, we reported that examinations of large partnerships often result in no
change to tax, highlighting an increase in no-change rates from TY 2014 to TY 2021.14 A
no-change result on a partnership examination occurs when there is no adjustment to the
partnership’s income, loss, deductions, or credits on the partnership return or the partners’ share
of these partnership attributes. The IRS has developed models in recent years to attempt to
improve the no-change rate, with the latest attempt through the Large Partnership Compliance
Program. However, its efforts to date have had little success.
As shown in Figure 11, large corporate examination starts decreased in FY 2024 but increased
overall from FY 2023 to FY 2025 by 7 percent while partnership examination starts decreased by
76 percent in the same period from a high of 6,709 in FY 2023 to 1,589 in FY 2025.

The significant decline in partnership audits in FY 2024 was attributed to multiple factors. The
IRS stated that closures for partnership examinations were impacted by events related to the
launch of the Pass-Through Field Compliance function in October 2023. LB&I Division officials
stated that standing up the Pass-Through Field Compliance function required moving
employees from the SB/SE Division and other functions in the LB&I Division to the Pass-Through
Entities (PTE) Practice Area. This affected their ability to start new examinations as they focused
on closing existing cases and transitioning them to the PTE. SB/SE Division officials stated that
efforts to change the focus of revenue agents’ work resulted in a need to rewrite partnership
training and reorder training. As a result, trainees who completed Form 1040, U.S. Individual Tax
Return training in FYs 2024 and 2025 could not attend partnership training until FY 2026.
In FY 2025, work volumes were also impacted by staffing issues. While the IRS Direct Hire
initiative was underway by March 2023, most of the hiring and onboarding was completed in
the first quarter of FY 2025. This required participation from frontline managers and employees,
impacting their work. The workforce rebalancing efforts in Calendar Year 2025 further impacted
both starts and closures due to personnel actions taken with respect to probationary employees
and employees participating in voluntary separation programs.
While the number of corporate examinations closed increased by 29 percent from FY 2023 to
FY 2025, the proposed additional tax from corporate examinations is down 41 percent. IRS
officials attributed this decrease to, among other things, the change in the corporate tax rate
from 35 percent to 21 percent.15 As more current tax years close with the 21 percent rate, there
was a reduction in dollars assessed.

As noted above, hiring in FY 2024 increased Examination and Collection staffing significantly
from FY 2023 to FY 2024. The workforce reduction efforts in FY 2025 led to a 27 percent
reduction in Examination and Collection staff levels in comparison to FY 2024. As of
January 10, 2026, staffing levels had fallen even further. These losses present a challenge to
improving taxpayer service and enforcing the nation’s tax laws. This challenge is even greater as
the IRS implements the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and manages the downstream effects of
restarting several collection programs in FY 2024. We are concerned about how staffing losses
are impacting the IRS’s ability to ensure that it meets Department priorities and we have
separate reviews that will evaluate their plans.

Conclusion
Taxpayers paid the IRS $5.3 trillion in total tax revenue in FY 2025 amounting to a 13.2 percent
increase from FY 2023, and the most tax revenue ever paid by taxpayers, without adjusting for
inflation. This increase in revenue appears to be driven by individual income taxes, which
increased by 17 percent since FY 2023.

The IRS’s compliance operations went from collecting $86.1 billion in enforcement revenue in
FY 2023 to collecting a historic high of $98.7 billion in FY 2024 before decreasing to $93.8 billion
in FY 2025. The significant increases in enforcement revenue are attributed to the restart of
automated collection notices in February 2024. In addition, the increased collection notices
resulted in increased volumes of work across several work streams including the nonfiler
programs, balance due notices, and notifications of levy, and led to higher inventory levels. IRS
Collection officials stated that the effects from workforce reduction efforts were felt beginning in
March 2025 and again in June 2025 with the departure of significant numbers of staff. While the
workforce reductions influenced metrics in FY 2025, the downstream effects of these reductions
are likely to become more apparent over time.
Further, in FYs 2023 and 2024, the IRS expanded hiring and its enforcement of taxpayers with
complex tax filings and high-dollar noncompliance. As a result, the IRS was able to increase the
proportion of high-income examinations started relative to all examination starts. However, the
workforce reductions in FY 2025 had a significant impact on staffing levels, with total
Examination and Collection staffing levels at the end of FY 2025 lower than in FY 2023. These
reductions contributed to a 30 percent decrease in examination starts in FY 2025 compared to
FY 2024. Both SB/SE examination closures and proposed additional tax assessments notably
declined from the second quarter to third quarter of FY 2025, coinciding with workforce
reductions, and remained lower for the remainder of the year. IRA enforcement funds are now
depleted with the last monies totaling approximately $136 million spent in FY 2026.

Glossary of Terms
Term

Definition

Automated Collection
System

A legacy system that provides a computerized telephone tax collection
system designed to assist Collection employees with automatic contact and
follow-up on delinquent taxpayers.

Return Program

A system designed to assess taxes on wage earners who fail to file tax
returns. It analyzes information submitted to the IRS and historical tax
return information.

Balance Due

Occurs when a taxpayer has an outstanding liability for taxes, penalties, and
interest.

Balance Due Notice

Automated IRS systems send notices to the taxpayer informing them of the
outstanding liability for taxes, penalties, and/or interest.

Campus

The data processing arm of the IRS. The campuses process paper and
electronic submissions, correct errors, and forward data to the Computing
Centers for analysis and posting to taxpayer accounts.

Enforcement

Appropriation for necessary expenses of the IRS to determine and collect
owed taxes, to provide legal and litigation support, to conduct criminal
investigations, to enforce criminal statutes related to violations of Internal
Revenue laws and other financial crimes, to purchase and hire passenger
motor vehicles, and to provide other services as authorized by
5 U.S.C. § 3109.

Field Collection

The unit in the Area Offices consisting of revenue officers who handle
face-to-face contacts with taxpayers to collect delinquent accounts or
secure unfiled returns.

Fiscal Year

Any yearly accounting period, regardless of its relationship to a calendar
year. The federal government’s fiscal year begins on October 1 and ends on
September 30.

IRS Data Book

Published annually by the IRS and contains statistical tables and
organizational information presented on a fiscal year basis. It provides
information on activities conducted by the IRS, such as taxes collected,
enforcement, taxpayer assistance, budget, workforce, and other selected
activities.

Levy

A method used by the IRS to collect outstanding taxes from sources such
as bank accounts and wages or a legal seizure of property to satisfy a tax
debt.

Nonfiler

Individual and business taxpayers who have been identified as liable to file
a tax return, but who have not filed a tax return by the return due date or
extended due date.

Term

Definition

Revenue Agent

An employee in the Examination function who conducts face-to-face
examinations of more complex tax returns, such as businesses,
partnerships, corporations, and specialty taxes.

Revenue Officer

An employee in the Collection function who provides customer service by
explaining taxpayer rights and responsibilities, collects delinquent accounts,
secures delinquent returns, counsels taxpayers on their tax filing and
payment obligations, conducts tax investigations, files Notices of Federal
Tax Lien, releases federal tax liens, and performs seizures and sales of
delinquent taxpayer assets.

Revenue Officer
Compliance Sweeps

A revenue officer compliance sweep is an assignment of work strategy used
by Field Collection to support compliance initiatives. The purpose of
revenue officer compliance sweeps is to address an increase in unassigned
high priority inventory aligned to a compliance initiative (e.g., egregious
employment tax cases, high-income nonfilers or specialty program) in a
given area or program due to current field compliance staffing or to
support a compliance initiative.

Tax Compliance Officer

An employee in the Examination function who primarily conducts
examinations of individual taxpayers through interviews at IRS field offices.

Tax Examiner

In the context of this report, an employee located in a field office who
conducts examinations through correspondence. However, the tax
examiner position is also used for many other types of positions located in
various IRS offices.

Tax Gap

The estimated difference between the amount of tax that taxpayers should
pay and the amount that is paid voluntarily and on time.

Total Positive Income

The sum of all positive amounts shown for the various sources of income
reported on the individual tax return and, therefore, excludes losses.
