What GAO Found
The Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) reduced
its workforce by 29 percent in 2025 and modified the types of programs it funds
in response to presidential directives and executive branch guidance. HUD
largely achieved workforce reductions through deferred resignation programs. Over
half the employees who separated had at least 10 years of service at HUD, including
41 percent with more than 20 years of service. HUD did not publish notice of
any reorganization plans that led to field office closures in 2025, though in
2026 it announced plans to consolidate one field office into a nearby regional
office.

About half of HUD’s 68 active grant programs made new
awards in fiscal year 2025, but the median value of those awards was higher
than in previous years. HUD also terminated about 21 percent of its active
contracts in 2025, valued at about $367 million. Most terminated contracts
involved professional or IT services.
Representatives of 20 stakeholder organizations GAO
interviewed—including fair housing grantees and agencies, public housing
agencies, and Section 8 project-based rental assistance property
managers—reported confusion, delays, and operational disruptions following
HUD’s changes. Nearly all said that experienced points of contact had left,
making it harder to navigate HUD programs. Fair housing stakeholders reported
reducing services or staffing during monthslong funding gaps. Stakeholders also
reported delays with physical inspections of HUD-assisted properties.
Since January 2025, HUD has reported launching initiatives
aimed at increasing efficiency and streamlining operations. For example, HUD
reported developing a unified grants system to standardize grant administration
and a project voucher initiative to automate aspects of rental assistance
processing. HUD did not respond to GAO’s requests to discuss these initiatives
or provide supporting documentation. As a result, Congress and the public have
limited information on the status or effectiveness of these initiatives.
Why GAO Did This
Study
HUD administers programs related to housing assistance,
community development, and fair housing. To carry out these programs, HUD
headquarters and field staff work with public housing agencies, private
property owners, nonprofit organizations, and other stakeholders.
Executive orders and other actions issued in 2025 called
for workforce and program changes at HUD. GAO was asked to examine the effects of
these changes. This report describes (1) changes HUD made to its workforce,
field office structure, and grant and contract spending in 2025; (2) effects of
those changes on selected HUD functions reported by housing stakeholders; and
(3) steps HUD has publicly reported to manage the changes.
HUD did not respond to GAO’s requests for information
throughout the audit. GAO analyzed publicly available 2025 data on HUD’s
workforce, terminated contracts, and grant spending. GAO selected three HUD
functions for review, chosen to reflect a mix of funding levels and other
criteria: (1) enforcement of the Fair Housing Act and Violence Against Women
Act of 1994, (2) administration of the Section 8 Project-Based Rental
Assistance Program, and (3) coordination of inspections of HUD-assisted
properties.
GAO also conducted four site visits and interviewed
representatives of a nongeneralizable selection of 20 local and national
housing organizations, including public housing agencies and fair housing
organizations. GAO also reviewed court documents and publicly available data,
and agency documentation related to HUD’s workforce and efficiency planning.

Letter
September 30, 2026
Congressional Requesters
The Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)
administers a wide variety of programs aimed at strengthening communities,
preventing housing discrimination, and helping households obtain affordable
housing. Beginning in January 2025, in response to presidential directives, HUD
took steps to reduce its workforce, streamline operations, and change the types
of programs it funds. [1]
For example, HUD offered employees opportunities to voluntarily resign,
announced reviews and terminations of grants and contracts that did not align
with administration priorities, and reportedly planned to close 34 of its 54
field offices. [2]
These changes prompted questions from members of Congress about potential
effects on HUD’s capacity and service delivery. [3]
You asked us to examine the effects of workforce
reductions, grant and contract terminations, and field office closures on HUD’s
ability to meet its statutory obligations and carry out its mission, as well as
the extent to which HUD followed statutory requirements in planning and
implementing these changes. This report describes
1.
changes HUD has made to its workforce, field office structure, and grant
and contract spending since January 2025;
2.
the effects of those changes on selected HUD functions, as reported by
HUD housing stakeholders; and
3.
steps HUD has publicly reported to manage these changes.
To describe changes to HUD’s workforce, we analyzed Office
of Personnel Management (OPM) federal workforce data as of February 2026 on
hires and separations from December 2024 to December 2025. To assess the
reliability of these data, we reviewed related documentation and performed
manual testing. We determined the data were sufficiently reliable for reporting
on changes in HUD’s number of employees, hires, and separations. To describe
changes HUD made to its field office structure, we reviewed Federal Register
notices from January 2025 through June 2026 for plans to consolidate, relocate,
or close field offices.
To describe changes in HUD’s grant and contract spending,
we analyzed USAspending.gov data on the number and value of new grants awarded
in fiscal year (FY) 2025 and compared them with the same data from FY 2016
through FY 2024. We also analyzed System for Award Management (SAM.gov) data on
contracts HUD terminated from January 20, 2025, to December 31, 2025. To assess
the reliability of both sets of data, we reviewed documentation and performed
electronic testing. We determined the data were sufficiently reliable for
describing changes in grant and contract spending.
To identify effects reported by HUD housing stakeholders,
we selected three HUD functions because they had high funding levels, some
degree of field office involvement, and reportedly experienced staffing
reductions: (1) enforcement of the Fair Housing Act and Violence Against Women
Act of 1994, (2) administration of the Section 8 Project-Based Rental
Assistance (PBRA) program, and (3) coordination of property inspections.
We conducted site visits to four cities with HUD field or
regional offices: San Francisco, California; Omaha, Nebraska; Manchester, New
Hampshire; and Nashville, Tennessee. We selected these locations to provide
geographic diversity, reflect a mix of urban and rural settings, and include
locations where HUD offices reportedly experienced staffing changes. During the
visits, we interviewed representatives of fair housing stakeholders—Fair
Housing Initiatives Program (FHIP) grantees and Fair Housing Assistance Program
(FHAP) agencies—as well as public housing agencies (PHA) and private property
managers. We also interviewed representatives of national organizations
representing fair housing, affordable housing management, and PHAs and reviewed
information submitted in court cases involving HUD regarding the effects of
funding disruptions and potential reductions in force. [4] While information collected through
our site visits and other interviews cannot be generalized to the larger
population of HUD housing stakeholders, it provides perspectives on effects
experienced by stakeholders.
To describe steps HUD has publicly reported to manage
these changes, we reviewed publicly available documentation on its initiatives
and plans. HUD officials did not provide information in response to our
requests on various topics, including any activities HUD is planning or recent
changes to steps reported in public documents, as discussed later in the
report. For more information on our objectives, scope, and methodology, see
appendix I.
We conducted this performance audit from April 2025 to
September 2026 in accordance with generally accepted government auditing
standards. Those standards require that we plan and perform the audit to obtain
sufficient, appropriate evidence to provide a reasonable basis for our findings
and conclusions based on our audit objectives. We believe that the evidence
obtained provides a reasonable basis for our findings and conclusions based on
our audit objectives.
Background
HUD’s Structure and Partners
HUD administers a wide variety of programs through a
network of staff in its Washington, D.C., headquarters, 10 regional offices,
and 54 field offices. [5]
Regional and field office staff partner with local PHAs, private property
owners, and nonprofits to implement HUD programs and services related to
affordable housing, fair housing, property inspections, and other areas.
HUD is required by law to have an office in every state to
ensure the adequate processing of applications for loan and mortgage insurance
under the National Housing Act. [6]
Before reorganizing a HUD field, regional, insuring, or area office, HUD must
issue a notice in the Federal Register that includes a cost-benefit analysis of
the proposed action. [7]
Selected HUD Programs and Functions
HUD headquarters and field office staff help carry out the
following programs and functions discussed in this report:
Fair Housing Act enforcement. HUD’s Office of Fair
Housing and Equal Opportunity (FHEO) investigates housing discrimination
complaints, promotes fair housing laws and education, and conducts civil rights
compliance reviews. FHEO also administers FHAP, which funds state and local
enforcement agencies to investigate and resolve complaints of housing
discrimination, and FHIP, which funds state and local governments, nonprofit
organizations, or other private or public entities to carry out programs
designed to obtain enforcement of fair housing laws, and to provide education
and outreach. HUD is to use funding to conduct—through contracts with fair
housing enforcement organizations—a range of investigative activities designed
to carry out testing, discover and remedy discrimination in real estate
markets, and provide technical assistance to local fair housing organizations. [8]
Violence Against Women Act enforcement. FHEO
enforces the Violence Against Women Act in HUD-assisted (subsidized) housing.
The act seeks to protect survivors of domestic and dating violence, sexual
assault, and stalking from being denied admission, evicted, or denied housing
assistance because of their status. [9]
FHEO carries out this responsibility through rulemaking, oversight of housing
providers, compliance monitoring, and complaint investigations.
Section 8 Project-Based Rental Assistance Program.
PBRA provides approximately 1.3 million affordable housing units for low-income
and very low-income families. [10]
PBRA assistance is attached to a housing unit and households benefit from the
assistance if they continue to live in the unit. HUD pays property owners the
difference between the tenant’s contribution and a rent set by HUD.
Physical Inspections of HUD-Assisted Properties.
HUD’s Real Estate Assessment Center primarily uses contractors to inspect
multifamily and public housing properties on a risk-based schedule, generally
every 1 to 3 years. Property owners, including PHAs and PBRA property managers,
are responsible for correcting identified deficiencies, and HUD field office
staff monitor their compliance.
Executive Orders and HUD Actions in 2025
Beginning in January 2025, the President issued several
memorandums and executive orders directing federal agencies to reduce the size
of their workforces and align their programming with new administration
priorities. In response, HUD took steps to reduce staffing and modify the types
of programs it funds (see table 1). Several housing groups and other HUD
stakeholders challenged some of these actions in court.

HUD Lost 29 Percent of
Its Staff, and About Half of Its Grant Programs Did Not Make Awards in 2025
HUD’s Workforce Decreased by 29 Percent, Mostly Through Deferred
Resignation
HUD’s workforce decreased by about 29 percent between
December 2024 and December 2025, from about 8,800 to about 6,300 employees (see
fig. 1). In total, 3,188 employees separated from HUD in 2025, according to OPM
federal workforce data. [11]
Of these, 2,296 employees, or about 72 percent, left through a voluntary
HUD-sponsored or
government-wide deferred resignation program. [12] The remaining 892 left through
terminations, retirements, resignations, or other means. Additionally, from
January 2025 to December 2025, HUD hired 193 employees.

Median Award Values Generally Increased
HUD administers various programs that award grants to
state and local governments, PHAs, and nonprofits for community development,
affordable housing, homelessness assistance, and other purposes. Of the 68 HUD
programs that made new grant awards from FY 2016 through FY 2024, 32 made new
awards in FY 2025, according to our analysis of USAspending.gov data. [18] Twenty-one of those 32 programs made
fewer new awards in FY 2025 than their median annual number from FY 2016
through FY 2024. However, 24 had higher median award values in FY 2025 than
during the earlier period (see fig. 4). Appendix II contains additional

Stakeholders Reported
Confusion, Delays, and Operational Disruptions
HUD stakeholders we spoke with reported that the agency’s
workforce, spending, and other changes in 2025 resulted in confusion about
agency contacts and guidance, delays in communication, and operational strain
during funding disruptions. We interviewed representatives of 20 stakeholder
organizations, including FHIP grantees, FHAP agencies, PHAs, PBRA property
managers, and national housing organizations. [22]
HUD did not provide information in response to our questions about these
reported issues.
Confusion About Changes to Contacts, Guidance, and Meetings
Nearly all stakeholders we interviewed described confusion
following the departure of experienced HUD staff, and most also cited
cancellations of standing meetings with the agency and changes in HUD guidance.
·
Loss of experienced points of contact. Nearly all
stakeholders told us that longtime HUD contacts left in early 2025 without
warning or a handoff, making it harder to get reliable information and answers.
One fair housing stakeholder said all its HUD contacts had left, including
employees with decades of experience and relatively new hires. Another said
they no longer knew where to refer Violence Against Women Act complaints.
Stakeholders told us that before 2025, they often contacted field or regional
staff, sometimes daily, with questions about complex grant renewals or project
approvals.
Most stakeholders also expressed
concern about a loss of institutional or local knowledge. Most said that newly
assigned HUD staff lacked experience with complex programs and processes, and
that the information they received was not always as reliable or consistent as
before. For example, one fair housing stakeholder said a new HUD employee was
unaware of basic legal distinctions when advising on fair housing requirements.
Stakeholders noted that the loss of institutional knowledge and unreliable
information could lead to inconsistent implementation of program requirements.
·
Disruptions to standing meetings and conferences. National
housing organizations and two fair housing stakeholders reported cancellations
of standing meetings and reduced HUD participation in conferences in 2025. One
stakeholder said their twice-monthly meetings with Office of Public Housing
staff were canceled in January 2025 without warning, and HUD staff did not
respond to follow-up emails. Another said HUD, historically an active
participant in its annual conference, did not participate in 2025.
Stakeholders said these
disruptions reduced knowledge sharing and created uncertainty about agency
contacts. Two fair housing stakeholders said they previously had regular
check-ins with HUD grant administrators, which also allowed HUD staff to learn
about and share best practices. All FHIP grantees and FHAP agencies we
interviewed said they were either unsure who their grant administrator was or
had less communication with a newly assigned grant administrator.
·
Rescinded or insufficient guidance. Stakeholders reported
confusion when HUD rescinded guidance or did not issue new guidance explaining
how to comply with new executive orders. Two fair housing stakeholders said HUD
rescinded guidance on accommodating requests for emotional support animals
without replacing it. One of these stakeholders told us they researched the
changes and created new materials for housing providers and tenants, while the
other said they did not have the resources to do so. [23] National housing organizations
similarly said HUD directed members to modify existing plans to align with
executive orders but did not explain what changes were required. According to
the national organizations, when field office staff could not provide clear
answers, members turned to national organizations for assistance.
Delayed Communications and Program Operations
Stakeholders described delays in communication and program
operations in 2025.
·
Delayed responses from HUD staff. Nearly all stakeholders
we interviewed reported reduced responsiveness from HUD staff in 2025. One fair
housing stakeholder noted that questions that previously received same-day
responses could take up to 30 days. Three stakeholders attributed the delays to
increased workloads and said they thought the remaining HUD staff were working
hard to respond. Three PHAs said they had not experienced delayed responses.
·
Delayed approvals. Most stakeholders reported delayed
approvals for activities such as training, grant proposals, and press releases
after regional and field office staff were directed to route requests through
HUD headquarters. One PBRA property manager said approval to refinance a
rehabilitation project that began in 2024 remained pending well into 2026,
creating financial pressure. One fair housing stakeholder also said they
learned one week before a conference that HUD training funds could not be used,
requiring them to find alternative funding.
·
Delayed fair housing complaint processing and closure. Nearly
all FHIP grantees and FHAP agencies we interviewed said changes to HUD’s
complaint process increased case closure times. Two fair housing stakeholders
said complaints are now sent to a centralized online form rather than regional
FHEO staff, and that they no longer receive prompt confirmation or updates.
Stakeholders also linked longer processing times to increased workloads. [24] Two fair housing stakeholders said
that because of FHEO workforce reductions, FHEO started referring more fair
housing cases to them, increasing their workloads and wait times for
complainants.
·
Delayed property inspections. A PHA, three PBRA
property managers, and a national housing organization reported inspection
delays in 2025, while two PHAs and one property manager said they did not
experience delays. One PHA said historically HUD contractors would reach out
early in the year to schedule inspections throughout the year. However, the PHA
noted that they believe, because of the workforce reductions in 2025,
contractors did not reach out until November that year and scheduled hundreds
of unit inspections during a 2-week period between Thanksgiving and Christmas,
creating an administrative burden for the PHA. One PBRA property manager said
several inspections were delayed or canceled in 2025 because of slow responses
from HUD, potentially delaying the identification and correction of needed
maintenance. According to the property manager, as of April 2026, those
inspections had not been scheduled.
·
Delayed assistance with technology. PHAs and PBRA property
managers reported delays in accessing HUD technology systems in 2025. They
attributed the delays to fewer HUD staff with technical knowledge of and
administrative rights to the systems. For example, one PHA could not access
HUD’s grant and subsidy disbursement system for 3 months after it hired a new
executive director. Staff at the PHA said that it took time to find a HUD
employee with the proper administrative rights to the system to grant its new
executive director access to the system. During that period, the PHA used
reserve funds to cover salaries, utilities, and other operating costs but was
unable to pay some contractors.
Funding Disruptions
All FHIP grantees and FHAP agencies, along with two PHAs
and three PBRA property managers, reported gaps in HUD funding in 2025 that
strained their operations. Four stakeholders said they reduced services as a
result, affecting fair housing enforcement and affordable housing services.
·
Monthslong gaps in fair housing grant funding. As
described earlier, in February 2025, HUD terminated 78 FHIP grants and did not
release funding for the second and third years of existing multiyear awards,
resulting in a 10-month funding gap. [25]
Grantees we spoke with reported reducing staff, services, or office space as a
result. One reported that they stopped all fair housing testing, accepted fewer
new complaints, and referred potential clients elsewhere or to HUD. Another
FHIP grantee said it opened 25 discrimination cases during the 10-month funding
gap, compared with 82 during the preceding 10 months.
·
Delayed rental assistance payments and loss of grant funding. Two
PBRA property managers said delayed rental assistance payments led them to
delay or reduce routine maintenance, potentially resulting in more costly
repairs later. Members of a national affordable housing organization and two
PBRA property managers also reported losing grants supporting resident
services. For example, the national organization told us that HUD changed the
terms of a previously awarded $23 million grant to one of its members. The
member intended to use the grant for upgrades to housing for older adults and
people with disabilities, including installing air conditioning units, but
could no longer use the funds because of the term changes.
HUD Reported
Initiatives to Improve Efficiency
Since 2025, HUD has reported launching initiatives aimed
at increasing efficiency and streamlining operations. These efforts included
establishing a HUD Efficiency Task Force to streamline grant and other business
processes, modernize IT systems, and simplify rules and regulations, according
to HUD’s 2025 agency financial report published in December 2025. [26]
The report described other initiatives, including the
following:
HUD Unified Grants System. HUD reported developing
this system to consolidate and standardize the grant process and increase
transparency into how grantees and PHAs spend grant funding. According to the
report, the new system will use artificial intelligence for multiple functions,
including analyzing grantee expenditures for early identification of fraud,
waste, and abuse.
Project Voucher. HUD reported that this initiative
aims to improve the integrity and efficiency of rental assistance programs by
analyzing voucher payments to identify improper payments. Additional proposed
activities include collecting tenant documentation through a web-based portal
to identify missing information and automating the calculation of rental
assistance payments based on tenant documentation.
HUD EfficienCX. HUD reported that EfficienCX is
intended to improve the efficiency of service delivery through proposed
projects such as streamlining HUD’s website and consolidating customer service
phone numbers into a single point of contact.
These initiatives may help HUD modernize and improve the
delivery of its programs and services. However, it is too soon to assess their
effectiveness or the extent to which they will help HUD achieve its mission.
HUD officials did not respond to our request to discuss the status of these
efforts—including any changes made since these efforts were announced—or the
extent to which they believe they will address the effects stakeholders
reported in our interviews. As a result, Congress and the public have limited
information on the status or effectiveness of these efforts.

Appendix I: Objectives,
Scope, and Methodology
This report describes (1) changes the Department of
Housing and Urban Development (HUD) made to its workforce, field office
structure, and grant and contract spending since January 2025; (2) the effects
of those changes on selected HUD functions reported by HUD housing
stakeholders; and (3) steps HUD has publicly reported to manage these changes.
To address our first objective, we analyzed Office of
Personnel Management (OPM) federal workforce data as of February 2026 covering
HUD employees from December 2024 to December 2025. We analyzed employee hires
and separations, including separations through deferred resignation programs,
by separation type, length of service, and official duty station.
To assess the reliability of the data, we reviewed
information on OPM’s website and conducted manual data testing. We found the
data sufficiently reliable for purposes of reporting HUD employee hires and
separations and analyzing separations by type, length of service, and official
duty station. However, we determined the program-office data provided by HUD in
the federal workforce data were not sufficiently reliable for analyzing hires
and separations by program office. We requested program office hires and
separations data directly from HUD, but HUD did not provide information in
response to our request or respond to our questions about how it compiles and
submits information to OPM. As a result, we could not analyze separations by
program office. OPM’s data also does not include regional- or
field-office-level data, so we could not analyze employee changes at those
levels.
To describe changes to HUD’s field office structure, we
reviewed Federal Register notices published by HUD from January 2025 through
June 2026 that contained the terms “field,” “cost benefit,” “reorganization,”
or “consolidation.” [27]
We identified 78 notices, one of which discussed consolidating and relocating
one field office. [28]
We also requested from HUD, but did not receive, information on field office
restructurings, consolidations, or closures.
To describe changes in HUD’s grant spending, we analyzed
USAspending.gov data to identify the 68 HUD grant programs that awarded at
least one new grant from FY 2016 through FY 2025. [29] For each program, we compared the
number and median value of new awards made in FY 2025 with the median annual
number and median award value from FY 2016 through 2024. [30] To assess the reliability of the
data, we reviewed USAspending.gov documentation and performed electronic
testing on data we used. We determined that the data were sufficiently reliable
for reporting on the number and value of new grants. We could not analyze the
number or value of grants HUD terminated in 2025 because data on terminated
grants are not publicly available and HUD did not provide information in
response to our request.
To describe contracts HUD terminated in 2025, we analyzed
data from the System for Award Management (SAM.gov), formerly maintained in the
Federal Procurement Data System, to identify the number and value of terminated
contracts and their obligations. [31]
To identify active contracts, we searched SAM.gov for contracts with
performance periods ending after January 20, 2025, as of January 19, 2025. We
then identified active contracts terminated for convenience from January 20 to
December 31, 2025, and analyzed them by services provided, HUD program office,
and business size. [32]
To assess the reliability of these data, we reviewed
SAM.gov documentation and performed electronic testing on data we used. We
determined the data were sufficiently reliable for reporting on the number,
value, obligations, and characteristics of terminated contracts.
To address the second objective, we selected three HUD
functions for our discussions with stakeholders about the effects of HUD’s
workforce and other changes in 2025: (1) enforcement of the Fair Housing Act
and Violence Against Women Act, (2) administration of the Section 8
Project-Based Rental Assistance program (PBRA), and (3) coordination of
property inspections. We selected these functions because they had high funding
levels, involved field offices to some degree, and had reportedly experienced
staffing reductions.
We conducted site visits to San Francisco, California;
Nashville, Tennessee; Manchester, New Hampshire; and Omaha, Nebraska, in March
and April 2026. We selected these locations to provide geographic diversity, a
mix of urban and rural perspectives, and include locations with reported HUD
staffing changes. To identify locations with a mix of settings, we used the
U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Rural-Urban Commuting Area categories
(metropolitan, micropolitan, small town, and rural) to identify cities in states
that ranked in the 75th percentile for each category. We also reviewed court
documents and news articles to identify field offices that reportedly
experienced workforce reductions or received reduction-in-force notices that
were later blocked by a court. In addition, we selected one location with a HUD
regional office to obtain the perspectives of stakeholders who work with a
regional office rather than a field office.
Across the four selected locations, we interviewed
representatives of four Fair Housing Initiatives Program (FHIP) grantees, two
Fair Housing Assistance Program (FHAP) agencies, four public housing agencies
(PHA), three PBRA property management companies, and one state housing agency
that managed PBRA properties. [33]
In each location, we interviewed at least one FHIP grantee, PHA, and PBRA
property manager. We interviewed FHAP agencies in two of the four locations. [34]
Our interviews focused on the effects of HUD’s workforce
reductions, grant and contract terminations, and funding disruptions on housing
stakeholders, particularly as they related to our three selected HUD functions.
We also planned to interview HUD field office staff in each location regarding
the effects of staffing reductions on these functions and staff workloads, but
HUD did not provide contact information or facilitate our interviews with field
office staff in response to our request.
In addition, from August 2025 to April 2026, we conducted
semi-structured interviews with representatives from six national housing
organizations: the Council of Large Public Housing Authorities, National
Affordable Housing Management Association, National Association of Housing and
Redevelopment Officials, National Fair Housing Alliance, National Low Income
Housing Coalition, and Public Housing Authorities Directors Association. The
information collected through our site visits and other interviews cannot be
generalized to the larger population of HUD housing stakeholders.
In total, we interviewed 20 HUD stakeholder organizations,
including six national organizations and 14 organizations from site visits. To
characterize how many of the 20 HUD stakeholder organizations reported an
effect, we used “nearly all” for 16 to 20 stakeholder organizations, “most” for
10 to 15, and “several” for five to nine. We also reviewed relevant court
documents from HUD lawsuits related to the effects of funding disruptions and
potential reductions in force. [35]
To address the third objective, we reviewed publicly
available information regarding steps HUD has taken or plans to take to manage
the changes initiated in 2025. This information included congressional budget
justifications, annual financial reports, and testimony before Congress. HUD
officials did not provide information in response to our requests to discuss
the status of these efforts nor the extent to which they may address the
effects stakeholders reported.
We conducted this performance audit from April 2025 to
September 2026 in accordance with generally accepted government auditing
standards. Those standards require that we plan and perform the audit to obtain
sufficient, appropriate evidence to provide a reasonable basis for our findings
and conclusions based on our audit objectives. We believe that the evidence
obtained provides a reasonable basis for our findings and conclusions based on
our audit objectives.
