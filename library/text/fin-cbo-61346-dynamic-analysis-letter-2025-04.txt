Re: How CBO and Joint Committee Staff Prepare Dynamic Analyses

Dear Chairman Pfluger and Chairman Westerman:
This letter responds to your questions regarding how the Congressional
Budget Office (CBO) and staff of the Joint Committee on Taxation (Joint
Committee staff) develop estimates of the budgetary and economic effects
of legislation. The agencies have collaborated on the responses to your
questions. Joint Committee staff took the lead in responding to the first
three questions, and CBO drafted the responses to the last two questions.
Question 1. How do you define a static score, a conventional score, and a
dynamic score?
CBO and Joint Committee staff provide the Members of Congress with
estimates of the changes in future outlays and receipts that would result
from proposed changes in federal legislation. Neither CBO nor Joint
Committee staff produces what are generally understood to be “static
estimates.” Every estimate of the effects of legislation prepared by CBO
and Joint Committee staff includes potential behavioral changes by
individuals and business entities in response to the changes in the economic
environment that would be created by the proposed legislation.

Our understanding is that when people refer to a “static estimate,” they
believe that the estimate accounts for absolutely no possible changes in
behavior by either individuals or business entities in response to the
changes in the economic environment that would be created by the
proposed legislation.
Aside from when House Rule XIII requires otherwise, CBO and Joint
Committee staff provide the Members of Congress with “conventional
estimates” of proposed changes in legislation. A conventional estimate
compares projected federal direct spending or receipts under the proposal
with projected outlays or receipts under present law—that is, compared
with the direct spending and receipts projected under the baseline that CBO
prepares in compliance with provisions of the Deficit Control Act. All
conventional estimates include potential behavioral changes by individuals
and business entities in response to the changes in the economic
environment that would be created by the proposed legislation, but they do
not assume that such changes would alter the size of the U.S. economy as
projected in CBO’s macroeconomic baseline. In short, conventional
estimates prepared by CBO and Joint Committee staff are not “static”; they
reflect myriad microeconomic behavioral responses but do not reflect
possible macroeconomic changes.
House Rule XIII requires that in certain circumstances, CBO and Joint
Committee staff include macroeconomic behavioral changes in addition to
the behavioral responses reflected in conventional estimates. The estimates
that include those macroeconomic behavioral changes are commonly
referred to as dynamic and are discussed in more detail below.
Question 1a. What factors do you generally consider in a conventional
score that are not included in a static score?
CBO’s and Joint Committee staff’s models generally assume that
individuals and business entities behave rationally and incorporate other
behaviors as indicated by data and recent research. Behaviors in
conventional estimates include the following:
• Changes in the timing of transactions and income recognition;
• Changes among business sectors or the legal form of doing business;
• Changes in types of portfolio investments;
• Changes in the form in which an employee receives compensation;

• Changes in the amount, types, and timing of consumption; and
• Tax planning and tax avoidance strategies.1
Question 1b. Does your conventional analysis always hold GDP fixed?
Generally, a conventional estimate prepared by CBO or Joint Committee
staff does not incorporate a change in the size of the U.S. economy as
projected in the CBO baseline of gross national product (GNP). However,
if the legislation would significantly change the population of the United
States, then the agencies allow for the size of the economy to change by
estimating the income of the affected portion of the population and
accounting for the budgetary effects of benefits provided to and taxes paid
by those people.
Question 1c. What factors in a dynamic score would you generally
consider not present in a conventional score?
Macroeconomic analyses prepared by CBO or Joint Committee staff
generally include changes in aggregate labor supply, changes in aggregate
domestic investment, total factor productivity, changes in aggregate
household saving rates and incomes, national saving, and changes in
interest rates, wages, and prices.2

Increases in explicit government debt due to a policy proposal can direct
savings away from private investment toward federal debt service and
increase the interest rate that the Federal government has to pay to attract
additional funds to finance the budget deficit. The extent to which an
increase in government debt can ultimately “crowd out” private investment
depends on how people in the United States alter their savings behavior in
response to such a change in economic conditions, as well as the response
of foreign investors and monetary policy. Each of the three macroeconomic
models used by Joint Committee staff accounts for domestic and foreign
responses to increases in government debt. The models also incorporate
different assumptions about how individuals make their saving decisions.
(Those assumptions include expectations about the future and saver versus
non-saver households.) For a given policy change, the magnitude of the
crowding-out effect can therefore differ across models and ultimately
depends on the specific policy under analysis.
In CBO’s assessment, increases in the federal deficit reduce private
investment. Larger deficits boost aggregate demand and reduce national
saving, which in turn raises interest rates and crowds out private
investment. The extent to which a policy would affect aggregate demand
and national saving depends on its specific details.
Implicit government debt from Social Security and Medicare obligations is
relevant for saving behavior in macroeconomic models in which people are
assumed to anticipate future changes to federal benefits. In such models,
there is a distinction between a payable benefits scenario, in which future
benefits are limited to contemporaneous funding after trust fund reserve
depletion, and a scheduled benefits scenario, in which future benefits are
those specified under current law without regard to the trust fund
balances. A payable benefits scenario requires households to forecast an
indeterminate reduction in their Social Security benefits at some time in the
future. For consistency across all models with varying degrees of individual
foresight, Joint Committee staff assumes that scheduled benefits will be
paid and that the government’s budget is unified. CBO uses a suite of
models, including its overlapping generations model, to analyze the effects
of changes in debt related to Social Security and other policies.3

Question 3. Provide information about processes for reconciling
differences in static and dynamic scoring approaches between the CBO and
JCT, especially on legislation with both revenue and expenditure
implications.
When proposed legislation involves policy changes that could alter
individuals’ or business entities’ decisions in ways that would affect federal
outlays as well as similar or related policy changes that alter individuals’ or
business entities’ decisions in ways that would affect federal tax receipts,
CBO and Joint Committee staff collaborate. Sometimes that collaboration
involves specific joint modeling exercises; such is the case when the two
agencies provide estimates of changes in health policy. Other times, the
collaboration involves using the estimates developed by one agency in the
estimates of the other agency. For example, in assessing certain proposals
that would change legal immigration and improve tax compliance, Joint
Committee staff uses the output of CBO’s immigration analysis as the basis
for its estimates of the policy’s tax provisions. Likewise, CBO uses Joint
Committee staff’s estimates of the income and payroll tax offset for its
analysis of a proposed new fee. In still other situations, the collaboration
involves conversations to ensure that CBO and Joint Committee staff
incorporate comparable behavioral response parameters when providing
estimates to Members who may be weighing options to achieve a policy
goal through an outlay program or through a tax expenditure.
CBO’s macroeconomic models focus on the details of federal outlays and
incorporate tax changes more generally. By contrast, Joint Committee
staff’s macroeconomic models focus on the details of the Internal Revenue
Code and incorporate federal outlays more generally. The two agencies
have limited experience conducting collaborative analysis of the effects of
macroeconomic changes, as required under House Rule XIII, of legislation
that involves significant changes in direct spending programs and
significant changes in the Internal Revenue Code.4 In such situations, the
agencies would probably separately estimate macroeconomic changes using
existing models and then discuss the results to determine how to report the
macroeconomic results to the House of Representatives to fulfill the
requirement of House Rule XIII.

Analyses from CBO and Joint Committee staff are used by the Congress in
several ways. The term “score” typically refers to cost estimates provided
to the Budget Committees for budget enforcement purposes.5 Those
advisory estimates provide information that the Congress can use as it
implements rules and procedures when considering legislation that would
affect the federal budget. Those rules and procedures are determined and
enforced by the Budget Committees.
There are three significant ways in which the cost estimate for a bill might
differ from the full budgetary consequences reflected in CBO’s economic
and budgetary baseline after enactment.6 First, CBO and Joint Committee
staff may be directed not to include certain budgetary effects when
preparing a cost estimate to be used for budget enforcement. If the bill is
enacted, however, the effects that were excluded from the estimate are
reflected in the baseline. Second, unless the Congress requires or requests
otherwise, cost estimates typically reflect the expectation that the size of the
economy remains unchanged. The budgetary effects of the bill incorporated
in the baseline reflect the effects of the bill on the size of the economy and
on other macroeconomic variables. Finally, cost estimates are made relative
to the scoring baseline. If the effects of the bill interact with the economic
and legislative changes that have occurred since the scoring baseline was
set, the total effect reflected in subsequent baselines will differ from the
amount reported in the cost estimate. Two recent reconciliation acts provide
concrete examples of those differences.
In the case of the 2022 reconciliation act (Public Law 117-169), all three of
the reasons described above contributed to differences between the cost
estimate for the act and the effects reflected in subsequent baselines
produced by CBO. (Joint Committee staff provided the estimates for
changes to tax policy enacted in the 2022 reconciliation act. By statute,
Joint Committee staff provides revenue estimates for legislation that would
change the Internal Revenue Code.)

Question 5. Increasing domestic energy production can reduce U.S. energy
prices, which would significantly impact every sector of the U.S. economy.
Please provide a detailed description of the model and methodology you
use to dynamically score the effects of changes in domestic energy
production on the U.S. economy and gross domestic product. What
assumptions underlie the model, what input variables does your model
consider, how are they weighted, and which inputs have the greatest
potential to significantly change the model's output?
To assess the dynamic effects of policies intended to increase energy
production—that is, the effects that those policies would be expected to
have on the nation’s gross domestic product (GDP)—CBO extended a
model it previously constructed to evaluate the economic effects of
producing oil and natural gas from shale resources.11 The extended model
accounts for additional short-run and long-run effects that stem from
proposed policies aimed at increasing energy production. To illustrate the
model’s framework, CBO assessed the expected economic effects of two
alternative scenarios. The first scenario is based on the Energy Information
Administration’s (EIA’s) High Oil and Gas Supply (HOGS) case examined
as part of EIA’s most recent Annual Energy Outlook.12 Under that scenario,
the production of oil and other liquid fuels increases by almost 30 percent
in some years over the 2025–2035 period and natural gas production, by
10 percent. The second scenario is based on a proposed policy to expand
offshore lease sales of oil and natural gas in which production increases by
smaller amounts.13

scenario would increase GDP by about $10 billion and would reduce the

second scenario would result in smaller increases in GDP, in part because
the changes in energy supplies are much smaller, but also because
significant changes in energy production take time. (The EIA scenario
assumed that the increases occurred rapidly.) In both scenarios, the
increases in GDP stem from an increase in energy production only. CBO’s
estimates do not incorporate other provisions of any future bills that might
interact with those changes in energy production.
Analytical Method. CBO’s approach to evaluating the effect of changes in
domestic oil and natural gas production on GDP is made up of three
components. The first reflects the primary investment effects and measures
the changes in aggregate demand and changes in the capital stock
attributable to increased investment in the oil and natural gas sector. The
second component reflects the productivity effects. It measures how the
increased supply of oil and natural gas affects the productivity of labor and
capital and how it raises GDP over the longer term as labor and capital are
allocated to their more efficient uses—in particular, to energy production.
The final component reflects the effects of an increased supply of labor and
higher savings, which boost output in the longer term. Following the
agency’s standard method for incorporating longer-term economic effects,
CBO phases in part of the primary investment effect (the increase of the
capital stock), the productivity effect, and the increase in labor and savings
over four years. The short-run change in aggregate demand (part of the
primary investment effect) phases out over the same period.
Primary Investment Effects. Initial investments in the extraction of new oil
and natural gas resources directly stimulate the economy and boost GDP in
the short term. In CBO’s estimates, the additional investment in oil and
natural gas extraction is the amount of investment that would be needed to
produce a given increase in oil and natural gas production if the supply of
oil and natural gas rose (whether because of a policy change or some other
development). Such investment contributes to the total amount of capital in
the economy—boosting the ratio of capital to labor each year and
increasing GDP in the long term. That increase in investment would crowd
out (or reduce) investment in other sectors of the economy, partly offsetting
the investment in oil and natural gas. In the first scenario, CBO preserved
EIA’s assumption that oil and natural gas production would quickly rise

and modeled that the additional investment would take place quickly
enough to generate the large increase in production in the first year. When
evaluating a proposed policy change, however, CBO would develop its
estimates using information about the time between project development
and the start of production. For example, in the second scenario, CBO
estimated that production would begin about five years after new leases
would be put up for auction.
Productivity Effects. The main productivity effect stems from the increased
productivity of labor and capital resources used to produce the new oil and
natural gas resources, measured as the market price of those supplies under
the policy minus the average breakeven price of those supplies. Smaller
productivity effects stem from gains from reallocating resources from the
more costly oil and natural gas supplies produced to the new, less costly
supplies and the productivity gains associated with lower prices of oil and
natural gas for consumers and businesses.14
Increased Supply of Labor and Greater Savings Stemming From
Productivity Effects. As productivity rises, households have more income to
save and invest. That investment boosts the economy’s productive capacity
and GDP. Higher productivity also boosts wages, encouraging people to
work more and also boosting GDP.
Many factors determine the magnitude of the three components, such as the
amount of additional energy produced because of the policy, how energy
prices are affected, and how sensitive energy use is to changes in those
prices. But how quickly investment responds to the new policy determines
the sizes of those three effects over the 2025–2035 period. If long lead
times were required before additional capital investment could occur, the
effects of a policy on GDP would generally happen later, possibly outside
the projection period. Conversely, if investment responded more quickly,
the short-run increase in demand and the longer-run increases in the capital
stock and change in productivity would provide larger effects on GDP
during the projection period.
