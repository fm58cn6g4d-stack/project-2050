## PREFATORY NOTE: THE HARDEST POLITICAL CHALLENGE IN THE FRAMEWORK

This chapter is different from every other chapter in Project 2029 in one important respect: the primary governing tool — federal legislation protecting a right to abortion — faces a Supreme Court that already ruled Congress and the states can do whatever they want on abortion, and may get worse before it gets better.

That constitutional reality does not mean Democrats are helpless. It means the strategy must be more sophisticated than "pass the Women's Health Protection Act and declare victory." The full strategy involves five simultaneous tracks: federal legislation (for what is achievable), executive action (for immediate protection), state partnership (the primary near-term battlefield), constitutional amendment (the long-horizon goal), and Comstock defense (the most urgent and least discussed threat). Each track is developed in detail below.

A second distinctive feature: reproductive rights is the issue where political positioning and governing substance most directly reinforce each other. Democrats have won 12 consecutive abortion-related ballot measures since Dobbs (2022). The issue is a proven electoral winner — but only when framed correctly and prosecuted aggressively. Timidity on reproductive rights does not produce moderate voters; it produces low turnout among the young and college-educated women who are the most reliable Democratic constituency on this issue. The chapter is designed to be both politically aggressive and governmentally serious.

## PART I: THE LANDSCAPE — WHERE WE ARE IN MARCH 2026

### I.1 The State-Level Patchwork

The post-Dobbs landscape as of March 2026 has stabilized into a deeply unequal geography:

**Near-total ban states (12):** Alabama, Arkansas, Idaho, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, North Dakota, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, West Virginia — all with criminal penalties for providers; most with no meaningful exceptions for rape, incest, or fetal anomaly; several with "bounty hunter" enforcement mechanisms

**Gestational limit states:** Georgia (6 weeks, before most women know they are pregnant), Florida (6 weeks, after voters rejected a 6-week ban in 2024 but courts allowed the existing law to stand), South Carolina (6 weeks), Iowa, Nebraska (12 weeks)

**Protection states (24+ states):** Including California, New York, Illinois, Colorado, Vermont, Minnesota, and others where abortion is protected either by statute, state supreme court interpretation, or state constitutional amendment

**Shield law states (8):** California, New York, Massachusetts, Colorado, Illinois, Washington, Connecticut, New Jersey — with varying degrees of protection for providers who prescribe remotely to patients in ban states

**The medication abortion reality:** As of July 2025, 25 states allowed telemedical abortions without restriction, 13 states allowed abortion but had restrictions on telemedical abortions, and 12 states banned abortion. In December 2024, 27% of clinician-assisted abortions were provided through telemedicine — a share that has continued to grow as shield-state providers expand telehealth services.

**The 2026 ballot measure landscape:** Abortion rights advocates in Nevada and Virginia have placed a constitutional amendment protecting the right to abortion on the November ballot. The Missouri legislature has placed an initiative on the ballot to repeal a state constitutional amendment protecting the right to abortion approved by voters in 2024. Idaho and Nebraska may also have measures on the ballot. Twelve states have passed ballot initiatives since 2022, usually to protect abortion rights.

### I.2 The Human Cost — Named and Documented

The political debate about abortion rights has obscured a public health catastrophe unfolding in real time. The chapter begins with names, not abstractions:

**Adriana Smith**, a 30-year-old Black mother and nurse from Atlanta, was kept on life support for more than 90 days — against her family's wishes, and long after being declared brain dead — because of Georgia's extreme abortion ban and so-called fetal personhood ideology.

**Tierra Walker**, a 37-year-old Black mother from San Antonio, died from preeclampsia after being denied an abortion during a high-risk pregnancy — despite repeatedly asking for care — under Texas' extreme abortion ban.

The consequences of abortion bans became even more visible in 2025 as investigative reporting documented more heartbreaking and preventable deaths of pregnant people denied care. Maternal mortality rates are on the rise in states with abortion bans, yet those same states are making it harder to investigate by obfuscating and suppressing data.

These are not edge cases. They are predictable, documented consequences of laws that substitute legislative mandates for physician judgment in the most complex and high-stakes medical decisions that exist. The Democratic governing agenda must treat this as the public health emergency it is.

### I.3 The Comstock Threat — The Most Underreported Danger

Federal officials could undermine state abortion rights using the Comstock Act, a 19th-century obscenity law that anti-abortion leaders have framed as a de facto ban on mailing any abortion-related drugs or paraphernalia. The abortion provisions of Project 2025 focus on the Comstock Act, and prominent anti-abortion groups have pushed Comstock strategies. This leaves open the possibility of a federal Comstock prosecution against a high-profile defendant, like drug companies or Planned Parenthood.

Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas have already signaled their support for anti-abortion interpretations of the Comstock Act, and their conservative colleagues might share this view.

**What this means in practice:** If the Trump DOJ decides to enforce the Comstock Act as a de facto national abortion ban — prohibiting the mailing of mifepristone and other abortion medications — it could effectively eliminate medication abortion access even in states where abortion is legal, without passing a single new law. This is the most dangerous near-term threat to reproductive rights that Democrats have largely failed to communicate.

**The Florida/Texas mifepristone lawsuit:** In December 2025, the states of Florida and Texas filed a lawsuit against the FDA challenging its approval of mifepristone in 2000 and approval of a generic in 2025. This is a direct attack on the drug's federal approval — if successful, it would eliminate medication abortion nationwide regardless of state law.

### I.4 The Interstate Conflict Escalation

Escalating interstate legal conflicts and congressional efforts to preempt shield laws are expected as the GOP continues to pursue a national abortion ban. Copy-cat legislation as anti-abortion lawmakers seek to replicate Texas's HB 7, the new bounty-hunter ban targeting manufacturers, distributors, and providers of medication abortion.

Providers in California and New York have faced both criminal and civil cases from states hostile to abortion. Both states used their shield laws to protect providers from extradition and severe civil penalties.

The legal framework governing interstate abortion conflicts is genuinely unsettled. Can Texas prosecute a California doctor who prescribes medication abortion remotely to a Texas patient? Can federal courts enforce a judgment from a ban state against a provider in a shield state? These questions are working their way through federal courts with no clear resolution yet.

### I.5 The IVF Dimension

IVF was a key consideration for several states in 2025, in response to an Alabama court case (LePage v. Center for Reproductive Medicine), which found that all unborn children, regardless of their location, are considered children under the state's Wrongful Death Act. In 2025, Tennessee, Georgia, Louisiana, and Nevada enacted legislation protecting access to IVF.

The fetal personhood logic underlying many abortion bans threatens IVF directly — because IVF creates embryos that are not implanted and may be discarded. This is the reproductive rights issue that reaches deepest into the demographic groups Democrats need to persuade: married couples and women over 35 who have used or considered IVF are not part of the "abortion rights" coalition's stereotypical image but have direct, personal stakes in the fetal personhood question.

## PART II: THE POLLING REALITY — WHERE THE PUBLIC STANDS

About three-quarters of White evangelical Protestants (74%) think abortion should be illegal in all or most cases. By contrast, 82% of religiously unaffiliated Americans say abortion should be legal in all or most cases, as do 68% of Black Protestants, 63% of White nonevangelical Protestants and 57% of Catholics.

Upward of six-in-ten women (64%) and 55% of men say abortion should be legal in all or most cases.

Among Republicans and independents who lean toward the Republican Party, 63% say abortion should be illegal in all or most cases. By contrast, 84% of Democrats and Democratic leaners say abortion should be legal in all or most cases.

Among moderate and liberal Republicans, 53% say abortion should be legal.

**The electoral math:** Supermajority public support for legal abortion exists in virtually every competitive state — but the intensity of opposition among White evangelical Protestants (who are the most reliable Republican voters) is enormous. The political challenge is not persuading the median voter — who already agrees that abortion should be legal — but translating that supermajority support into sufficient political salience to overcome the intensity advantage of the anti-abortion movement.

**The IVF supermajority:** Support for IVF access polls even higher than support for abortion access — above 80% nationally, including among Republicans. This is the issue that best bridges the abortion-rights constituency with persuadable voters who are uncomfortable with the "abortion" framing.

**The ballot measure record:** Since Dobbs in June 2022, abortion rights have won on the ballot in every state where it appeared, including in red states like Kansas (retained protections by 18 points), Kentucky (two separate measures both failed to restrict), Ohio (enshrined right by 14 points), Montana, and Missouri (2024). This record — 12 wins in a row — is the most reliable indicator that the public, given a direct vote, supports reproductive rights far more than standard partisan elections suggest.

## PART III: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

### III.1 The Core Principle

**The government has no business making medical decisions that belong to a patient and their doctor.** Full stop. This is the framing that wins with the broadest possible coalition — it invokes medical authority, patient autonomy, privacy, and anti-government-overreach values simultaneously. It is more powerful than "a woman's right to choose" (which triggers the "what about the baby's rights?" counter) and more specific than "reproductive freedom" (which is abstract).

**Bodily autonomy is foundational.** A government that can force a woman to remain pregnant against her will — and can force a brain-dead woman to remain on life support against her family's wishes — has asserted a claim on bodily autonomy that has no logical stopping point. The same principle that allows the state to criminalize abortion allows it to mandate vaccines, compel medical procedures, or imprison patients for medical decisions. This is a libertarian and conservative argument as much as a liberal one — and it should be made to those audiences.

**Healthcare, not ideology.** The governing agenda treats abortion as healthcare — because it is healthcare. Medication abortion, emergency contraception, miscarriage management, ectopic pregnancy treatment, and pregnancy termination are all medical procedures regulated by the FDA, governed by evidence-based medical standards, and appropriately decided between patients and their physicians. A government that removes physicians from these decisions and substitutes criminal law is a government that has made a theological choice and imposed it on citizens who do not share that theology.

### III.2 What the Framework Commits To

This framework commits to a specific, multi-track strategy to protect and restore reproductive rights:

1. **Immediate executive protection** of what can be protected by executive action
2. **Federal legislation** at the maximum achievable scope given the Senate math
3. **Mifepristone and medication abortion protection** through FDA defense and Comstock neutralization
4. **State ballot measure strategy** as the primary near-term expansion pathway
5. **Shield law federal codification** to protect interstate care access
6. **IVF federal protection** as the bipartisan bridge
7. **Comstock Act neutralization** as the most urgent defensive priority
8. **Constitutional amendment** as the long-horizon goal

## PART V: THE LEGISLATIVE AGENDA

### V.1 The Women's Health Protection Act 2.0

**Pathway:** Filibuster carve-out (requires 51 votes to change rules, then 51 to pass) OR reconciliation for budget-adjacent provisions. This is one of the clearest cases for filibuster carve-out — the 14th Amendment's equal protection clause provides constitutional grounding for federal reproductive rights protection.

**The constitutional argument for WHPA:**
The Supreme Court in Dobbs held that abortion is not a fundamental right under the 14th Amendment's due process clause. It did not hold that Congress cannot protect it under other constitutional authority. Two potential constitutional foundations:
1. **14th Amendment Section 5 (congressional enforcement):** Congress has broad authority to enforce the equal protection and due process clauses through legislation; forced pregnancy that falls disproportionately on women raises serious equal protection concerns that Congress can address
2. **Commerce Clause:** Abortion services and medications involve interstate commerce (drug manufacturing, interstate travel for care, telehealth); Congress has broad Commerce Clause authority to regulate

The legislation should be drafted to invoke both grounds, building the strongest possible constitutional record for judicial defense.

**Core provisions:**
- Establish a statutory right to abortion in states that allow it, through the point of fetal viability (approximately 22-24 weeks of gestation)
- After viability: protect abortion where necessary to protect the life or health of the pregnant person (physician's judgment)
- Prohibit specific burdens: mandatory delays, mandatory ultrasounds without clinical indication, targeted regulation of abortion providers that imposes requirements not applied to comparable outpatient procedures
- Prohibit civil or criminal liability for any person who performs, assists, or receives abortion services that are legal in the state where performed
- Create a private right of action: any person harmed by a violation may sue for injunctive relief and damages

**The scope limitation:** The WHPA 2.0 does not nationalize abortion law. It establishes federal statutory protections that apply in states that choose to allow abortion. It does not override state bans — that would require a constitutional amendment or a Supreme Court reversal. It protects access in protective states, and provides the strongest possible argument for federal jurisdiction that courts must grapple with.

**Political design note:** This version of the WHPA is deliberately scoped to what can be defended in court given current doctrine. A broader bill that attempts to affirmatively override all state bans would almost certainly be enjoined immediately and struck down by the current Supreme Court — which would be worse than no bill, because it would establish precedent. The goal is to pass something that survives and protects access where it exists, while the longer-term strategy (amendment, court reform) builds toward broader protection.

### V.2 The Protecting Access to Medication Abortion Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (FDA funding and authorization are budgetary) AND regular order for the statutory components

**Core provisions:**
- Codify FDA approval of mifepristone and misoprostol for medication abortion in statute — removing FDA approval from executive branch discretion and protecting it against both executive and judicial attack
- Neutralize the Comstock Act: statutory clarification that the Comstock Act does not apply to FDA-approved medications mailed for lawful purposes; alternatively, explicitly repeal the abortion-specific provisions of the Comstock Act (18 U.S.C. § 1461-1462)
- Prohibit FDA from withdrawing approval of mifepristone or misoprostol on political grounds; require any withdrawal be based on clinical evidence reviewed by an independent scientific panel
- Fund telehealth infrastructure for medication abortion: HHS grants to states to support telehealth abortion care within legal states
- Prohibit federal interference with pharmacies dispensing FDA-approved medications under applicable state law

**The Comstock repeal is the single highest-priority legislative action in the reproductive rights chapter.** It is the only provision that, if not addressed, could effectively ban medication abortion nationally without any new legislation — simply through a DOJ enforcement decision by the next hostile administration.

### V.3 The Freedom to Travel for Healthcare Act

**Pathway:** Regular order (Commerce Clause authority; potentially bipartisan)

**Core provisions:**
- Prohibit any state from imposing civil or criminal liability on a person who travels to another state to obtain a medical procedure that is legal in the destination state
- Prohibit any state from imposing liability on any person who provides transportation, lodging, or financial assistance to another person traveling for legal healthcare
- Create a federal civil rights claim: any person subjected to prosecution or civil liability for interstate healthcare travel may bring suit in federal court
- Provide DOJ authority to bring injunctive actions against state laws that violate this Act
- This Act has potential bipartisan support (medical tourism is a general interest; states like Florida have residents who travel to other states for various medical procedures); frame as "healthcare freedom to travel"

**The Texas HB 7 problem:** Texas has passed and other states are considering laws that impose liability on people who help patients travel for abortion care, including targeting manufacturers and distributors of medication abortion. The Freedom to Travel Act directly preempts these laws under the Commerce Clause.

### V.4 The IVF Protection Act

**Pathway:** Regular order (strong bipartisan potential — 8+ Republican senators have expressed support for IVF protection)

**Core provisions:**
- Establish a federal right to access IVF and other fertility treatments
- Prohibit any state from imposing criminal or civil liability on IVF providers for the creation, storage, or disposal of embryos that occurs as part of normal clinical practice
- Protect patients from prosecution for the use of IVF services
- Preempt any state fetal personhood law as applied to in vitro embryos
- Restore and expand coverage: require insurance coverage of IVF for plans subject to ACA; expand coverage in Medicaid
- Fund fertility treatment access for service members and veterans who experienced service-related infertility

**Political design:** The IVF bill is the bipartisan opener. It passes first, with 60+ Senate votes, demonstrating that reproductive rights legislation can succeed. It also pulls the middle ground out from under Republicans' feet — they cannot support "fetal personhood" laws and also claim to support IVF. Forcing that contradiction is itself a political win. Every Republican senator who votes against IVF protection is voting against something that 80%+ of their constituents support.

### V.5 The Maternal Health Emergency Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (healthcare spending) and regular order (regulatory provisions)

**Core provisions:**
- Declare a maternal mortality emergency: the United States has the highest maternal mortality rate of any wealthy nation, and rates have increased in states with abortion bans
- Emergency funding for maternal health: $5B over 5 years for maternal health centers, obstetric units in rural hospitals, midwifery training, and postpartum care in underserved communities
- Data transparency: prohibit states from suppressing or withholding maternal mortality data; require uniform CDC reporting standards; reinstate and expand maternal mortality review committees in every state
- EMTALA enforcement: the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act requires emergency stabilization care; the Biden administration interpreted EMTALA to require emergency abortion services in states with bans; codify this interpretation in statute and fund robust enforcement
- Momnibus provisions: fully fund the Mamas First Act, the Perinatal Workforce Act, and the maternal mental health provisions of the Momnibus — programs that had strong bipartisan support before being stalled by Republican leadership

## PART VI: DAY ONE EXECUTIVE ACTIONS

**Mifepristone defense:**
- EO: Direct DOJ to formally withdraw any pending Comstock Act enforcement actions or guidance related to abortion medications; issue a DOJ opinion that the Comstock Act does not prohibit mailing of FDA-approved medications for lawful purposes
- EO: Direct FDA to defend its mifepristone approval vigorously in all pending litigation; direct FDA to resist any political pressure to withdraw or restrict mifepristone approval
- EO: Direct HHS to restore the Biden-era EMTALA guidance requiring emergency stabilization including abortion services in emergency situations; defend this interpretation in court

**Healthcare and access:**
- EO: Restore full Title X family planning funding; restore Planned Parenthood and other comprehensive reproductive healthcare providers to Title X funding eligibility
- EO: Direct CMS to restore Medicaid coverage for all family planning services and providers; require states to cover contraception and family planning with no cost-sharing
- EO: Direct HHS to restore all contraceptive coverage requirements under the ACA; close the employer "conscience exemption" loophole that has allowed employers to deny contraceptive coverage to employees
- EO: Restore and expand international family planning funding (the "Mexico City Policy" / Global Gag Rule, which Trump reinstated, should be permanently rescinded; pursue statutory codification)

**Maternal health:**
- EO: Declare a maternal mortality public health emergency; direct HHS to convene an emergency maternal health task force within 30 days
- EO: Direct CDC to restore suppressed maternal mortality data; require states to report to CDC on standardized timeline; reconstitute the CDC maternal mortality surveillance program
- EO: Direct DOJ to investigate whether states' suppression of maternal mortality data constitutes obstruction of federal public health functions

**Federal employee protections:**
- EO: Restore and expand federal employee health plan coverage for reproductive healthcare including abortion services, with travel and lodging coverage for care obtained in a legal state
- EO: Protect federal employees and contractors from prosecution for exercising legal rights in their state of residence or travel

## PART VII: THE STATE STRATEGY

### VII.1 The Ballot Measure as Primary Near-Term Tool

The most reliable mechanism for restoring and protecting reproductive rights in the near-to-medium term is the state constitutional amendment ballot measure. The record since Dobbs is perfect: 12 for 12 in ballot measure wins for reproductive rights, including in red states.

**The 2026 priorities:**
- **Nevada**: Nevada requires citizen-initiated constitutional amendments to be approved in two consecutive elections; voters will consider this measure for the second time in 2026, which would enshrine it permanently. This is a must-win.
- **Virginia**: Voters will consider a constitutional amendment protecting "the right to make and effectuate one's own decisions about all matters related to one's pregnancy" — a critical swing-state win that would also protect a Democratic governor's record
- **Missouri**: The Missouri legislature has placed an initiative on the ballot to repeal the 2024 constitutional amendment protecting abortion rights. Defending this is a critical test — Republicans are attempting to overturn voter-enacted rights through a misleadingly drafted counter-measure
- **Idaho and Nebraska**: Support ballot measure campaigns in both states if they qualify

**The 2028 priorities:** Ballot measure campaigns in Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, Arizona, Wisconsin, and Michigan — states with competitive presidential races where a reproductive rights measure on the ballot simultaneously motivates base turnout and attracts persuadable voters

### VII.2 The Shield Law Federal Codification

The patchwork of state shield laws protects providers in California and New York from Texas and Louisiana prosecution — but with significant gaps. The federal Freedom to Travel Act (Section V.3) provides the uniform federal floor. In the interim, the Democratic administration should:

- DOJ: Formally decline to provide any assistance to states seeking to enforce anti-abortion laws extraterritorially against providers in other states
- State Department: Issue guidance that the U.S. government will not extradite individuals sought by states for providing legal healthcare in another state
- Fund shield law coordination: HHS grants to states to build out their shield law infrastructure, provider identity protection, and legal defense funds

### VII.3 Federal-State Partnership for Access Expansion

In states with protective laws, the federal government should actively expand access:
- Fund abortion provider training in medical schools and residency programs — the provider shortage is the greatest access barrier in many states even where abortion is legal
- Fund transportation assistance for patients who must travel within large states to reach a provider
- Fund comprehensive sex education that reduces unintended pregnancy (prevention is always better than crisis care)
- Fund crisis pregnancy center accountability: CPC deception (advertising as medical clinics while providing no medical care) causes measurable harm; fund state enforcement of consumer protection laws against deceptive practices

## PART VIII: THE CONSTITUTIONAL LONG GAME

### VIII.1 The Amendment Path

A constitutional amendment protecting reproductive rights is the only mechanism that permanently places this right beyond the reach of SCOTUS, Congress, or any state legislature. It requires:
- **2/3 majority in both chambers of Congress** — currently requires approximately 67 Senate votes and 290 House votes; possible in a large Democratic trifecta or with significant Republican defection
- **Ratification by 38 states** — currently, 15 states have explicit anti-abortion constitutional provisions; achieving 38 ratifying states requires winning in at minimum some states that currently restrict abortion

**The realistic timeline:** A constitutional amendment protecting reproductive rights is not achievable in the 2029-2033 term under realistic political conditions. It is a decade-long project beginning with: state ballot measure wins that build momentum; legislative wins at the federal level that establish the political norm; political realignment that makes Republican opposition more costly; and ultimately a political moment where 2/3 majorities exist in Congress and 38 ratifying states can be assembled.

**Why it must begin now:** Every state constitutional amendment that protects reproductive rights is a step toward the 38 ratification threshold. Every ballot measure win shifts the political terrain. Every federal legislative win normalizes the right at the statutory level. The amendment project begins on Day 1 not because it will finish this term, but because it never finishes if it never begins.

**Amendment language (proposed):**
> "The right of a person to make and effectuate decisions about their own reproductive health, including the decision to continue or to terminate a pregnancy, shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State. Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."

### VIII.2 The Supreme Court Path

The current Supreme Court's 6-3 conservative supermajority is the structural obstacle to federal reproductive rights legislation surviving judicial challenge. The democracy chapter (Chapter 6) addresses Supreme Court reform — term limits, ethics code, and potential expansion. All of these affect the reproductive rights agenda:

- **18-year term limits (statute):** Would rotate the Court's composition naturally; with two new appointments per presidential term, a 2029 Democratic president makes two appointments in the first term
- **Court expansion (legislation, harder):** Adding Justices would shift the balance; this is the most controversial option and should be pursued only when political conditions support it
- **The constitutional argument for WHPA:** Building the strongest possible legislative record for equal protection and Commerce Clause arguments is essential; the goal is to give a future, more balanced Court the maximum opportunity to uphold federal reproductive rights legislation

## PART IX: THE COALITION MANAGEMENT CHALLENGE

### IX.1 The Internal Tension

The reproductive rights coalition contains a genuine internal tension:
- **Absolutist position (majority of the organized reproductive rights movement):** No gestational limits; no exceptions conditions; full funding including Medicaid; framing as "abortion on demand without apology"
- **Pragmatic position (most competitive-district candidates):** Viability limit with health exceptions; parental notification with judicial bypass; Medicaid funding; "safe, legal, and rare" framing
- **Coalition members with cross-pressures:** Catholics (57% support legal abortion but many uncomfortable with the issue's prominence); rural voters (economic populism voters who support abortion access but don't want it as the party's dominant identity)

**The framework's position:** The legislative agenda (WHPA 2.0 with viability framework) reflects the pragmatic position because it is both more judicially defensible and more politically durable. Candidates in deep-blue districts can run on broader ground; candidates in competitive districts should lead with the medical-authority frame and the IVF/maternal mortality angle rather than the elective abortion frame. The underlying policy — abortion is a medical decision between patient and doctor — is consistent across both approaches.

### IX.2 The "What to Say" Guide for Candidates

**The safest universal answer:**
> "I trust women and their doctors to make the best decisions for their own health. That means I support access to abortion care, access to IVF, access to contraception, and good prenatal and maternal healthcare. What I don't support is a politician — or a criminal court — substituting their judgment for a doctor's."

**When pushed on gestational limits:**
> "I support viability as the standard — the point at which a fetus could potentially survive outside the womb — with exceptions for the life and health of the mother when her doctor determines it's necessary. After viability, those decisions are medical emergencies that belong with the patient and their physician."

**When pushed on taxpayer funding:**
> "No one should be denied medical care because of their income. I support Medicaid coverage for abortion care because healthcare is healthcare."

**When pushed on parental notification:**
> "I support minors having access to confidential healthcare, including judicial bypass for young people in unsafe home situations. The courts, not politicians, are the right mechanism for handling difficult individual cases."

**When accused of supporting "abortion up to birth":**
> "That phrase describes a medical emergency where a wanted pregnancy is ending because something has gone terribly wrong — and only a doctor with the patient in front of them can make that call. The idea that politicians should be making that decision while a woman is dying is not a pro-life position. It's cruelty."
