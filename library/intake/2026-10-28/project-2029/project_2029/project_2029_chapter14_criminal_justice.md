## PREFATORY NOTE: THE POLITICAL TRAP AND HOW TO ESCAPE IT

Criminal justice is the policy area where Democrats have been most successfully manipulated into positions that are both politically weak and substantively wrong. The "defund the police" moment of 2020 — whatever its original intent — was used to devastating electoral effect for years afterward. The implicit message it sent to voters, including voters of color who disproportionately live in high-crime communities and want more effective policing, was that the Democratic Party cared more about ideological commitments than about their safety.

This chapter rejects that framing entirely. It proceeds from a single, defensible, empirically grounded principle: **the goal of criminal justice policy is public safety, and the current system is failing at it.**

- A 43% federal recidivism rate is not a success. It is a catastrophically expensive failure.
- Mandatory minimums that fill prisons with low-level drug offenders at $43,000/person/year while doing nothing to reduce drug use are not tough. They are wasteful.
- A system that has produced a 5-to-1 racial disparity in incarceration rates, with no corresponding public safety benefit, is not just. It is a documented failure that costs communities and corrodes legitimacy.
- Police forces that operate without accountability, transparency, or meaningful oversight are not effective law enforcement tools. They are liability generators and community destroyers.

The Democratic position is not "less law enforcement." It is **better law enforcement, smarter incarceration, genuine rehabilitation, and accountability systems that make the whole enterprise more effective and more legitimate.**

This chapter also takes seriously the bipartisan opportunity. Criminal justice reform is the policy area with the broadest genuine conservative partnership available. The First Step Act passed the Senate 87-12. The Koch brothers and the ACLU have co-signed reform proposals. Business groups, faith communities, and law enforcement leaders have united around evidence-based reform. Democrats should pursue this coalition aggressively, not cede it.

## PART I: THE DAMAGE INVENTORY — WHAT TRUMP'S SECOND TERM HAS DONE

### I.1 The Bove Memorandum and the Return to Maximum Charging

On January 21, 2025, Acting Deputy Attorney General Emil Bove issued a department-wide memorandum directing federal prosecutors to return to the charging and sentencing policies of earlier administrations — charging and pursuing "the most serious, readily provable offenses," defined as those with the highest mandatory minimum sentences and highest Sentencing Guidelines ranges.

AG Bondi issued 14 memos on her first day, rescinding Biden-era policies like the Garland Memo that called for prosecutors to make charging and sentencing decisions based on individualized assessment of each case.

What this means in practice: prosecutors are now directed to seek the harshest possible charge for every offense, regardless of individual circumstances, the role of the defendant, their history, or the actual public safety benefit. This is the policy that produced the 1990s mass incarceration crisis. It did not reduce crime. It filled prisons with people who posed little public safety risk while doing little to address the violent crime that actually endangers communities.

### I.2 The Death Penalty Reversal

On his first day in office, President Trump signed an executive order ending the moratorium on the federal death penalty. While former President Biden commuted the sentences of all but three individuals on federal death row to life without parole before leaving office, Attorney General Pam Bondi has already begun seeking new death sentences — and has called for using the death penalty in cases "involving extremely large quantities of drugs."

The expansion of the death penalty to drug trafficking cases would represent the most dramatic expansion of capital punishment in American history. It has no evidence base for deterrence. It has every historical precedent for racial disparity. And it runs directly counter to the global consensus among democracies that capital punishment is incompatible with the rule of law.

### I.3 Consent Decrees Ended — Police Accountability Dismantled

Through Trump's executive order on policing and Bondi's DEI memo, the administration has sought to end the use of consent decrees, which under the Obama administration emerged as the federal government's key mechanism for police accountability.

Consent decrees are the primary tool by which the DOJ Civil Rights Division has compelled police departments with documented patterns of constitutional violations to reform their practices. Minneapolis, Louisville, Memphis, Baltimore — cities that experienced high-profile police killings and subsequent DOJ investigations — have seen their consent decree processes terminated or threatened. Without federal oversight, documented patterns of constitutional violations go unremedied and recur.

### I.4 The First Step Act: Still on the Books, Still Being Undermined

More than 6,000 people remain in federal prison serving drug sentences that are longer than they would be if they were sentenced today — Congress has already decided these sentences are too long, but the changes were not made retroactive. The Trump administration has not used its clemency authority to remedy this. Instead:

- The administration is using clemency at a high rate — for January 6th insurrectionists, for political donors, for convicted fraudsters whose family members attended million-dollar fundraisers.
- Notable clemency orders include pardons for Trevor Milton (convicted of federal fraud, whose wife donated $1.8M to Trump's 2024 campaign) and Paul Walczak (convicted of tax crimes, whose mother attended a $1M per-person fundraiser one month before the pardon).
- The 6,000+ people serving sentences under rules Congress has already repudiated are not the beneficiaries of this clemency. Political allies are.

### I.5 Conditions in Federal Prisons — Deteriorating

In December 2025, the Trump administration ordered the DOJ to stop using standards designed to protect transgender and LGBTQ incarcerated people when evaluating prisons under the Prison Rape Elimination Act. In February 2026, the administration attempted to transfer people previously sentenced to death to oppressive maximum security facilities despite no security need, overriding Bureau of Prison officials' own assessments.

These are not isolated incidents. They reflect a governing philosophy that treats incarcerated people as deserving of punishment beyond the sentence imposed by a court — a philosophy that is both constitutionally dubious (courts impose sentences; prisons execute them) and empirically counterproductive (harsher prison conditions increase recidivism, not decrease it).

## PART II: THE PRE-EXISTING CRISIS

The Trump administration made a broken system worse. But the pre-existing conditions define the scale of genuine reform required:

**The mass incarceration reality:**
- The United States incarcerates more people per capita than any country on Earth — 2 million people in prison and jail at any given time; 600+ per 100,000 population vs. 100-150 in comparable democracies
- 1 in 3 Black men will be incarcerated at some point in their lifetime; the Black-white incarceration disparity is approximately 5:1
- Nearly half of people in federal prison are serving sentences for drug offenses — most with sentences longer than 10 years
- Taxpayers spend about $43,000 per federally incarcerated person per year; the recidivism rate is 43%
- 77 million Americans — roughly 1 in 3 adults — have a criminal record that can affect their ability to get a job, housing, education, and professional licenses

**The policing crisis:**
- Police kill approximately 1,000 Americans per year — more than any comparable democracy by a factor of 10 or more
- Black Americans are killed by police at 2.6 times the rate of white Americans
- Less than 2% of police killings result in any criminal charge against the officer
- The militarization of police forces — driven by federal 1033 program transfers of military equipment — has demonstrably increased use of force without corresponding reductions in crime

**The drug policy failure:**
- The United States has spent more than $1 trillion on the "War on Drugs" since 1971
- Drug use rates in the U.S. are not lower than in comparable countries with less punitive approaches; in some categories they are higher
- The overdose crisis — killing 80,000+ Americans annually — is not addressed by incarceration; it is a public health crisis requiring public health responses
- Marijuana: 70%+ of Americans support legalization (addressed in Chapter 6); the disparate enforcement of marijuana laws against Black Americans is documented and egregious

## PART III: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

**Public safety, not punishment for its own sake.** Every element of this agenda should be evaluated against one question: does it make communities safer? Incarceration that produces hardened criminals with no reentry support does not make communities safer. Policing that destroys community trust does not make communities safer. Drug criminalization that treats addiction as a moral failure rather than a medical condition does not make communities safer. Evidence-based approaches that reduce recidivism, build community trust, and treat addiction as a health issue do make communities safer.

**Accountability is not anti-police — it is pro-effective-policing.** Police departments that operate without accountability face a legitimacy crisis that makes their core public safety mission impossible. When communities don't trust police, crimes go unreported, witnesses don't cooperate, and investigations fail. Accountability — clear standards, transparent enforcement, meaningful consequences for violations — is what produces the trust that makes policing effective.

**Redemption and reintegration are conservative values too.** The faith community, the business community, and fiscal conservatives have all recognized that permanently excluding people from economic participation after they have served their sentence is wasteful, counterproductive, and unjust. The Clean Slate movement, the Fair Chance hiring movement, and the First Step Act all reflect a broad bipartisan consensus that people who have paid their debt to society deserve a genuine second chance.

**The racial justice dimension cannot be avoided but must be framed carefully.** The racial disparities in every stage of the criminal justice system — from stops and arrests through charging, sentencing, and parole — are documented and severe. Addressing them is not "soft on crime." It is fixing a system that has demonstrably failed to apply the law equally, which is the foundational commitment of American justice. The frame is: equal justice, not lenient justice.

## PART V: THE LEGISLATIVE AGENDA

### V.1 The Second Step Act — Completing the Bipartisan Reform Agenda

**Pathway:** Regular order; strong bipartisan potential — modeled on First Step Act's 87-vote coalition

The First Step Act was explicitly framed as the beginning of a process. The Second Step Act completes it.

**Sec. 1 — Retroactivity for the 6,000**
- Grant retroactive application of all First Step Act sentencing provisions to the estimated 6,000+ people still serving sentences longer than they would receive today
- Direct DOJ to identify all affected individuals and process resentencing motions within 180 days
- The First Step Act has been a success: individuals released under it return to federal prison at a 55% lower rate than similar people released before the law took effect. The retroactivity fix extends a proven success to people Congress already intended to include.

**Sec. 2 — EQUAL Act: End the Crack/Powder Cocaine Disparity**
- Eliminate the remaining 18:1 sentencing disparity between crack cocaine and powder cocaine
- The 100:1 disparity was reduced to 18:1 by the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010; the First Step Act made that change retroactive; the EQUAL Act eliminates the remaining disparity entirely
- There is no pharmacological basis for differential treatment; the disparity was a documented driver of racial sentencing inequality; the EQUAL Act has had bipartisan support for years

**Sec. 3 — Clean Slate Act: Automatic Record Sealing**
- Automatically seal federal criminal records for: (1) arrests that did not result in conviction; (2) convictions for low-level nonviolent offenses after successful sentence completion and a crime-free period (7 years for misdemeanors, 10 years for nonviolent felonies)
- Twelve states have already passed their own versions of this legislation; the federal Clean Slate Act extends this nationally
- Sealing does not expunge — law enforcement retains access; the record is not visible to most employers, landlords, and licensing boards
- A record that blocks employment and housing creates the conditions for recidivism; the Clean Slate Act addresses the cause, not the symptom

**Sec. 4 — Mandatory Minimum Reform**
- Restore judicial discretion to deviate below mandatory minimums in individual cases where the judge finds the mandatory minimum would produce an unjust sentence
- Expand the "safety valve" (first created in 1994, expanded in First Step Act) to cover all drug offenses, not just those without prior convictions
- Eliminate mandatory minimums for simple drug possession (not trafficking) entirely — possession is a public health matter, not a mandatory minimum matter
- Retain mandatory minimums for: violent offenses; large-scale drug trafficking; offenses involving weapons; sexual exploitation of children; terrorism

**Sec. 5 — Safer Supervision Act**
- Allow people on probation and supervised release to earn their way off supervision through demonstrated compliance and positive contributions (employment, education, community service)
- Currently, supervision can extend for years beyond what public safety requires, consuming resources and creating technical violations that return people to prison for bureaucratic infractions rather than new crimes
- The bipartisan Safer Supervision Act would allow more resources to be focused on those who are actually a public safety risk

**Sec. 6 — Federal Marijuana Expungement**
- Automatically expunge all federal convictions for simple marijuana possession and use
- Direct DOJ to identify all affected cases and process expungements within 12 months
- (Coordinate with Chapter 6's descheduling provision — expungement follows descheduling)

### V.2 The Death Penalty Abolition Act

**Pathway:** Regular order; this will require 60 votes or filibuster carve-out; pursue as standalone; bipartisan potential is limited but not zero

- Abolish the federal death penalty
- Commute all existing federal death sentences to life without parole
- The United States is an outlier among liberal democracies; 106 countries have abolished the death penalty; the evidence on deterrence is zero; the evidence on wrongful convictions is overwhelming (186 death row exonerations since 1973)
- The risk of executing an innocent person is not theoretical — it has happened
- The racial disparity is documented and unremedied: Black defendants are significantly more likely to receive death sentences, particularly in cases with white victims

*Note: Death penalty abolition is unlikely to achieve 60 Senate votes in the 2029-2033 term. It should be introduced, voted on, and put every senator on record. The goal is political truth-telling as well as policy change. The more immediately achievable executive actions — restoring the moratorium on Day 1, commuting existing federal death sentences — should be the primary first-term focus.*

### V.3 The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act (Updated)

**Pathway:** Regular order; filibuster carve-out; this is one of the strongest cases for carve-out alongside voting rights

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act passed the House twice with near-unanimous Democratic support. It died in the Senate each time due to Republican filibuster. Its core provisions represent the minimum accountability standard for federal law enforcement and for departments receiving federal funds.

**Sec. 1 — Federal Use of Force Standards**
- Prohibit federal officers from using chokeholds and carotid holds
- Require de-escalation before use of force; mandate exhaustion of reasonable alternatives
- Restrict no-knock warrants to cases where a judge finds imminent danger and no alternatives
- Require officers to intervene and report when they witness another officer using excessive force
- These standards apply directly to federal officers; apply to state and local departments as conditions of receiving federal law enforcement grants

**Sec. 2 — Accountability and Transparency**
- End qualified immunity for federal officers; allow individuals to sue for constitutional violations without the near-insurmountable "clearly established law" barrier that has shielded officers from accountability
- Create a National Police Misconduct Registry: a federal database of sustained misconduct findings, terminations for cause, and civil judgments against officers — preventing "wandering officers" who are fired for misconduct in one jurisdiction and rehired in another
- Require federal reporting on all use of force incidents, all police killings, all deaths in custody; currently no comprehensive national database exists
- Require body cameras for all federal law enforcement officers; require retention of footage for 90 days; establish strict access and release protocols

**Sec. 3 — Ending Racial Profiling**
- Federal prohibition on racial, religious, and discriminatory profiling by law enforcement at any level
- Require data collection on all stops, searches, and arrests by race, ethnicity, and gender
- Direct DOJ to analyze this data annually for disparate impact and publish findings publicly

**Sec. 4 — Restoring Consent Decrees**
- Restore and expand the DOJ Civil Rights Division's authority to investigate police departments and enter consent decrees
- Reverse the Trump administration's orders discouraging consent decrees
- Require the DOJ to investigate any department with two or more sustained civil rights violations in a 3-year period
- Fund the Civil Rights Division with 300 additional attorneys to investigate and monitor police departments

**Sec. 5 — Community Violence Intervention**
- Fund evidence-based community violence intervention programs at $5B over 5 years
- These programs — focused on interrupting cycles of retaliatory violence in communities with high rates of gun violence — have the strongest evidence base of any violence reduction intervention; they are not alternatives to policing, they are complements to it
- Chicago's READI program, Oakland's Ceasefire model, and similar programs have demonstrated 20-60% reductions in violence in target communities

**Sec. 6 — Demilitarization**
- Reform the 1033 Program: restrict transfer of military equipment to local police to items with documented law enforcement application; prohibit transfer of weaponized aircraft, grenade launchers, bayonets, and tracked armored vehicles
- Fund a "right-sizing" program for departments that voluntarily return equipment — they receive grants for community policing and mental health co-responder programs instead

### V.4 The Mental Health and Crisis Response Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (spending provisions) and regular order (grant conditions)

This is the alternative to "defund" that actually solves the problem:

**Sec. 1 — Transforming 911 Response**
- Fund a national co-responder program: every jurisdiction above 50,000 population must develop an alternative response pathway for 911 calls involving mental health crisis, welfare checks, homelessness, and substance use — staffed by mental health professionals, social workers, and crisis counselors rather than (or alongside) armed police
- $3B over 5 years for program establishment, training, and ongoing operations
- Model: Eugene, Oregon's CAHOOTS program (operating since 1989) handles 24% of all 911 calls with zero use of force and at 1/6 the cost of police response
- Participating jurisdictions must track: response outcomes, cost per call, officer hours redirected to violent crime response

**Sec. 2 — Mental Health Crisis Infrastructure**
- Fund crisis stabilization centers as alternatives to emergency departments and jail: short-stay facilities that provide mental health assessment, treatment initiation, and connection to ongoing care
- 98% of people in mental health crisis do not need to be arrested; they need treatment
- Fund mobile crisis teams: trained mental health responders who can be dispatched directly for non-violent mental health calls

**Sec. 3 — Reentry Mental Health**
- 20% of people in prison have a serious mental illness; they receive inadequate treatment inside and no continuity of care upon release — a direct driver of recidivism
- Fund Medicaid enrollment on release day: every person leaving state or federal prison is enrolled in Medicaid (where the state has expanded) on their release date; no gap in coverage
- Fund medication continuity: people with serious mental illness leaving prison must receive a 30-day supply of their medications on release day and a warm handoff to a community mental health provider

### V.5 The Second Chance and Reentry Investment Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (spending provisions) and regular order (tax incentives)

Every dollar spent on evidence-based reentry programs saves multiple dollars in future incarceration costs. This is fiscal conservatism and criminal justice reform in the same bill.

**Sec. 1 — Education in Prison**
- Restore and expand Pell Grant eligibility for incarcerated people (restored under Biden after 30-year ban, now under pressure)
- Fund prison education comprehensively: high school equivalency, vocational training, college courses, and professional certification
- The evidence is unambiguous: every dollar invested in prison education returns $5 in reduced incarceration costs; programs that help people earn credentials reduce recidivism by up to 43%

**Sec. 2 — Vocational Training and Employment**
- Fund the Federal Prison Industries (UNICOR) program with a mandate to focus on skills training for post-release employment — not just cheap prison labor for government contracts
- Create a federal Second Chance Hiring Tax Credit: employers who hire people within 3 years of release from state or federal prison receive a 40% wage subsidy for the first year of employment
- Extend and strengthen the Fair Chance Hiring Act: prohibit federal contractors from asking about criminal history before a conditional offer of employment; encourage states to adopt "ban the box" standards

**Sec. 3 — Housing After Incarceration**
- The single largest predictor of recidivism after release is housing instability; people who leave prison with nowhere to go are far more likely to return
- Fund transitional housing: 10,000 transitional housing beds in communities with high rates of incarceration and release
- Prohibit public housing authorities from permanently excluding people with criminal records from federal housing assistance (current law allows indefinite exclusion); require individualized assessment rather than categorical exclusion
- Fund "returning citizens" navigator programs: case managers who assist with housing, benefits enrollment, employment, and healthcare during the first 90 days after release

**Sec. 4 — Voting Rights Restoration**
- Restore federal voting rights to all U.S. citizens upon release from incarceration — the 2 million Americans currently in prison and the 4.6 million on probation or parole who have lost their voting rights
- The Voting Rights Restoration for the Incarcerated Act establishes that federal elections cannot be conditioned on completion of sentence, probation, or parole
- This is a democratic principle, not a partisan one: people subject to laws should have a voice in making those laws

### V.6 The Juvenile Justice Reform Act

**Pathway:** Regular order (bipartisan potential) and reconciliation (spending provisions)

**Sec. 1 — Raise the Age**
- Federal standard: juveniles under 18 may not be prosecuted as adults for any offense, including violent offenses, in federal court
- Federal grants incentivize states to adopt the same standard; currently 44 states have raised the age of adult prosecution to 18 but implementation varies
- The evidence is clear: trying juveniles as adults increases recidivism and produces worse outcomes than juvenile court; the juvenile brain is neurologically different in ways that affect impulse control and decision-making

**Sec. 2 — End Solitary Confinement for Juveniles**
- Prohibit use of solitary confinement for any person under 18 in any facility receiving federal funds
- The psychological damage of solitary confinement on developing brains is severe and well-documented

**Sec. 3 — Invest in Prevention**
- Fund evidence-based juvenile crime prevention: mentorship programs, after-school programming, gang intervention, and family support services
- The most cost-effective criminal justice investment is preventing the first contact with the system; every youth diverted from the juvenile justice system is a lifetime of costs avoided

Criminal justice reform has a uniquely durable bipartisan coalition that Democrats should exploit aggressively:

- Veterans' justice: veterans overrepresented in justice-involved population; bipartisan support for veterans' treatment courts

### VII.2 The "Defund" Ghost

Every Democratic candidate must have a direct, practiced answer for this:

> "I believe police departments should be adequately funded, professionally trained, and equipped for the jobs we ask them to do — and I believe we've been asking them to do jobs they were never trained for. A police officer is not a mental health counselor. An armed response is not the right answer to someone having a psychiatric crisis. We fund the right response for the right call. That is not defunding police — it is right-sizing government so every responder is set up to succeed."

The data to support this: jurisdictions that have implemented co-responder models have reduced officer injuries, reduced unnecessary hospitalizations and incarcerations, and freed up police for actual law enforcement functions. This is a story about making police more effective, not less.

### VII.3 The Crime Reality

Democrats have allowed Republicans to own the "public safety" frame despite the empirical reality:

- Violent crime rates are significantly lower today than in the 1990s
- The crime spike during COVID has largely receded
- The best predictors of crime are poverty, inequality, housing instability, and untreated mental illness — all of which the broader Project 2029 agenda addresses
- Police killings are not correlated with crime rates — they are correlated with police department culture, training, and accountability systems

**The Democrat's case on crime:**
> "Violent crime is lower today than it was when Republicans were writing three-strikes laws and filling prisons. The question is not who is tougher on crime — it's who has a plan that actually reduces crime. Incarceration that produces a 43% recidivism rate is not a plan. Co-responders, mental health treatment, education in prisons, housing after release, and policing that communities trust — that is a plan."

## PART VIII: THE RACIAL JUSTICE DIMENSION — EXPLICIT AND HONEST

This chapter has been written in a politically careful frame — public safety, fiscal responsibility, bipartisan appeal. But the racial justice dimension must be stated plainly somewhere, and this section is that place.

**The documented disparities at every stage:**
- Black Americans are stopped and searched at 2-3x the rate of white Americans in comparable situations
- Black defendants are charged with offenses carrying mandatory minimums at higher rates than white defendants with similar conduct
- Black defendants receive sentences approximately 19% longer than white defendants for comparable offenses
- Black Americans are imprisoned at 5x the rate of white Americans
- The death penalty is applied in a documented racially disparate pattern: defendants are significantly more likely to receive death sentences when victims are white

**These disparities are not explained by criminality — they are the product of the system itself.** The same crack/powder cocaine disparity that the EQUAL Act would eliminate was not created by accident. Drug enforcement has historically been concentrated in Black and Latino communities even when drug use rates across communities are comparable.

**The governing commitment:** Every provision in this chapter — individualized sentencing, consent decrees, Clean Slate, Second Chance hiring, voting rights restoration — disproportionately benefits communities of color not because they are targeted at those communities but because those communities have been disproportionately harmed by the current system. Equal justice, applied consistently, produces less racially disparate outcomes. That is the goal.

**The political frame:** This should not be framed primarily as a racial justice issue in general election communications — not because the racial justice dimension doesn't matter, but because the most effective political frame for the same policy is public safety and fiscal responsibility, which is also true. Democrats can hold both without apology: this agenda is good for all communities and it addresses documented racial disparities as a natural consequence. Name both, in the right order for the right audience.
