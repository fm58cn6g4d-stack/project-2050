## PREFATORY NOTE: WHAT THIS CHAPTER IS ABOUT

The economic fairness agenda is the material core of Project 2029 — the domain where the anti-oligarchy frame connects most directly to kitchen-table reality. It answers the questions that dominate actual voter conversations:

- Why is my rent still so high?
- Why do I work two jobs and still can't get ahead?
- Why are grocery prices still up when the company just posted record profits?
- Why does my boss get a $30 million bonus while they're cutting my hours?
- Why did the tariffs just make everything more expensive while the trade deficit got worse?

Every section of this chapter has a villain that is specific and named — not "the economy" or "global forces" but corporations that colluded to fix prices, private equity firms that extracted value from communities, and a trade policy that transferred wealth from American households to the very companies it pretended to punish.

This chapter covers six interconnected domains:

1. **Antitrust and Anti-Monopoly** — the structural root cause
2. **Worker Power** — wages, organizing, and the balance of power at work
3. **Housing** — the most acute cost-of-living crisis in a generation
4. **Financial System Reform** — the plumbing of the economy that serves the wealthy
5. **Trade Policy** — reconstruction after the tariff disaster
6. **Rural and Agricultural Economics** — the food system's buried monopoly crisis

## PART I: THE DAMAGE INVENTORY — WHAT TRUMP MADE WORSE

### I.1 Antitrust: Reversal of the Revival

The Biden administration had launched the most aggressive antitrust revival since the New Deal — restoring the FTC and DOJ Antitrust Division to serious enforcement after four decades of bipartisan neglect guided by the "consumer welfare standard" that systematically assumed large corporations are more efficient and that the risks from concentration are minimal.

Since returning to office in 2025, President Trump has taken a very different approach to antitrust, pushing back against the revival of recent years — scaling back or settling aggressive cases, installing industry-friendly officials, and steering policy in a pro-business, anti-regulatory direction.

Specific reversals:
- Trump's DOJ backed off Google's AI investment divestiture remedy — the most structural element of the landmark Google monopoly decision
- Trump illegally attempted to remove Democratic FTC Commissioners Alvaro Bedoya and Rebecca Kelly Slaughter, though legally they can be removed only for cause — a direct attack on the FTC's bipartisan independence structure
- The OCC and the Fed greenlit Capital One's $35.3 billion acquisition of Discover, consolidating the U.S.'s two largest nonprime lenders, despite Democratic opposition
- The Robinson-Patman Act — a 100-year-old antitrust law that prevents producers from giving large businesses unfair pricing advantages over small businesses — has seen revival efforts threatened while independent grocery store closures have skyrocketed
- The OBBBA would impose a ten-year moratorium on any state and local laws governing AI, including bills that would check Big Tech and limit state regulation of algorithmic rental price-fixing schemes — a sweeping preemption of state antitrust innovation disguised as tech deregulation

The backdrop: following significant deregulation of merger control and antitrust policies in the 1980s, 75% of American industries became more concentrated from the 1990s through the 2010s. The Biden revival had begun reversing this trend. Trump is accelerating it.

### I.2 Housing: The Algorithm-Collusion Crisis

Over twenty-one million American households — nearly half of American renter households — were rent-burdened in 2023, meaning they spent more than 30% of their income on housing costs.

The housing crisis has a concentrated structural cause that extends well beyond supply constraints: algorithmic collusion. Rental pricing algorithms add an average of $70 per month to the cost of rent for units in algorithm-utilizing buildings. In 2023 alone, such algorithms are estimated to have cost renters more than $3.8 billion.

RealPage's clients comprise nearly 90% of the U.S. market for multifamily rental housing units with 50 units or more. With such a large share of the market, decisions made by RealPage clients have a magnified impact on rental prices across the country, dramatically increasing rent prices and eviction rates.

The DOJ sued RealPage — but the outcome was a settlement that critics called a missed opportunity: RealPage would not have to pay any damages or admit any wrongdoing. Under the terms of the proposed settlement, RealPage can no longer use real-time data to determine price recommendations, but the only nonpublic data that can be used must be at least one year old. This leaves the core business model largely intact.

Meanwhile, the OBBBA's AI moratorium would preempt the growing wave of state and local algorithmic rent pricing bans — at least 11 jurisdictions have now prohibited collusion facilitated by algorithmic pricing tools — freezing state innovation on the most effective near-term remedy.

### I.3 Trade: The Tariff Disaster's True Ledger

The Trump tariffs are the largest U.S. tax increase as a percent of GDP since 1993 and amount to an average tax increase per U.S. household of $1,500 in 2026, with the tariffs in 2025 amounting to an average tax increase of $1,000 per household.

The SCOTUS ruled against IEEPA tariffs in February 2026 (case: *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* [case name to verify before publication]), but Trump responded immediately. After the Supreme Court ruling, Trump announced a global tariff of 10% under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, to remain in effect for 150 days, until July 24, 2026. Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminum, cars, and other sectors remain in force. The government estimated that it collected $166 billion specifically from IEEPA tariffs that the Supreme Court found unconstitutional — that amount is now subject to refund. Total tariff revenues across all mechanisms ran approximately $175 billion, with the difference representing non-IEEPA tariff collections under Section 232 and other authorities. All of it was paid by American businesses and consumers, not by foreign countries.

By Trump's own stated goals, the tariffs failed: by Trump's own yardstick — his three goals of making foreigners pay, narrowing the U.S. trade deficit, and punishing China — tariffs have clearly failed. The $175 billion in extra government revenues was a tax on Americans, not foreigners. The trade deficit did not narrow. China overtook the United States as Germany's top trading partner. Washington's threats of tariffs may no longer be credible — allies and foes have shown that they can at least adapt to them and at best turn them into an opportunity to diversify away from the United States.

The supply chain reality: Xi weaponized rare earth permanent magnets and certain semiconductors in 2025. The consequences were frightening for carmakers and workers worldwide who remain heavily dependent on China for such vital inputs. Trump's trade war, while failing on its own terms, exposed real supply chain vulnerabilities that a serious industrial policy must address.

### I.4 Worker Power: Systematic Dismantlement

The OBBBA and the Trump administration together have systematically eroded worker power:
- NLRB leadership replaced with management-friendly appointees; enforcement guidance withdrawn
- Overtime protections rolled back; salary threshold for overtime eligibility frozen
- Worker classification rules loosened, expanding gig economy misclassification
- Wage theft enforcement capacity gutted at the Department of Labor
- Federal contractor wage and benefit standards weakened
- Non-compete agreements no longer constrained by the FTC rule finalized under Biden
- Public sector collective bargaining rights reduced by executive order

The structural backdrop: corporate profits as a share of GDP are at or near historic highs; worker wages as a share of GDP remain below pre-1980 levels. The worker-employer power imbalance has been the dominant driver of wage stagnation and inequality for four decades. This form of monopoly power has contributed to the rise in income inequality over the past several decades — concentration in product markets is inseparable from concentration in labor markets; when one employer dominates a region, workers have less ability to find alternatives or demand higher wages.

### I.5 Grocery and Food System Concentration

Just four corporations — Walmart, Kroger, Costco, and Albertsons — control more than two-thirds of the U.S. grocery market. In many communities, Walmart alone holds more than half of the market share.

For some foods, farmers now get only 15 cents out of every dollar consumers spend at the grocery store. The same consolidation that squeezes farmers squeezes consumers: mega-retailers use their market dominance to raise prices and drive record margins — a quiet transfer of billions from American households to corporate shareholders. They can get away with it because in the absence of other grocers, consumers must submit to mega-retailers' price hikes.

The egg price crisis is the most recent vivid example: five companies control 46% of U.S. egg-laying hens, with Cal-Maine controlling over 20% of the market and now earning $1 billion annually. The DOJ Antitrust Division launched an investigation into whether anticompetitive conduct is driving high prices and profits.

## PART II: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

### II.1 The Core Frame: Competitive Markets vs. Captured Markets

The economic fairness agenda is not anti-market. It is pro-market — in the genuine sense of markets that are competitive, transparent, and subject to the rule of law. The Democratic economic vision rests on a distinction that is rarely articulated clearly:

**Competitive markets** — where firms must innovate and offer value to win customers, where workers have bargaining power because employers must compete for their labor, where prices reflect supply and demand rather than market power — are good for workers, consumers, small businesses, and the economy.

**Captured markets** — where a handful of firms set prices without meaningful competition, where workers have no alternatives and no bargaining power, where the rules are written by the industries they govern — concentrate wealth, stifle innovation, and hollow out communities.

The agenda below is designed to restore the former and dismantle the latter. It does not apologize for using government power to enforce competitive conditions — that is what antitrust law has always been, and it is what built the American middle class.

### II.2 The "Good Capitalism" Frame

The most politically effective way to present this agenda is not as government intervention against the market but as government enforcement of fair market rules:

> "We believe in capitalism. We believe in the right to start a business, compete, and succeed. What we don't believe in is a system where a handful of giant corporations rig the rules so no one else can compete, fix prices in secret using algorithms, and use their political spending to write the regulations that govern them. That's not capitalism. That's a cartel. And we're going to enforce the law."

This frame:
- Aligns with small business owners and entrepreneurs who are harmed by monopoly power
- Distinguishes the agenda from "socialism" attacks
- Has genuine bipartisan appeal — the antitrust movement has always had Republican supporters
- Is consistent with the "rigged rules" narrative established in Chapter 2

## PART IV: THE LEGISLATIVE AGENDA

### IV.1 Antitrust Restoration and Enforcement Act

**Purpose:** Restore, modernize, and dramatically strengthen U.S. antitrust law and enforcement capacity across all sectors.

#### Title I: Restore and Fund the Enforcers

**Sec. 1.1 — FTC Restoration**
- Restore the FTC to its full bipartisan 5-commissioner composition immediately; fill all vacancies within 60 days
- Restore and expand FTC Section 5 authority to prohibit "unfair methods of competition" — the broadest authority Congress gave the FTC in 1914, narrowed by Trump-era interpretations
- Reverse any Trump-era personnel actions against FTC commissioners that violated the cause-requirement for removal; codify the cause requirement in statute
- Double the FTC's budget over 5 years: from ~$400M to $800M annually; fund 500 additional enforcement staff

**Sec. 1.2 — DOJ Antitrust Division Restoration**
- Appoint an Assistant Attorney General for Antitrust committed to structural relief (divestiture) not just behavioral remedies (pinky-promise agreements)
- Double the Antitrust Division's budget over 5 years
- Restore and expand the Civil Investigative Demand (CID) process — the investigative authority the Trump DOJ weakened
- Reinstate and strengthen the DOJ/FTC merger guidelines updated by the Biden administration and rolled back by Trump

**Sec. 1.3 — State Antitrust Coordination**
- Create a Federal-State Antitrust Coordination Office at DOJ; fund state AGs to bring antitrust cases in parallel with federal action
- Repeal the OBBBA's 10-year moratorium on state AI regulation, which would have preempted state and local algorithmic pricing laws
- Establish federal minimum standards that states may exceed (not federal ceilings that states cannot exceed) for antitrust enforcement

#### Title II: Modernize the Substantive Law

**Sec. 2.1 — Merger Standards Reform**
- Restore the presumption of illegality for mergers that create market concentration above the HHI (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index) thresholds established in the 2023 merger guidelines
- Shift the burden of proof: in concentrated markets, the merging parties must prove their merger is pro-competitive, not regulators prove it is anti-competitive
- Prohibit "killer acquisitions": dominant platforms may not acquire companies with less than $50M in revenue unless they can affirmatively demonstrate the acquisition is not intended to neutralize competitive threat
- Establish presumptive prohibition for acquisitions by companies with >40% market share in relevant markets

**Sec. 2.2 — Platform Monopoly Act**
- Prohibit dominant digital platforms (defined: $500B market cap or 30%+ market share in their primary market) from:
  - Self-preferencing their own products in search, ranking, or recommendation results
  - Acquiring potential competitors without clear and convincing evidence of consumer benefit
  - Using data collected in one market to compete in adjacent markets
  - Conditioning access to their platform on use of their own services
- Modeled on the American Innovation and Choice Online Act, which had bipartisan support before stalling under lobbying pressure

**Sec. 2.3 — Algorithmic Pricing Prohibition**
- Federal prohibition on use of algorithms that share non-public, competitively sensitive pricing data across competing firms — whether the algorithm is operated by a third party (RealPage model) or directly coordinated
- Apply to all markets, not just rental housing: applies to grocery pricing, hotel pricing, healthcare service pricing, and any other essential goods and services
- Civil penalty: up to 3x the estimated harm to consumers; private right of action for affected consumers
- Direct the FTC to issue implementing regulations within 180 days; coordinate with state enforcers
- Explicitly preempt the OBBBA's AI moratorium as applied to consumer protection and antitrust enforcement

**Sec. 2.4 — Robinson-Patman Act Revival**
- Direct the FTC to reinstate active Robinson-Patman Act enforcement: this 1936 law prohibits sellers from offering discriminatory prices that benefit large buyers at the expense of small buyers — its gutting has been the single most significant driver of independent grocery store closures and farm income erosion
- Fund a dedicated RPA enforcement unit within the FTC: 50 attorneys focused specifically on price discrimination in food, retail, and essential goods sectors
- Extend RPA to digital markets: apply price discrimination prohibitions to algorithm-driven pricing systems that offer volume discounts structured to foreclose small business competition

**Sec. 2.5 — Healthcare Antitrust**
- Establish a Health Care Market Oversight Authority within the FTC: separate bureau with dedicated funding and authority to review all healthcare mergers (hospitals, physician practices, pharmacy, insurance, pharmacy benefit managers) regardless of size
- Lower the notification threshold for healthcare mergers from $119M to $10M in transaction value — capturing the "roll-up" acquisitions that individually evade scrutiny
- Presumptive bar on vertical integration that creates conflicts of interest: UnitedHealth-Optum model (insurer owning physician practices, PBMs, and pharmacies simultaneously) should face structural separation requirements
- Require annual competitive impact reports from the five largest health insurance companies and hospital systems

**Sec. 2.6 — Private Equity Reform Act**
- End the carried interest loophole: tax private equity profits at ordinary income rates, not the preferential capital gains rate (this is a tax provision coordinated with Chapter 2's revenue agenda)
- Anti-stripping rules: prohibit PE-owned firms from issuing dividends to owners that leave the company with debt-to-EBITDA ratios above 4x within 5 years of acquisition
- Worker notification and severance: PE-owned firms must notify workers 90 days before mass layoffs; workers receive 2 weeks severance per year of service paid from any acquisition proceeds before owner distributions
- Healthcare carve-out: prohibit PE acquisition of hospitals, emergency medical services, and nursing homes in markets with fewer than 3 competing providers

### IV.2 The American Workers First Act

**Purpose:** Restore the balance of power between workers and employers; raise wages; protect workers from systematic legal abuse.

#### Title I: The Minimum Wage

**Sec. 1.1 — Federal Minimum Wage**
- Raise the federal minimum wage from $7.25/hour (unchanged since 2009) to $17/hour effective 18 months after enactment; $19/hour at 36 months; indexed to inflation thereafter using the Consumer Price Index
- Note: The chapter framework originally proposed $20/hour but the phased approach — landing at $19 by end of Year 2 — reflects the Senate vote math constraint identified in Chapter 5. The $20 target remains correct for the second term.
- Eliminate the tipped minimum wage sub-floor: all workers receive the full minimum wage; tips are additional income
- Apply to all employers; eliminate small employer and age sub-minimum wage exceptions (with 3-year transition period for businesses under 20 employees)

**Sec. 1.2 — Overtime Protection**
- Restore and raise the salary threshold for overtime eligibility: workers earning less than $80,000/year are automatically eligible for overtime pay (restoring and exceeding the Biden rule blocked by courts)
- Index the threshold to inflation going forward
- Close the "manager exemption" abuse: employees must spend at least 75% of their time on managerial duties to qualify for the executive exemption, not merely have a manager title

#### Title II: The Right to Organize

**Sec. 2.1 — PRO Act Core Provisions (Reconciliation-Eligible Elements)**
- Restore and expand NLRB enforcement funding: $1B annually (current ~$300M) — the NLRB's budget has barely grown in 20 years while case complexity has exploded
- Codify card-check certification: if a majority of workers sign authorization cards, the employer must recognize the union without requiring a NLRB election (elections are subject to months of employer delays and intimidation)
- Mandatory mediation and arbitration for first contracts: if a newly certified union cannot reach a first contract within 90 days, a federal mediator steps in; if no agreement in 120 days, a federal arbitrator sets the first contract terms; this eliminates the "win the election, then get starved out" strategy
- Expand protected concerted activity: workers who engage in collective action (including strikes and work stoppages) cannot be permanently replaced; secondary boycotts permitted for solidarity action
- Eliminate right-to-work provisions that allow workers to free-ride on union contracts without paying dues (Janus v. AFSCME response)
- Extend PRO Act protections to domestic workers, agricultural workers, and gig workers — categories historically excluded from NLRA coverage
- *Note: PRO Act full passage requires filibuster reform (Chapter 5). Budget-adjacent provisions pass via reconciliation in Year 1; full PRO Act requires carve-out, targeted for Year 3.*

**Sec. 2.2 — Sectoral Bargaining**
- Establish sectoral wage boards in five high-density, low-wage industries: fast food, home care, retail, warehouse/logistics, and agriculture
- Each board includes: equal representation of workers, employers, and public members; authority to set minimum wage and working condition floors for the entire sector, not just unionized workplaces
- Modeled on New York's fast food wage board (which produced the $15 minimum wage) and California's AB 1228
- Sectoral minimums are a floor; individual workplace agreements can exceed them

#### Title III: Wage Theft and Worker Protection

**Sec. 3.1 — Wage Theft Crackdown**
- Wage theft is the single largest property crime in the United States — employers steal more from workers annually (~$50B) than all robberies, burglaries, and auto thefts combined
- Restore DOL Wage and Hour Division to full capacity: hire 1,500 additional investigators (from current ~800)
- Criminal wage theft: make intentional wage theft a federal felony with a 5-year maximum sentence; currently it is rarely prosecuted as a crime
- Joint employer standard restored: franchisors and staffing agencies are jointly liable for wage violations by their franchisees and clients; reverses Trump NLRB interpretation
- Expand private right of action: workers can sue for 3x unpaid wages plus attorney fees; statute of limitations extended from 2 to 5 years

**Sec. 3.2 — Non-Compete Reform**
- Federal ban on non-compete agreements for workers earning under $150,000/year; for workers above that threshold, non-competes must be time-limited (maximum 1 year), geographically limited, and accompanied by compensation during the restricted period
- Ban on no-poach agreements between employers that prevent workers from moving to competitors in the same industry
- Restore and strengthen the FTC non-compete rule finalized in 2024 and immediately rescinded by Trump; codify in statute so it cannot be reversed by executive action

**Sec. 3.3 — Worker Classification**
- Establish a federal "ABC test" for worker classification: a worker is an employee unless the hiring entity proves (A) the worker is free from control and direction, (B) the work is outside the usual course of the company's business, and (C) the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade — this is the California AB5 test, applied federally
- Platform companies (gig economy) that fail the ABC test must: provide minimum wage, overtime, workers compensation, unemployment insurance, and the right to organize
- *Note: This provision is the most legally and politically contested element of the worker power agenda. Expect intense litigation. Design the provision with a 2-year phase-in and dedicated DOL guidance process to manage implementation.*

### IV.3 The American Housing Investment and Fairness Act

**Purpose:** Address the housing affordability crisis through supply, anti-gouging, tenant protection, and homeownership access — the three-part problem that requires a three-part solution.

#### Title I: Build More Housing

**Sec. 1.1 — Federal Affordable Housing Trust Fund**
- $200 billion over 10 years for the National Affordable Housing Trust Fund: capital grants to states, localities, and nonprofits for construction of affordable rental units (targeting households earning below 80% of Area Median Income)
- Prioritize mixed-income developments in high-opportunity neighborhoods — not segregated low-income housing projects
- Fund modular and factory-built housing technology to reduce construction costs (current construction cost inflation is itself a crisis)

**Sec. 1.2 — Exclusionary Zoning Reform**
- Condition eligibility for HUD Community Development Block Grants and Surface Transportation grants on state or local adoption of inclusionary zoning policies: localities must permit multi-family housing by-right within 1/2 mile of transit stops, and ADUs (accessory dwelling units) by-right on all single-family lots
- Create a federal "Yes In My Backyard" grant program: $20B over 10 years for localities that reform exclusionary zoning and achieve housing production targets
- *Note: Direct federal preemption of local zoning is constitutionally and politically fraught. The funding-conditioned approach — localities choose between federal grants and exclusionary zoning — is the path of least resistance and has precedent in transportation and education law.*

**Sec. 1.3 — Federal Land for Housing**
- Direct BLM, DOD, and other federal land-holding agencies to identify and transfer to state and local governments at below-market cost any federal land within urban and suburban areas suitable for housing development
- Estimated available: several hundred thousand acres nationally; even a fraction represents significant potential housing supply in high-cost markets

**Sec. 1.4 — Low-Income Housing Tax Credit Expansion**
- Double the LIHTC allocation to states over 5 years; extend the credits from 10 to 15 years to improve project economics
- Add a new "middle-income" LIHTC tier for units affordable to households earning 80-120% of AMI — the "missing middle" that neither private market nor poverty-focused programs serve
- Simplify the LIHTC compliance process: current complexity adds $50,000–$75,000 in soft costs per unit without improving housing quality

**Sec. 1.5 — High-Speed Rail and Transit-Oriented Development**
- The connection between transportation and housing is structural: cities with good transit systems can grow without requiring every new resident to own a car, reducing both housing and transportation cost burdens simultaneously
- National High-Speed Rail Authority established within DOT: $300B over 15 years for a national rail spine connecting the 50 largest metro areas
- Accelerate intercity rail: initial corridor investment in Northeast, Midwest (Chicago hub), Texas Triangle, California, and Pacific Northwest — where ridership is proven and political will exists
- Transit-oriented development fund: $50B for state and local transit agencies to fund housing development on transit-owned land

#### Title II: End Algorithmic Rent Gouging and Protect Tenants

**Sec. 2.1 — Federal Algorithmic Rent Price-Fixing Ban**
- Codify as a federal statutory violation (not just antitrust common law): no landlord may use algorithmic tools that share non-public rental pricing data across competing landlords to coordinate rents
- Explicitly preempt the OBBBA's AI moratorium as applied to rental pricing
- Private right of action for tenants: sue for $7,500 per violation or actual damages, whichever is greater, plus attorney fees
- FTC civil penalty authority: up to $50,000 per day per violation; treble damages in cases of willful conduct

**Sec. 2.2 — Corporate Landlord Transparency Act**
- Require disclosure of beneficial ownership for all corporate entities owning more than 5 rental units; reported to FinCEN and publicly searchable database
- Require disclosure of algorithmic pricing system use to prospective and current tenants
- Require large landlords (500+ units) to publicly disclose annual rental pricing data, vacancy rates, and eviction rates by ZIP code

**Sec. 2.3 — National Tenant Protection Standards**
- Establish federal minimum renter protections that states may exceed:
  - 90-day notice required for rent increases exceeding 5% or CPI, whichever is greater
  - "Just cause" eviction requirement: landlords must have documented reason (non-payment, lease violation, property sale) to evict; no "no-cause" evictions
  - Right to renew lease on same terms absent documented cause for non-renewal
  - Security deposit cap: maximum 1.5 months' rent; return within 21 days with itemized accounting
- *Note: Federal rent control is constitutionally complex and politically toxic with some constituencies. These are process protections, not price controls — and they are much more durable.*

#### Title III: Homeownership Access

**Sec. 3.1 — First-Generation Homebuyer Act**
- $25,000 down-payment assistance grant for first-generation homebuyers (defined: households where neither buyer nor their parents ever owned a home in the United States)
- Income limit: household income below 120% of area median income
- Paid for through LIHTC reform and housing trust fund (existing reconciliation vehicle)
- Estimated beneficiaries: 400,000-600,000 households annually

**Sec. 3.2 — Community Land Trust Expansion**
- $10B over 10 years for Community Land Trust capitalization: CLTs purchase land permanently, removing it from speculative markets; buyers purchase only the structure, dramatically reducing entry cost
- CLT homeownership has consistently produced stable ownership at 1/3 to 1/2 the cost of market-rate purchase
- Federal model legislation for states to enable CLT formation and operation

**Sec. 3.3 — Mortgage Market Reform**
- Preserve Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac in public conservatorship; do not privatize — privatization would increase mortgage rates and reduce access for first-time and lower-income buyers
- Strengthen the Community Reinvestment Act: require banks to demonstrate affirmative lending to underserved communities in proportion to their deposit bases
- Expand HUD-approved housing counseling: $500M annually for pre-purchase and foreclosure prevention counseling, proven to significantly reduce default rates

### IV.4 Financial System Reform Act

**Purpose:** Reform the financial system to serve workers, savers, and communities — not primarily to generate fees and speculative returns for financial industry insiders.

**Sec. 1 — Modern Glass-Steagall**
- Separate commercial banking (deposit-taking, lending) from investment banking (securities underwriting, trading) and insurance
- Banks with more than $250B in assets must choose between commercial and investment banking activities within 5 years
- Eliminate cross-subsidiaries that allow commercial banks to subsidize risky trading activities with FDIC-insured deposits
- *Note: This is politically ambitious. Frame it as protecting depositors and taxpayers from being on the hook for Wall Street gambling — which is exactly what it is.*

**Sec. 2 — Credit Card Interest Rate Cap**
- Cap consumer credit card interest rates at 15% (currently averaging 22-24%)
- Grandfather existing balances at current rates for 24 months to allow transition
- This is a genuinely popular bipartisan measure — Trump himself has endorsed the concept
- Estimated consumer savings: $12B-$18B annually in interest costs on existing balances

**Sec. 3 — Postal Banking**
- Authorize the United States Postal Service to offer basic financial services: checking accounts, savings accounts, small-dollar loans, and bill payment
- 12 million unbanked and 24 million underbanked Americans currently pay predatory fees to check-cashing stores and payday lenders — often 10-30% of their transaction amounts
- Postal banking has historical precedent (operated 1911-1967) and proven models internationally (Japan, UK, France)
- Revenue: USPS generates service fees; reduces payday lending predation without direct subsidy

**Sec. 4 — CFPB Restoration and Expansion**
- Restore CFPB to full operational capacity: all DOGE-era firings reversed; dropped lawsuits against Capital One, Berkshire, Wells Fargo, and JPMorgan reinstated
- Codify CFPB funding independence: direct Fed funding removed from annual congressional appropriations process, preventing future defunding attempts
- Expand CFPB jurisdiction: add authority over non-bank credit reporting agencies, data brokers, and fintech lenders that currently evade oversight
- Restore the overdraft fee cap: reinstate the CFPB rule capping bank overdraft fees at $5, rescinded by the Trump administration in 2025; estimated consumer savings: $5B annually

**Sec. 5 — Student Loan System Reform**
- Income-driven repayment at 5% of discretionary income (Biden SAVE plan framework, legislatively codified to prevent future reversal)
- Expand and strengthen Public Service Loan Forgiveness: all public-sector and nonprofit workers qualify; eliminate the categorical exclusions that denied forgiveness to tens of thousands of eligible workers
- Restore and codify broad student loan debt cancellation authority: $50,000 per borrower for Pell Grant recipients; $25,000 for other borrowers with high debt-to-income ratios (addressing OBBBA student loan restrictions)
- Constrain for-profit college predation: restore gainful employment rules; restore state oversight primacy; ban recruiting tactics that target veterans' GI Bill benefits

### IV.4.5 The Build America Fast Act — Federal Permitting Reform

**The gap nobody talks about:** The Project 2029 agenda proposes building enormous things — 2 million affordable housing units, 15,000 miles of new transmission lines, national high-speed rail, broadband for every American, school buildings in poor districts, clean energy facilities across the country. None of this can happen at the required speed without confronting the most consequential and least discussed obstacle: the American permitting system, which has made us one of the slowest countries in the wealthy world at building anything.

Major clean energy projects currently take 5-10 years to permit. Transmission lines face multi-state gauntlets where any state can veto a line that benefits the entire region. Housing construction is blocked by environmental review processes that function as NIMBY veto tools. This is not primarily an environmental problem — the vast majority of permitting delays do not produce better environmental outcomes; they produce the same outcome, later, at higher cost.

Permitting reform is politically complex: environmental organizations that should be allies of clean energy and affordable housing have sometimes defended permitting processes as environmental protections. The framework must be direct: the choice is not between environmental protection and fast permitting; it is between a system that produces good environmental outcomes efficiently and one that produces the same outcomes expensively and slowly while enriching delay-as-veto practitioners.

**Sec. P.1 — Clean Energy Categorical Exclusions**
- Establish categorical exclusions from lengthy NEPA review for:
  - Solar and wind installations on previously disturbed land or in pre-approved zones
  - Transmission line upgrades within existing rights-of-way
  - Battery storage co-located with existing generation facilities
  - Rooftop solar and distributed generation
- Full NEPA review retained for: first-of-kind projects in sensitive ecosystems; projects requiring new rights-of-way through undisturbed habitat; offshore development in sensitive marine environments
- The principle: NEPA was designed to prevent harm, not to delay the prevention of harm from climate change

**Sec. P.2 — Transmission Permitting Federalization**
- Grant FERC primary siting authority for interstate transmission lines determined to be in the national interest, overriding state PUC proceedings that currently allow any state along a route to veto a nationally essential project
- Retain: meaningful environmental review; landowner compensation rights; consultation with affected communities
- Remove: single-state veto power over multi-state infrastructure with national significance
- 24-month shot clock for all FERC permitting decisions on nationally significant transmission

**Sec. P.3 — Housing Permitting Reform**
- One Federal Decision for housing: a single federal agency is designated lead for all federal environmental and regulatory approvals for major housing developments receiving federal funding; concurrent rather than sequential review; 18-month maximum review period
- Stronger exclusionary zoning preemption: any local zoning ordinance that prohibits multi-family housing as-of-right within 1/2 mile of a transit stop, or that prohibits ADUs on any residential lot, is void as a matter of federal law for projects receiving federal funding. States may preserve these restrictions only by forgoing federal housing, transportation, and infrastructure grants.
- Land value taxation pilots: fund states and localities to pilot land value taxes (taxing land at higher rates than structures) — the most economically sound mechanism for encouraging development on underused urban land

**Sec. P.4 — Infrastructure Shot Clocks**
- All federal permit decisions for federally-funded infrastructure projects: 18 months maximum from application to decision
- If a federal agency misses its shot clock, the project applicant may seek a writ of mandamus in federal district court compelling a decision
- One federal lead agency designated for all major infrastructure projects; other agencies provide comments but cannot independently block
- Fund federal and state permitting agencies to hire sufficient staff to meet these timelines — the bottleneck is often not political will but raw capacity

### IV.5 The Fair Trade and American Manufacturing Act

**Purpose:** Replace the tariff chaos with a coherent, worker-centered trade policy that rebuilds allied relationships, addresses Chinese economic aggression through multilateral coordination, and maintains strategic domestic manufacturing capacity — without imposing a regressive tax on American consumers.

#### Title I: The Post-Tariff Framework

**The Fundamental Principle:** Tariffs are a tax on American consumers and businesses, not on foreign countries. The correct tool for addressing unfair trade practices is coordinated action with allies, not unilateral tariffs that isolate America and allow trading partners to route around them. The tariff agenda failed by its own metrics — the U.S. trade deficit did not narrow, China adapted and found new markets, and the $175 billion in tariff revenues was paid by American households and businesses, not by foreign governments.

A Democratic trade policy is neither free-trade globalism nor Trumpian protectionism. It is **fair trade with rules that work for workers** — structured agreements that include enforceable labor standards, environmental requirements, currency manipulation prohibitions, and anti-dumping disciplines, enforced multilaterally rather than unilaterally.

**Sec. 1.1 — Tariff Unwinding**
- Immediately eliminate or reduce the Section 122 tariffs (10% on nearly all imports, expiring July 24, 2026) rather than allowing them to expire or be replaced with new tariffs
- Negotiate with allies (Canada, Mexico, EU, Japan, South Korea, Australia) to restore bilateral trade relationships damaged by Trump tariff wars; prioritize restoration of supply chain partnerships critical to clean energy and defense manufacturing
- Maintain Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum where domestic capacity serves genuine national security purposes — but discipline their scope to actual security-relevant production, not political payoffs to specific producers
- Process IEEPA tariff refunds promptly: the $166B in unconstitutional tariffs must be returned to the 330,000+ affected businesses; fund USCBP technology to process refunds within 180 days

**Sec. 1.2 — Allied Trade Architecture**
- Lead the formation of a "Democratic Trade Alliance": a coalition of like-minded democracies (EU, UK, Canada, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, and others) that agree to: common labor standards, common environmental standards, common technology export controls vis-à-vis China, and mutual market access — with enforcement through agreed dispute resolution mechanisms
- This is the correct alternative to both WTO-style free trade (insufficient standards) and unilateral tariffs (isolating and ineffective): multilateral standards-based trade among allies
- Re-engage the Trans-Pacific Partnership successor (CPTPP) with labor and environmental standard upgrades — the original TPP was the right strategic vision imperfectly executed; the Democratic version insists on enforceable core ILO labor standards as a condition of entry

**Sec. 1.3 — China Strategy**
- Maintain and strengthen technology export controls on advanced semiconductors, AI, and dual-use technologies — bipartisan consensus exists on this
- Do not maintain blanket consumer goods tariffs on China — they harm American consumers without changing Chinese behavior
- Pursue targeted Section 301 action against specific documented unfair practices (forced technology transfer, IP theft, state subsidies to specific industries) through WTO dispute settlement and multilateral coordination with allies
- Critical supply chain diversification: use the CHIPS Act model — domestic investment plus allied-country investment to reduce dependence on Chinese supply chains for critical minerals, batteries, and semiconductors — without the trade war taxes on everything else

#### Title II: Industrial Policy and Manufacturing

**Sec. 2.1 — Restore and Expand the IRA's Industrial Policy**
- The IRA's domestic content requirements, manufacturing tax credits, and supply chain investment provisions were the correct domestic manufacturing strategy — restore them fully (the Climate chapter covers the energy provisions)
- Expand domestic content requirements to: defense procurement, federal infrastructure projects, and healthcare equipment — a "Buy Clean and American" standard for all major federal procurement
- Extend the CHIPS Act semiconductor manufacturing investment program; fund R&D and production at the pre-commercial stages where private investment is insufficient

**Sec. 2.2 — Manufacturing Communities Investment Act**
- $100B over 10 years for communities that experienced deindustrialization since 1980: direct investment in manufacturing infrastructure, workforce training, broadband, and industrial site remediation
- Eligibility tied to documented manufacturing job loss relative to population; prioritizes the Rust Belt, Appalachia, and rural manufacturing communities
- Workforce training: fund sector-specific apprenticeship programs with union labor partners; fund community college technical programs aligned with specific employer needs
- This is the companion to the trade policy agenda: you cannot credibly ask manufacturing workers to accept trade liberalization unless you invest in the communities and workers most affected by it

**Sec. 2.3 — Agricultural Trade Protection**
- Restore and strengthen the farm income safety net: Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage programs; prioritize family farms over corporate agribusiness
- Negotiate market access for American agricultural exports in allied trade agreements; use tariff reciprocity to protect farmers from dumping
- Enforce antitrust law in agricultural markets: meatpacking (4 companies control 80%+ of beef processing), poultry, dairy, and grain — all are dramatically more concentrated than they were 30 years ago; restore the Grain Inspection, Packers and Stockyards Administration (GIPSA) rules that the Trump administration rescinded

## PART VI: THE POLITICAL STRATEGY

### VI.1 Natural Coalition

This agenda has genuine cross-partisan appeal in specific elements:
- **Credit card interest rate cap**: Trump himself endorsed this; pursue bipartisan passage via regular order
- **Antitrust against Big Tech**: bipartisan agreement exists; Klobuchar-Grassley model; pursue 60-vote path
- **Robinson-Patman for small grocers**: rural Republican senators whose small-town grocers are closing have direct constituency interest
- **Agricultural antitrust in meatpacking**: Republican farm state senators should support GIPSA rules
- **Postal banking**: Rural communities without bank branches are a bipartisan constituency
- **Non-compete ban**: Bipartisan legislative history; worker mobility benefits employers too

**Strategy:** Lead with the bipartisan elements; use early wins to build momentum for the harder partisan provisions (minimum wage, PRO Act, worker classification).

### VI.2 The Small Business Alliance

This agenda should be explicitly presented as pro-small business, not anti-business:
- Monopoly power harms small businesses more than any government regulation
- The Robinson-Patman revival directly protects small grocers, distributors, and retailers
- The Credit Card cap saves small businesses on processing costs
- The housing construction agenda creates work for small contractors
- The PRO Act and minimum wage, while opposed by some small business groups, actually level the playing field against large corporations that can absorb wage pressure better than small competitors

The Democratic Party should host a "small business antitrust summit" within the first 100 days — make clear that this agenda serves Main Street, not Wall Street.

### VI.3 The "Who Pays and Who Benefits" Accountability Frame

Every provision of this agenda should come with a specific answer to "who does this help and who does it cost":

| Provision | Who benefits | Who pays |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Antitrust enforcement | Consumers, small businesses, workers | Dominant corporations |
| $17→$19 minimum wage | 30M+ low-wage workers | Large employers (small employers phased) |
| PRO Act | Unionizing workers | Employers who use NLRB delays as union-busting tool |
| Algorithmic rent ban | 45M renter households | Corporate landlords + RealPage |
| Affordable housing construction | Renters, first-time buyers, construction workers | No one — this is new construction |
| Credit card interest cap | Card-carrying consumers | Credit card issuers (still profitable at 15%) |
| Glass-Steagall-lite | Taxpayers, depositors | Investment banking divisions of large banks |
| Fair trade policy | Consumers (lower prices), workers (stable supply chains) | Industries protected by specific Trump tariffs |
| Worker classification | Gig workers, platform economy workers | Platform companies (Uber, Lyft, DoorDash) |

## PART VII: FISCAL IMPACT

The economic fairness agenda has a different fiscal profile than the healthcare and climate chapters because much of it is regulatory and enforcement-driven, not spending-driven.

| Category | 10-Year Spending | 10-Year Revenue/Savings |
|----------|-----------------|------------------------|
| Affordable housing trust fund | $200B | — |
| High-speed rail | $300B | ~$50B in private investment leverage |
| Housing supply reforms (LIHTC, CLT) | $50B | — |
| First-generation homebuyer grants | $100B | — |
| Manufacturing communities | $100B | — |
| Antitrust enforcement build-up | $20B | Consumer savings: est. $100B+/year |
| Worker protection enforcement | $30B | Wage theft recovery: ~$20B/year |
| CFPB restoration | $5B | Consumer savings: $5B+/year |
| Postal banking | Self-funding | Eliminates payday lending subsidy |
| Carried interest loophole closure | Revenue item (Ch2) | ~$180B over 10 years |
| Credit card interest cap | Zero (regulatory) | Consumer savings: $12-18B/year |
| **Total net spending** | **~$800B** | — |

The $800B in spending for this chapter is consistent with the Chapter 2 fiscal framework, which allocated approximately $700-800B to infrastructure/housing. The manufacturing communities investment and high-speed rail are funded through the Fairness Reconciliation Bill (FY2031) paired with wealth tax revenue.

**The anti-monopoly agenda is self-financing in the aggregate.** Robust antitrust enforcement generates: higher consumer purchasing power (lower prices), higher wages (competitive labor markets), more entrepreneurship (lower barriers to entry), and higher tax revenues (distributed rather than concentrated profits). The CBO cannot score these benefits conventionally, but the economic literature consistently finds that market concentration depresses wages and raises prices by measurable and significant amounts.
