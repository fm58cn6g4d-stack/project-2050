## PREFATORY NOTE: EDUCATION AS ECONOMIC JUSTICE

Education policy is inseparable from economic policy. The K-12 school you attended was determined by your ZIP code — and your ZIP code was largely determined by your parents' income and race. Whether you could afford college, and whether you could afford to pay back what you borrowed, has determined the trajectory of your economic life. The teacher shortage, the school funding gap, the student debt crisis, and the dismantling of the Department of Education are all manifestations of the same underlying dynamic: a society that professes to believe in equal opportunity while systematically engineering unequal outcomes.

This chapter is organized around four distinct but connected domains:

1. **Restoring and rebuilding the Department of Education** — the institutional foundation without which nothing else works
2. **K-12 equity funding** — closing the gap between rich and poor school districts that has defined American education for generations
3. **Early childhood — universal pre-K** — the highest-return educational investment available
4. **Higher education and student debt** — restoring access, fixing the loan system, and addressing the crisis that is crushing 43 million Americans

## PART I: THE DAMAGE INVENTORY

### I.1 The Dismantling of the Department of Education

The Trump administration has spent its second term executing the Project 2025 playbook on education with remarkable efficiency. The damage as of March 2026:

**Workforce destruction:** In 2025, ED's workforce declined by about 1,700 employees, reducing the agency by almost half relative to the day Trump took office. Trump signed an executive order on March 20, 2025 telling Education Secretary McMahon to put herself out of a job and terminate all programs besides "core necessities." The result: thousands of layoffs, program cancellations, and a gutted agency trying to implement sweeping OBBBA changes with half the staff.

**Institutional dispersion:** The administration has reached ten interagency agreements to disperse large swaths of the Education Department's work to other agencies. Most dramatically, on March 19, 2026 — four days before this chapter was written — the Treasury Department announced it would take over management of student loans in default ($180 billion, 11% of the $1.7 trillion portfolio), with a second phase eventually moving all student loans to Treasury. This is legally questionable — federal law requires student loans to be overseen by the Education Department — but is advancing regardless.

**Civil rights enforcement collapsed:** The Office of Civil Rights was hit hard by the reduction-in-force announcements in March 2025, losing more than half of its staff and closing 7 of its 12 regional offices. OCR's usual work to investigate and resolve civil rights complaints for students with disabilities was deprioritized or ignored for months. Simultaneously, the administration weaponized OCR's funding-withholding authority against institutions for political reasons — withholding funds without substantive investigation or required procedures, targeting universities over DEI programs, gender-affirming care policies, and immigration-related activities.

**Billions in grants terminated:** Teacher training grants cut by hundreds of millions; community schools grants ($167M+) cut; English learner professional development grants cut; desegregation incentive grants eliminated; arts education grants cut. EdWeek documented the termination or discontinuation of billions of dollars in specific grant programs affecting schools in all 50 states. "What we're seeing right now is a generational change. I think it will be, could very well be, decades before there's a sufficient return to the levels and kinds of activity and federal assistance and role in education that we were accustomed to until January 2025," said Robert Kim, Executive Director of the Education Law Center.

**The legal reality check:** Despite all of this, the Trump administration cannot fully abolish the Department of Education without Congress. The Department of Education Organization Act of 1979 requires another act of Congress — with 60 Senate votes — to shut down the Department or terminate its core functions. Democrats' position should be unequivocal: the Department of Education is not being abolished, it is being restored.

### I.2 The OBBBA's Higher Education Overhaul

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act rewrote the federal student loan system from the ground up, imposing sweeping changes that will take full effect beginning July 1, 2026:

**Income-driven repayment gutted:**
- The SAVE plan — into which nearly 8 million borrowers enrolled — was eliminated; those borrowers have been in involuntary forbearance for nearly two years while legal challenges proceeded; a federal appeals court ordered SAVE's early end in March 2026
- All income-driven repayment plans except IBR sunset by July 1, 2028
- The new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) requires 30 years of payments before loan forgiveness — double the 15-year path under SAVE — and forgiveness becomes taxable income
- As of June 2025, more than a quarter of student loan borrowers (10.3 million) had at least one loan in forbearance and another 6 million were delinquent or facing default; about 9.2 million Americans are in default on student loans

**Borrowing caps:**
- Grad PLUS loans eliminated entirely as of July 1, 2026
- Graduate students capped at $20,500/year ($100,000 aggregate lifetime)
- Professional students (medical, law, dental) capped at $50,000/year ($200,000 lifetime)
- Parent PLUS loans capped at $20,000/year per child ($65,000 lifetime) with no income-driven repayment for new loans

**PSLF weaponized:**
- The Trump administration amended the definition of "qualifying employer" for Public Service Loan Forgiveness to exclude organizations engaged in what the Secretary characterizes as "substantial illegal activities" — targeted at nonprofits and government entities involved in immigration services, abortion care, gender-affirming care, or DEI programs
- This rule is subject to multiple lawsuits but represents a direct effort to weaponize student debt relief against political opponents

**Student loan management transferred to Treasury:**
- The March 19, 2026 announcement moves $180 billion in defaulted loans to Treasury immediately, with all student loans to follow
- Treasury's 2015 pilot of loan collection achieved lower success rates than private collection agencies contracted by Education — this is administrative disruption masquerading as reform

### I.3 K-12: The Pre-Existing Inequity Crisis

Before OBBBA, K-12 education in America was already defined by profound and measurable inequality:

- The average spending gap between the wealthiest and poorest school districts is approximately $12,000 per pupil annually — wealthy districts spend nearly twice what poor districts spend
- This gap correlates almost perfectly with race and geography: predominantly Black and Latino districts, and rural poor-white districts, are systematically underfunded relative to wealthy suburban districts
- Title I — the federal program providing supplemental funding to high-poverty schools — provides approximately $17 billion annually but covers only a fraction of the resource gap
- The teacher shortage crisis, predating OBBBA, has reached emergency proportions: 300,000+ teacher vacancies nationally; teacher pay has fallen in real terms relative to other college-educated professions over the past 30 years; Trump's teacher training grant cuts accelerated this crisis

### I.4 Early Childhood: The Unmet Investment

Universal pre-K remains one of the largest unmet policy opportunities in American education:
- Fewer than half of American 3- and 4-year-olds are enrolled in a publicly funded early childhood program
- The economic return on high-quality early childhood investment is estimated at $7-12 per dollar invested, driven by reduced special education costs, higher lifetime earnings, reduced incarceration, and improved public health outcomes
- The childcare system is simultaneously unaffordable for families (average childcare costs exceed $16,000/year nationally) and unsustainable for providers (median childcare worker earns $14/hour)
- Universal Pre-K and childcare stabilization were the most significant education provisions that never made it into Democratic legislation during the Biden years — the Build Back Better failure left this as unfinished business

## PART II: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

**Education as infrastructure.** The framework treats education investment as infrastructure investment — with comparable long-run economic returns, comparable public goods characteristics, and comparable rationale for federal investment. A country that underinvests in its schools is underinvesting in its future workforce, its innovation capacity, and its democratic citizenship. This is not a liberal position — it is a national competitiveness position.

**Federal role: equity and access, not uniformity.** The federal government's appropriate role in education is not to dictate curriculum, manage daily school operations, or override local and state authority on most educational questions. It is to: (1) ensure that every child has access to adequately funded education regardless of local property tax wealth; (2) protect civil rights in educational settings; (3) ensure that higher education is accessible regardless of family income; and (4) maintain the federal student loan system as a public good, not a debt trap.

**The workforce pipeline connection.** Education policy cannot be separated from workforce policy. The clean energy chapter needs electricians and engineers. The healthcare chapter needs nurses, physicians, and community health workers. The housing chapter needs construction workers and architects. Education investment is supply-side economic policy — it produces the workers the new economy needs. This connection should be explicit in every education communication.

## PART IV: THE LEGISLATIVE AGENDA

### IV.1 The Department of Education Restoration and Modernization Act

**Pathway:** Regular order (partially) and reconciliation (funding provisions)

**Sec. 1 — Restore the Department**
- Immediately rescind all interagency agreements dispersing Education Department functions to other agencies
- Direct Treasury to return student loan management to the Department of Education within 180 days; rescind the March 2026 transfer agreement pending legislation
- Restore and fully fund the Office of Civil Rights: restore all staff positions eliminated in the March 2025 RIF; reopen all 12 regional offices; eliminate the backlog of civil rights complaints
- Restore the Office of Special Education Programs to full operational capacity; restore compliance monitoring for IDEA
- Restore all ED offices and programs to pre-January 2025 staffing and funding levels as a floor, not a ceiling

**Sec. 2 — Restore Civil Rights Enforcement**
- Restore Title IX protections: reinstate the Biden-era Title IX regulations protecting LGBTQ+ students; restore protections against sexual harassment and assault in schools; restore the Dear Colleague letters gutted by Trump
- Restore the Affirmatively Furthering Fair Housing guidance to Title VI enforcement in schools
- Prohibit the use of OCR funding-withholding authority as a political weapon: require substantive investigation, due process, and compliance with established procedures before any funding withholding
- Restore the ED's data collection on school discipline, chronic absenteeism, and civil rights compliance — data that was suppressed or discontinued

**Sec. 3 — Restore and Reform PSLF**
- Rescind the October 2025 rule redefining "qualifying employer" to exclude organizations involved in immigration, abortion, or DEI activities; restore the traditional qualifying employer definition
- Provide retroactive PSLF credit for all months in which borrowers in qualifying employment were placed in forbearance due to SAVE litigation — these months should count toward PSLF even though payments were not made through no fault of the borrower
- Expand PSLF qualifying employment: all state and local government, federal government, and 501(c)(3) nonprofit employment qualifies, period; eliminate the bureaucratic distinctions that have denied PSLF credit to eligible borrowers for years

### IV.2 The Student Debt Relief and Loan Reform Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (spending and revenue provisions)

This is the most politically charged education provision and the one with the largest fiscal impact. The framework is designed to be both meaningful and honest about cost.

**Sec. 1 — Targeted Debt Relief**
- $50,000 cancellation for all Pell Grant recipients: these are students who demonstrated financial need in order to receive the grant; their subsequent debt burden is the most egregious example of a system that gives with one hand and takes with the other
- $25,000 cancellation for all other federal student loan borrowers with annual income below $125,000 (single) / $250,000 (married)
- Address the OBBBA's "tax bomb": restore the tax exclusion for student loan forgiveness; forgiven debt should not be treated as taxable income
- Relief from the OBBBA forbearance harm: restore credit toward IDR forgiveness for the months that SAVE borrowers were in involuntary forbearance; cancel accumulated interest for those periods

**Sec. 2 — Repayment System Restoration**
- Restore income-driven repayment at 5% of discretionary income (a simplified, improved version of SAVE's approach) for all borrowers
- Restore 10-20 year forgiveness timelines: 10 years for borrowers with undergraduate-only debt below $20,000; 20 years for other borrowers; 25 years for graduate/professional borrowers
- Restore interest subsidy: cap total repayment at the original loan principal — no borrower should ever repay more than they borrowed due to interest accumulation
- Restore Grad PLUS loans with a reformed structure: aggregate lifetime limits rather than annual limits, combined with institutional accountability measures
- Restore Parent PLUS loans with income-based repayment eligibility

**Sec. 3 — Institutional Accountability**
- The OBBBA's "gainful employment" earnings test has a legitimate core: programs should demonstrate that graduates can earn enough to repay what they borrowed. But the implementation is flawed and targets community colleges and credential programs disproportionately
- Replace the OBBBA test with a stronger accountability framework: disclosure of median graduate earnings and debt-to-income ratios; warning flags for programs with poor outcomes; loss of federal funding eligibility only after documented, persistent failure to produce viable economic outcomes; apply equally to for-profit, nonprofit, and public institutions
- Restore the Borrower Defense to Repayment rule: students who were defrauded by their institution have a right to loan cancellation; restore the Biden-era rule gutted by Trump
- Restore the gainful employment rule as applied to for-profit institutions specifically — where the documented predatory practices are most severe

**Fiscal cost note:** The $50,000 Pell Grant relief and $25,000 general relief represent a combined one-time cost of approximately $700B-$1T. This is consistent with the Chapter 2 fiscal framework and the FY2031 Fairness Reconciliation Bill allocation. The ongoing repayment reform (5% IDR restoration) reduces future federal revenue from loan repayment but is partially offset by reduced default rates — net cost approximately $200-300B over 10 years.

### IV.3 The Free Community College and Vocational Training Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (spending provision)

**Core provisions:**
- Universal tuition-free community college for all students at public two-year institutions, regardless of income; includes associate degrees, vocational certificates, and workforce training programs
- Expand Pell Grant eligibility to cover short-term credential programs (the "Workforce Pell" concept from OBBBA has merit — the mechanism not the rest of the bill)
- Restore and expand Pell Grant maximum award: increase from current ~$7,000 to $10,000 to restore purchasing power lost to inflation since the program's inception
- Fund apprenticeship expansion: double federal apprenticeship investment to create 1 million new registered apprenticeships per year in construction, healthcare, clean energy, and technology
- Fund community college capacity: $50B over 10 years for capital investment in community college facilities, technology, and faculty that have been neglected for decades
- Connect to workforce needs: require community colleges receiving federal investment to develop employer partnership agreements ensuring programs align with local labor market needs

**Political design:** Free community college is simultaneously a progressive policy goal and a rural economic development tool — community colleges are often the only higher education institution within reasonable distance of rural communities and small towns. It should be presented as an economic development measure as much as an education measure.

### IV.4 The School Funding Equity Act

**Pathway:** Regular order or reconciliation (federal formula changes affect spending)

This is the structural K-12 equity reform that has been discussed and deferred for decades. It requires political courage because it reallocates federal resources toward less affluent districts, which means reducing relative federal advantage for wealthy districts whose congressional representatives will resist.

**Sec. 1 — Reform the Title I Formula**
- The current Title I formula is complex, involves multiple distribution methods, and has been criticized for failing to direct resources to the highest-need districts with sufficient concentration
- Replace the four overlapping distribution formulas with a single, simplified formula based on: (1) number of students in poverty; (2) weighted for concentrated poverty (school-level poverty rates above 40% receive additional weighting); (3) with a hold-harmless provision that prevents any district from losing current funding in the first 5 years
- Increase total Title I funding from $17 billion to $30 billion annually over 5 years — meaningful equity reform requires more resources, not just redistribution

**Sec. 2 — Teacher Pay and Recruitment**
- Fund a National Teacher Pay Floor: federal matching grants to states that raise their minimum teacher salary to $60,000 by 2030 and $75,000 by 2033; this addresses the teacher shortage at its root — compensation that fails to attract and retain talented people
- Restore and expand Teacher Quality Partnership Grants (eliminated by Trump): university-district partnerships for teacher preparation
- Create a Rural Teacher Recruitment Program: loan forgiveness and housing subsidies for teachers who commit to 5-year placements in rural school districts — addressing the specific teacher desert problem in rural America
- Restore Grow Your Own programs that recruit and train community members as teachers — the most effective pipeline for teachers of color in their own communities

**Sec. 3 — School Infrastructure**
- Authorize a $100B federal school infrastructure bond program: many school buildings — particularly in low-income districts — are structurally inadequate, mold-infested, and unhealthy for students and teachers
- Priority for schools in districts that cannot fund infrastructure repairs through local property taxes
- Integrate clean energy and climate resilience: new school construction must meet green building standards; existing schools receive priority for weatherization and solar installation

**Sec. 4 — Special Education Full Funding**
- The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act was passed in 1975 with a promise that the federal government would pay 40% of the additional cost of educating students with disabilities; the federal government has never paid more than 20%; the gap is currently approximately $20B annually
- Commit to a 10-year phase-in reaching 40% federal IDEA funding — honoring a 50-year-old commitment that has been broken every single year

### IV.5 The Universal Pre-K and Child Care Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (federal spending program)

**Sec. 1 — Universal Pre-K**
- Federal-state partnership to provide universal free, high-quality pre-K for all 3- and 4-year-olds whose families choose to participate
- Design: states that opt in receive federal funding covering 90% of cost in Year 1, declining to 60% by Year 5 as states build capacity; all participating states must meet federal quality standards (class size, teacher credentials, curriculum standards)
- Federal quality standards: lead teacher must have at minimum an associate degree in early childhood education by Year 3; class size maximum 18 with at minimum one assistant teacher; developmentally appropriate curriculum
- Estimated cost: approximately $40-60B annually at full implementation; 10-year cost of $300-400B (consistent with Chapter 2 allocation)
- Implementation model: build on and expand existing state pre-K programs in states with strong track records (New Jersey, Oklahoma, West Virginia — the non-partisan credibility of universal pre-K in red states is a political asset)

**Sec. 2 — Child Care Stabilization**
- The child care market is broken from both ends: providers cannot charge enough to pay staff living wages; families cannot afford what providers need to charge to stay open. The result is a system that serves neither workers nor families adequately.
- $20B annually in child care stabilization grants to states: fund childcare provider operating costs to close the gap between what families can afford and what quality care actually costs
- Child care worker wage floor: providers receiving federal stabilization grants must pay child care workers a minimum of $20/hour; phase in over 3 years
- Universal child care subsidy: cap family child care costs at 7% of income for families below 250% of the federal poverty level; sliding scale to 150% FPL for families up to 85% of state median income
- Build on the Child Care and Development Block Grant with substantially increased funding and quality requirements attached to federal dollars

## PART VI: AGENCY REBUILDING PLAN

The Department of Education requires urgent institutional restoration before it can implement this agenda. The damage — 1,700 employees lost, 10 functions dispersed, systems disrupted — is significant but reversible:

**Year 1 priorities:**
- Restore Federal Student Aid staff: 500 immediate hires in loan servicing, oversight, and student support
- Restore OCR: 300 civil rights investigators reinstated or newly hired; 7 regional offices fully reopened
- Restore Office of Special Education Programs: 200 staff for compliance monitoring and technical assistance
- Reverse all interagency transfers: student loans returned to ED from Treasury; Office of Elementary and Secondary Education programs returned from wherever dispersed

**Critical system restoration:**
- Federal Student Aid IT systems: OBBBA's complexity, combined with staffing cuts, has created processing backlogs affecting millions of borrowers; IT modernization investment is essential — fund a dedicated $2B over 5 years for FSA technology modernization
- FAFSA: The simplification of FAFSA under the 2020 FAFSA Simplification Act was positive; the Trump administration's plans to potentially transfer FAFSA to Treasury must be reversed; FAFSA should remain at ED with a well-funded, staffed processing operation

## PART VII: POLITICAL STRATEGY

### VII.1 The Natural Coalition

Education has the broadest natural coalition of any domestic policy area:
- **Parents** (universal constituency): childcare and pre-K directly affect their lives and wallets; school quality affects every family; the teacher shortage is felt in communities of all types
- **Teachers and education workers**: one of the largest organized labor constituencies; directly affected by pay, training, and civil rights enforcement
- **Student loan borrowers**: 43 million people; disproportionately young adults, Black and Latino borrowers (who borrowed more and earned less return due to labor market discrimination), and graduate/professional students
- **Rural communities**: teacher shortages, rural school consolidation, and community college access are rural issues; Republicans have taken rural educational resources for granted
- **Business community**: businesses are vocal about workforce skill gaps; connecting education investment to workforce pipeline gives the agenda a competitiveness frame that reaches persuadable business-oriented voters

### VII.2 The Bipartisan Opening

Several education provisions have genuine bipartisan potential:
- **Special education full funding**: the 40% IDEA commitment was a bipartisan promise; Republicans who represent districts with high special education needs have constituent interest in fulfilling it
- **Rural teacher recruitment**: rural school districts in Republican states are facing teacher shortages; targeted federal investment with rural-friendly framing can attract Republican co-sponsors
- **Apprenticeship expansion**: workforce training and apprenticeship have strong Republican support in manufacturing and trades communities; frame as vocational education, not college preparation
- **PSLF restoration (narrow version)**: public school teachers, police officers, firefighters, and military veterans are PSLF-eligible; Republican senators from states with these communities have constituent interest in PSLF stability

### VII.3 The Student Debt Political Management

Student debt relief is the most politically contentious provision in this chapter — not because it lacks public support (polls show majority support for targeted relief), but because opponents characterize it as unfair to people who didn't go to college or already paid their loans. The responses:

> "If your roof collapses because the contractor who built it fraudulently cut corners, and we make the contractor fix it, that doesn't punish the neighbor who had a good contractor. This isn't about punishing people who paid their loans or didn't take them — it's about fixing a system that was predatory and broken."

> "We are not giving away money. We are correcting a decades-long policy failure that sent an entire generation into debt for an education that was supposed to be their path to the middle class. Every economist who has studied this finds that the economic drag of this debt — on home purchases, on business formation, on family formation — costs the entire economy, not just the borrowers."
