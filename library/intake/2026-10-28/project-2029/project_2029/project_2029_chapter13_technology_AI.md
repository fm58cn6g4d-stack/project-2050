## PREFATORY NOTE: THE MOST CONSEQUENTIAL GOVERNANCE CHALLENGE OF THE NEXT DECADE

Every chapter in this framework addresses a specific domain of American life — healthcare, climate, housing, education. This chapter is different: it addresses the technology that will reshape all of those domains simultaneously, in ways that are partially visible today and partially unknowable even to the people building it.

Artificial intelligence is not a sector. It is an infrastructure technology — like electricity or the internet — that will reorganize labor markets, healthcare delivery, education, democratic participation, national security, and the distribution of economic power more profoundly than any single policy in this document. The question is not whether AI transforms everything. It already is. The question is: in whose interest is it governed, who bears the costs of its disruption, and who captures its gains?

The Trump administration's answer to that question has been explicit and consequential: the largest technology companies, with the most political access, should govern AI themselves — with the federal government as junior partner and the states stripped of authority to regulate it. The Project 2029 answer is different: AI must be governed in the public interest, with democratic accountability, in a framework that promotes innovation and distributes its benefits widely rather than concentrating them in a small number of already-dominant firms.

This chapter covers five interconnected domains:
1. **AI governance framework** — the rules for building and deploying AI responsibly
2. **Worker transition and economic adaptation** — who pays the cost of AI-driven displacement and who captures the gains
3. **Data privacy and digital rights** — fundamental rights in a data economy
4. **Social media and information environment** — democracy in the algorithmic era
5. **Technology competition and national security** — the U.S.-China AI race and what winning actually requires

## PART I: THE DAMAGE INVENTORY — TRUMP'S AI GOVERNANCE

### I.1 The "Deregulation" That Isn't

The Trump administration's approach to AI governance is one of the most consequential — and most mischaracterized — policy choices of the second term. One of the most interventionist approaches to technology governance in the United States in a generation has cloaked itself in the language of deregulation.

What actually happened:

**January 23, 2025:** Trump issued EO 14179 "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," replacing Biden's comprehensive AI safety executive order. The Trump EO signals a significant shift away from the Biden administration's emphasis on oversight, risk mitigation and equity toward a framework centered on deregulation and the promotion of AI innovation.

**July 2025:** Trump signed an EO preventing the federal government from using AI models that include "ideological biases or social agendas" — effectively politicizing government AI procurement to exclude models that acknowledge documented racial disparities or other empirically grounded findings that conflict with the administration's ideology.

**December 11, 2025:** Trump signed the EO "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence," aiming to limit and pre-empt state-level regulation of AI. The EO directs the Attorney General to establish an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws, and directs the Secretary of Commerce to withhold BEAD broadband funding from any state with AI laws conflicting with national policy priorities.

The December EO specifically: mobilizes the DOJ to identify and challenge "onerous" state AI laws, discourages state enactment and enforcement of AI laws that conflict with federal policy — including by conditioning federal broadband funding on policy alignment — and advances federal preemption through litigation and agency action.

The EO cites Colorado's "algorithmic discrimination" law as a specific example of a state law at odds with the EO's policy, arguing the law could pressure models to produce "false results" to avoid differential treatment. An attempt to include a 10-year moratorium on new state AI laws in the NDAA failed, creating the impetus for the president to sign the order.

**The industrial policy dimension:** In August 2025, the U.S. Department of Commerce became the largest shareholder of Intel, acquiring a 9.9 percent stake in exchange for $8.9 billion in funding. The administration has since moved to take equity positions across semiconductors, critical minerals, and nuclear energy — committing more than $10 billion to ownership stakes in at least nine firms in six months. What distinguishes the current approach is not industrial policy itself but how it operates — circumventing ordinary accountability mechanisms in favor of discretionary power.

**The true nature of what happened:** The Trump administration didn't remove government from AI governance. It removed democratic accountability from AI governance. The states — the most responsive and innovative regulatory laboratories in the American system — are being stripped of authority. The Biden AI safety executive order, which required major AI developers to share safety testing results with the government and established standards for avoiding catastrophic risks, was revoked. Meanwhile, the administration is making multi-billion-dollar discretionary investment decisions in specific technology companies with no congressional approval and no transparent process. This is not deregulation. It is the capture of AI governance by the industry being governed.

### I.2 What the Biden AI Framework Did (That Must Be Restored)

Biden's October 2023 AI Executive Order — revoked on Trump's first day — established:
- Mandatory safety testing and government reporting for frontier AI models above defined compute thresholds
- Standards for AI watermarking and synthetic content labeling
- Protections against discriminatory AI in housing, credit, and federal benefits
- Federal agency guidance for responsible AI use
- International AI safety coordination with allied nations
- An AI Safety Institute at NIST to conduct independent safety research

All of this was gone within 24 hours of Trump's inauguration.

### I.3 The Worker Displacement Reality

While the governance debate has focused on safety and competition, the most immediate AI impact on most Americans is economic disruption:
- Goldman Sachs estimates AI could automate tasks equivalent to 300 million full-time jobs globally over the coming decade, with the U.S. among the most affected economies
- Unlike previous technological transitions (agricultural to industrial, industrial to service), AI-driven displacement is projected to hit white-collar and cognitive work — including many jobs in healthcare administration, legal services, financial services, customer support, and media — not primarily blue-collar manufacturing
- The communities and workers who receive the most political attention in the economic fairness chapter (non-college manufacturing workers) are not the primary first-wave displacement targets; but mid-level knowledge workers, who are politically less visible, face significant disruption
- The gains from AI productivity improvements are flowing primarily to capital owners and the highest-skilled workers, while the displacement costs fall on middle-skill workers — widening inequality in the absence of policy response

## PART II: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

### II.1 The Core Frame: Governance in the Public Interest

**AI is infrastructure, not just commerce.** The Internet became infrastructure. Electricity became infrastructure. Telecommunications became infrastructure. In each case, society eventually decided that the transformative importance and natural monopoly characteristics of the technology required governance in the public interest — not just market competition. AI is on the same trajectory, and the decisions made in the next five years about who owns it, how it is trained, how it is deployed, and who bears accountability for its failures will shape the distribution of its benefits for decades.

**Innovation and accountability are not opposites.** The Trump framing — that any AI governance is a threat to innovation — is wrong empirically (the EU's GDPR has not prevented European technological development; Europe has produced global champions in AI applications despite strong data protection) and wrong analytically (the AI companies most concerned about safety regulation are typically the incumbents who benefit from regulatory barriers to entry, not the startups who would benefit from clear rules). Responsible governance enables sustainable innovation; unaccountable deployment of powerful AI creates backlash that eventually produces far more restrictive responses.

**The distribution of AI's gains is a policy choice.** AI will make the economy more productive. Who captures that productivity gain is not determined by markets alone — it is determined by tax policy, labor law, intellectual property rules, education investment, and competition policy. The Project 2029 framework ensures that AI's productivity gains are distributed broadly: through worker transition support, through taxation of AI-generated profits to fund public investment, and through competition policy that prevents a small number of companies from capturing monopoly rents from AI dominance.

### II.2 The Three Principles

**Safety is not optional.** AI systems powerful enough to automate significant portions of the economy are powerful enough to cause significant harm — through discrimination, through security vulnerabilities, through manipulation of information environments, through potentially catastrophic misuse in biological, chemical, or cyber domains. A framework that requires safety testing, transparency, and accountability is not optional. The question is only who sets the standards and who enforces them.

**State innovation must be protected.** The states have been the most productive laboratory for technology governance: California's privacy law (CCPA) became the de facto national standard; Colorado's algorithmic discrimination law addressed documented harms; multiple states have prohibited algorithmic rent price-fixing (Chapter 7). The Trump administration's preemption of state AI governance removes the most democratically responsive layer of protection. Democrats must defend state authority as vigorously as Republicans have defended it in other domains.

**Workers must share in the gains.** AI will generate enormous wealth. The question is whether that wealth flows primarily to shareholders and founders or whether workers — including those whose labor trained the AI systems — share meaningfully in the value created. This requires: worker transition programs, profit-sharing requirements, data labor compensation, and tax policy that captures AI-generated returns for public investment.

## PART IV: THE LEGISLATIVE AND REGULATORY AGENDA

### IV.1 The Artificial Intelligence Accountability and Safety Act

**Pathway:** Regular order (bipartisan potential on safety and transparency); reconciliation for funding components

This is the most consequential and most complex piece of legislation in the chapter. It must thread the needle between genuine safety requirements and innovation-hostile overregulation — and it must be designed to survive both Republican deregulatory attacks and tech-industry lobbying.

**Title I — Frontier AI Safety Requirements**

**Sec. 1.1 — Safety Testing Mandate**
- Restore and strengthen the Biden EO's safety testing requirement: any AI model trained using more than a defined compute threshold (to be updated regularly by NIST as computing power evolves) must undergo mandatory safety testing before deployment
- Testing must cover: dangerous capabilities (bioweapons uplift, cyberattack assistance, chemical weapons synthesis guidance); discriminatory outputs in high-stakes decisions; security vulnerabilities; susceptibility to manipulation
- Results reported to the AI Safety Institute and made available to Congress and the public in summary form; proprietary implementation details protected as trade secrets
- Independent testing required: no model can be certified solely on the developer's own safety assessment; third-party evaluation required for highest-risk models

**Sec. 1.2 — AI Safety Institute Restoration and Expansion**
- Restore NIST's AI Safety Institute (defunded/dismantled under Trump) with mandatory, independent federal charter
- Fund to $2B over 5 years: hire 500 technical staff; establish red-team testing infrastructure; develop and maintain safety benchmarks
- International coordination: AI Safety Institute to serve as U.S. hub for international AI safety coordination with the UK, EU, Japan, and other allied nations
- Independent governance: AI Safety Institute director confirmed by Senate; cannot be removed by the President except for cause; insulated from political direction on technical findings

**Title II — High-Risk AI Applications**

**Sec. 2.1 — Prohibited Uses**
Federal statutory prohibitions on the highest-risk AI applications:
- Social scoring systems: prohibit any federal, state, or local government use of AI systems that aggregate behavioral data to create general-purpose social scores affecting access to public services
- Biometric surveillance: prohibit real-time facial recognition surveillance of public spaces by government agencies without a judicial warrant; prohibit law enforcement use of facial recognition as sole basis for arrest
- Manipulative AI: prohibit AI systems designed to exploit psychological vulnerabilities, addiction patterns, or emotional states to modify behavior without user awareness and consent (targeting children especially)
- AI in criminal sentencing and bail: prohibit use of AI risk assessment tools as binding or presumptive factors in sentencing; require explainability and independent audit of any AI tool used in criminal justice decisions

**Sec. 2.2 — High-Stakes Decision Requirements**
For AI systems making consequential decisions affecting individual rights or material interests (employment, credit, insurance, healthcare, housing, benefits eligibility, educational opportunities):
- Right to explanation: individuals must be informed when an AI system makes or materially influences a consequential decision affecting them; must receive meaningful explanation in plain language
- Right to human review: individuals have a right to request human review of any AI-generated adverse decision; human reviewer must have actual authority to override the AI system
- Algorithmic impact assessments: developers and deployers must conduct and publish annual assessments of their systems' differential impact across demographic groups
- Audit rights: the FTC, CFPB, and relevant sector regulators have authority to audit AI systems used in their regulated industries; subpoena authority for training data, model outputs, and impact assessments

**Title III — Restoring State Authority**

**Sec. 3.1 — Repeal Trump's AI Preemption Architecture**
- Repeal the December 2025 EO's preemption framework; restore state authority to regulate AI
- Explicit statutory rule: federal AI legislation establishes floors (minimum protections that all states must meet), not ceilings (maximum protections that states may not exceed); states may always provide stronger protection
- Restore BEAD broadband funding to states regardless of their AI regulatory choices; prohibit use of federal infrastructure funding as leverage against state consumer protection laws
- Disband the AI Litigation Task Force established to challenge state laws; direct DOJ to withdraw pending challenges

**Sec. 3.2 — Federal-State Coordination**
- Establish a Federal-State AI Coordination Council: representatives from state attorneys general, state consumer protection agencies, and federal regulators (FTC, CFPB, FDA, EEOC, HUD) to coordinate AI enforcement and share findings
- Fund state AI enforcement capacity: grants to state AGs and consumer protection offices to develop AI expertise

**Title IV — Competition in AI Markets**

**Sec. 4.1 — AI-Specific Antitrust**
- Prohibit dominant AI platform companies (defined: above threshold compute ownership OR above threshold market share in foundation model licensing) from engaging in self-preferencing of their AI products in adjacent services they control
- Prohibit exclusive data arrangements that prevent AI companies from accessing publicly generated data that incumbents have monopolized through contractual lock-up
- Mandate data portability: users must be able to export their AI-generated content, conversation histories, and preference data in interoperable formats
- Require interoperability for AI agents that access third-party services: no platform may block AI agents from accessing services available to human users

**Sec. 4.2 — Open AI Research Investment**
- Establish a National AI Research Cloud: federal investment ($10B over 5 years) in shared computing infrastructure accessible to academic researchers, nonprofits, and startups who cannot afford frontier AI computing costs
- Fund open-source foundation models: federal grants for development of high-quality open-source AI models that are not controlled by any single commercial entity
- Expand NSF AI research funding to $5B annually (from current ~$800M)

### IV.2 The Worker Transition and AI Dividend Act

**Pathway:** Reconciliation (spending and tax provisions)

**The Core Problem:** AI is generating enormous productivity gains. Under current policy, those gains flow almost entirely to capital — to the shareholders of AI companies and the companies that deploy AI to reduce their labor costs. Workers whose jobs are displaced receive: their unemployment insurance (designed for short-term joblessness, not structural displacement); their existing job training programs (chronically underfunded and designed for the last generation's job transitions); and the general social safety net. This is inadequate for a transformation of this scale.

**Sec. 1 — AI Transition Fund**
- Establish a dedicated AI Worker Transition Fund: $200B over 10 years
- Funded by: a 0.5% tax on revenues from AI-enabled services above $1B annually; employer contributions from firms that deploy AI to reduce headcount by more than 10% in a 24-month period
- Benefits: extended unemployment insurance (24 months, not 26 weeks, for workers displaced by documented AI deployment); wage insurance for workers who must accept lower-paying jobs in transition (up to 50% of wage gap for 2 years); relocation assistance for workers whose geographic labor market is severely disrupted
- Worker notification: employers with 100+ employees deploying AI that will eliminate positions must provide 180 days advance notice (up from WARN Act's 60 days)

**Sec. 2 — AI Upskilling and Workforce Development**
- Fund a national AI literacy program: every American adult should have access to free, high-quality AI skills training through community colleges, libraries, and online platforms
- Partner with the Civilian Climate Corps (Chapter 8) to integrate AI skills training into service terms
- Expand apprenticeship programs specifically in AI-adjacent occupations: data annotation, AI system monitoring, prompt engineering, AI ethics auditing, AI-assisted healthcare and legal services
- Fund retraining for healthcare workers, legal staff, financial services workers, and administrative professionals in the specific AI-augmented workflows that are replacing traditional job functions — the goal is augmentation, not replacement, wherever possible

**Sec. 3 — Data Labor Compensation**
- AI systems are trained on data generated by human labor: text written by humans, images created by humans, code written by human engineers, music composed by human artists
- Establish a Data Labor Compensation Fund: AI companies whose systems are trained on publicly accessible human-generated content must contribute 1% of revenues above $500M to a fund distributed to registered content creators, artists, journalists, and other affected workers
- Copyright reform for the AI era: clarify that AI training on copyrighted work requires either licensing or opt-out compliance; prohibit AI systems from reproducing substantial portions of copyrighted works without licensing; fund a rapid dispute resolution system for copyright infringement claims against AI outputs

**Sec. 4 — AI Profit Sharing**
- Companies that deploy AI to reduce their labor costs by more than 20% over a 3-year period must implement profit-sharing with remaining employees: minimum 10% of productivity gain attributed to AI deployment shared with the workforce through approved mechanisms (ESOP, profit-sharing plan, wage increase)
- Report to the Department of Labor annually on AI-driven productivity gains and worker compensation relative to those gains

### IV.3 The Federal Data Privacy and Protection Act

**Pathway:** Regular order (strong bipartisan support exists; the challenge is breadth)

The United States is one of the few wealthy democracies without a comprehensive federal data privacy law. California's CCPA has de facto become the standard for U.S. companies — but it covers only California residents, and the Trump administration's December 2025 EO threatens to preempt even California's protections. The governance vacuum must be filled.

**Core Rights (establish in statute):**
- **Right to know:** Every person has the right to know what personal data any company holds about them, where it came from, and how it is being used
- **Right to correct:** Every person has the right to correct inaccurate personal data
- **Right to delete:** Every person has the right to request deletion of their personal data (with exceptions for legal compliance, public interest, and legitimate business records)
- **Right to portability:** Every person has the right to receive their personal data in a machine-readable format and transfer it to another service
- **Right to opt out:** Every person has the right to opt out of sale of their personal data, use of their data for targeted advertising, and use of their data to train AI systems
- **Right to non-discrimination:** No company may discriminate in the quality or price of services offered to consumers who exercise their privacy rights

**Enforcement:**
- Federal Trade Commission as primary enforcer: fund 500 additional FTC privacy enforcement staff
- Private right of action: for willful violations or security breaches, individuals may sue for $100-$1,000 per violation plus attorney fees
- State floor rule: federal law establishes minimum national standard; states may enact stronger protections (explicitly rejecting the Trump preemption model)
- Safe harbor for small businesses: businesses with under $25M in annual revenue and processing fewer than 100,000 consumer records are exempt from most requirements

**Special Protections:**
- Children under 16: consent of parent or guardian required for any data collection; no behavioral advertising targeting children under 16; no data sale involving children under 16; enhanced penalties for violations
- Sensitive categories: health data, financial data, precise geolocation, biometric data, immigration status, sexual orientation, and political affiliation receive heightened protection; opt-in consent required (not merely opt-out)
- Government data requests: companies must notify users when responding to government requests for their data unless specifically prohibited by law; regular transparency reports required

### IV.4 The Social Media Accountability Act

**Pathway:** Regular order (contested; Section 230 reform has bipartisan interest; specifics are contested)

**The Problem:** Social media platforms currently operate under Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, which gives them broad immunity from liability for content posted by users. This immunity made sense when platforms were passive conduits. It makes much less sense when platforms are active amplifiers — using sophisticated algorithms to select, rank, and recommend content in ways that demonstrably increase engagement with outrage and divisive content, spread disinformation faster than accurate information, and expose users (especially children) to harmful material.

The reform challenge is that Section 230 also protects a great deal of legitimate moderation, enables free expression, and supports the open Internet. Wholesale repeal would be worse than the status quo. Targeted reform is the correct approach.

**Sec. 1 — Algorithmic Transparency and Accountability**
- Require all large social media platforms (100M+ U.S. users) to:
  - Publish quarterly algorithmic transparency reports: how does the recommendation algorithm work; what signals increase or decrease amplification; what content categories receive differential treatment
  - Offer users a chronological feed option without algorithmic amplification — every user has the right to choose what they see
  - Conduct and publish annual algorithmic impact assessments: does the algorithm differentially amplify content to specific demographic groups; does it increase exposure to extremist or violent content over time
  - Share anonymized data with qualified academic researchers under appropriate data governance agreements
- Limit behavioral advertising targeting to users under 18: prohibit algorithmic targeting of minors based on inferred psychological states, interests derived from behavior, or sensitive categories

**Sec. 2 — Targeted Section 230 Reform**
Narrow but consequential modifications to Section 230 immunity:
- Remove immunity for algorithmic amplification of content that has been adjudicated by a court as defamatory, fraudulent, or incitement to imminent violence — platforms should not be immunized for their choice to actively amplify content they know (or should know) is unlawful
- Restore duty of care for children: platforms are not immune from claims that their design features (infinite scroll, push notifications, engagement loops) caused documented harm to minors; this creates incentive to design less addictive products for young users
- Maintain core Section 230 immunity for hosting and good-faith moderation: the reform is narrow and targeted, not a general repeal

**Sec. 3 — Disinformation and Election Integrity**
- Require labeling of AI-generated synthetic media (deepfakes) in political advertising: any political ad containing AI-generated or materially digitally manipulated media must prominently disclose this
- Prohibit foreign purchase of political advertising on U.S. social media platforms
- Fund a Digital Media Literacy Initiative: $500M over 5 years for K-12 and adult education on evaluating online sources and recognizing manipulation
- Restore and expand public media investment (NPR, PBS, local public radio and TV): strong independent public media is the best structural counter to disinformation ecosystems

### IV.5 AI in Government — Accountability and Opportunity

**The Threat Side (Addressed in Chapter 6):**
DOGE's use of AI systems to process sensitive government data without authorization, oversight, or audit represents the most acute near-term AI governance failure. Chapter 6 addresses this directly: mandate algorithmic impact assessments for government AI, require human review of AI-generated adverse decisions on benefits, prohibit AI-only determinations on individual rights matters.

**The Opportunity Side:**
Responsible AI deployment in government could dramatically improve service delivery, reduce fraud, and make government more responsive. The framework:

**Sec. 1 — Federal AI Procurement Standards**
- Establish mandatory AI procurement standards: every federal agency must evaluate AI systems for accuracy, bias, security vulnerabilities, and civil rights impact before deployment
- Prohibit procurement of AI systems from companies that refuse to provide training data provenance, bias testing results, or security assessments
- Restore science-based AI procurement: rescind the July 2025 EO prohibiting federal use of AI models with "ideological bias" — a provision that is functionally a politicization of government AI procurement

**Sec. 2 — Government AI for Public Benefit**
- Fund AI-enhanced public services in healthcare (disease surveillance, drug interactions, clinical decision support), tax enforcement (identifying high-wealth avoidance), benefits administration (reducing error rates and processing backlogs), and public safety (predictive maintenance of infrastructure, wildfire detection)
- All government AI deployments subject to: public notice and comment before deployment; civil rights impact assessment; Inspector General audit authority; annual performance reporting
- Open government AI: where feasible, government-developed AI tools should be released as open source for use by states, localities, nonprofits, and researchers

## PART VI: THE CHINA TECHNOLOGY COMPETITION

The technology competition with China is real and requires genuine Democratic engagement — not dismissal and not hysteria. The Project 2029 framework on China AI competition (Chapter 9 sets the strategic frame; this chapter implements the domestic side):

**What we maintain:**
- Advanced semiconductor export controls (bipartisan consensus; prevents China from acquiring chips needed for cutting-edge AI training and military AI applications)
- Investment screening through CFIUS for Chinese acquisition of U.S. AI companies, sensitive data companies, and critical infrastructure
- Research security requirements at universities receiving federal funding for AI research

**What we add:**
- Allied technology coordination: U.S. export controls are only effective if allies adopt equivalent standards; fund and lead a G7+ technology export coordination framework
- Critical AI infrastructure investment: the National AI Research Cloud (Section IV.1) and expanded NSF funding build U.S. AI research capacity that is not dependent on commercial incumbents
- Trusted AI certification: develop a "trusted AI" certification standard that allied nations can adopt as a trade requirement — giving U.S. and allied AI products a market advantage over untrusted alternatives

**What we reject:**
- Blanket technology decoupling: severing all U.S.-China scientific and technological exchange would harm U.S. research capacity more than China's
- Using AI governance as a China pretext to eliminate all AI safety standards: the Trump argument that safety regulation harms U.S. competitiveness against China is empirically false — trusted AI will win the global market; unaccountable AI will not

## PART VII: THE POLITICAL STRATEGY

### VII.1 The Bipartisan Opening

Technology governance has more genuine bipartisan potential than almost any other chapter in this framework:
- **Children's online safety:** Both Republican and Democratic parents are alarmed; the Kids Online Safety Act had strong bipartisan support before being stalled by industry lobbying; this is the easiest 60-vote target
- **AI in criminal justice:** Civil libertarians on the right and criminal justice reformers on the left both oppose AI-driven mass surveillance and algorithmic sentencing; a coalition around facial recognition restrictions and algorithmic accountability in courts is viable
- **Data privacy:** Privacy is valued across the political spectrum; frame as government overreach by tech companies
- **Small business tech competitiveness:** Antitrust in AI markets benefits small businesses and startups; Republican small-business advocates have natural alignment

**Strategy:** Introduce children's online safety and AI criminal justice reform bills first; use early bipartisan wins to build relationships and demonstrate willingness to work across the aisle on technology; pursue the broader framework through reconciliation where possible, regular order where bipartisan support exists.

### VII.2 The Industry Engagement Strategy

The technology industry is not monolithic. The interests of:
- **Frontier AI labs** (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta): primarily concerned about safety regulation; some (Anthropic) support meaningful safety requirements; others fear regulation will disadvantage them relative to China; negotiate with them on safety testing design
- **AI application companies** (deployers): most affected by worker displacement and algorithmic accountability rules; engage on implementation design
- **Startups and smaller AI companies**: benefit from antitrust enforcement against incumbents; benefit from National AI Research Cloud; potential allies on competition provisions
- **Platform/social media companies**: strongest opponents of Section 230 reform and algorithmic transparency; no path to cooperation; frame as consumer protection vs. corporate profit
