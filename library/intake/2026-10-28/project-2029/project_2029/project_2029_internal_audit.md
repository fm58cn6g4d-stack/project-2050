# PROJECT 2029: INTERNAL AUDIT MEMORANDUM
## Chapters 1–6: Coherence, Fiscal Accuracy, Factual Integrity, and Principle Alignment
### March 22, 2026 | For Internal Review Only

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Six chapters have been written. The framework is coherent in its core architecture, politically realistic in its framing analysis, and substantively ambitious in the right ways. However, this audit identifies **five categories of problems** requiring correction before the framework is shared or developed further:

1. **A significant fiscal discrepancy** between Chapter 2's overall spending scorecard and Chapter 4's more detailed healthcare numbers — the gap is $750B–$1T and it breaks the "conservative estimates balance" claim in Chapter 2
2. **Several specific figures that are uncertain or internally inconsistent** across chapters
3. **A procedural over-promise** in the Chapter 1 campaign framework that Chapter 5's more rigorous coalition math then contradicts
4. **A potentially consequential factual claim** (the SCOTUS IEEPA tariff ruling) that should be flagged as uncertain given the knowledge cutoff
5. **Substantive gaps** relative to the user's stated guiding principles that need to be named explicitly

The framework's core architecture — anti-oligarchy framing, three-phase healthcare approach, comprehensive vs. piecemeal immigration, reconciliation sequencing, filibuster-as-prerequisite — is sound and should not change. The issues below are real but correctable.

## SECTION A: FISCAL DISCREPANCIES — THE MOST IMPORTANT PROBLEMS

### A.1 The Healthcare Cost Gap (Critical)

**The problem:** Chapter 2's consolidated spending scorecard shows healthcare net cost at **$1.7–2.0 trillion** over 10 years. Chapter 4's more detailed budget table arrives at **$2.75 trillion** net. The gap is $750B–$1.05T.

Chapter 4 contains a footnote acknowledging this discrepancy but dismissing it: *"The difference is bridged by additional drug price savings and premium revenue from AmeriCare enrollment that are modeled more precisely here."* This is not adequate. The numbers don't actually reconcile. Here's why:

**What Chapter 4 includes that Chapter 2 doesn't clearly account for:**

| Item | Chapter 2 | Chapter 4 | Gap |
|------|-----------|-----------|-----|
| Medicare PAYGO override | Not listed as a healthcare cost | $500B (Phase 1) | ~$500B |
| Rural hospital fund | $100B | $75B | -$25B |
| Long-term care insurance | $300–500B | $400B | Roughly consistent |
| AmeriCare net cost | $500–800B | $600B | Roughly consistent |
| Drug price savings | Listed as -$400B offset | -$400B | Consistent |
| Mental health parity | $100B | $50B | $50B difference |

The Medicare PAYGO override ($500B) is the single largest source of the discrepancy. It appears in Chapter 4 as a Phase 1 spending item — protecting Medicare from automatic OBBBA-triggered cuts — but it is absent from Chapter 2's healthcare spending scorecard, where it would clearly belong.

**The corrected fiscal picture:**

If we accept Chapter 4's more detailed analysis as more accurate (which it is), the overall spending total must be revised upward:

- Chapter 2 total spending: ~$7.5T (planning estimate)
- Healthcare adjustment: +$750B–$1.05T
- **Revised total spending: ~$8.25–8.55T**

At **conservative revenue of $7.6T**, this means the plan does **not** balance on conservative estimates — it produces a net deficit increase of approximately **$650B–$950B** before the wealth tax revenue ($2T+, Year 3-4, constitutionally uncertain) is counted.

**The honest revised fiscal bottom line:**

| | Conservative (pre-wealth tax) | With wealth tax (Year 3-4) |
|-|-------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Revenue | $5.6T (excl. wealth tax) | $7.6T |
| Spending | $8.4T | $8.4T |
| Net position | **-$2.8T** | **-$0.8T** |
| Deficit reduction goal | $2–3T | $2–3T |
| Honest position | **Deficit-increasing without wealth tax** | **Roughly neutral with it** |

**This matters because:** The Chapter 2 claim that "on conservative estimates, the full agenda including meaningful deficit reduction is achievable" is wrong once the more detailed healthcare figures are used. The correct honest statement is: **the agenda is fully fiscally sound once the wealth tax is enacted (Year 3-4); in Years 1-2, before the wealth tax revenue flows, the plan runs a meaningful deficit that narrows over the term.** This is actually defensible — it's similar to the trajectory of LBJ's Great Society or the ACA — but it must be stated honestly, not obscured.

**What needs to change:** Chapter 2's spending scorecard should be updated to include the Medicare PAYGO override; the fiscal bottom line table should be updated; and the framing in B.6 should be revised to honestly describe the trajectory (deficit-increasing in Years 1-2, improving toward revenue-neutral in Years 3-4 with wealth tax and IRS enforcement at scale).

### A.2 The SNAP Figure Inconsistency (Minor but Real)

**Chapter 1** (Appendix A, OBBBA damage inventory) states: **"$120B+ from SNAP"**

**Chapter 2** (framing section, B.1.1) states: **"cut SNAP by ~$175B"**

These are materially different. $175B is approximately 46% larger than "$120B+." Based on available CBO scoring, the SNAP cuts in the OBBBA are in the range of $150–185B, depending on the 10-year window used. The $120B figure in Chapter 1 appears to be outdated, from early scoring estimates before the final bill was enacted.

**What needs to change:** Chapter 1's $120B+ should be updated to approximately $175B, consistent with Chapter 2 and available CBO data.

---

### A.3 The "Healthcare from the Poor" Figure Inconsistency (Minor)

**Chapter 2 states:** "The OBBBA took **$1.4 trillion from healthcare** for the poor and working class."

**Chapter 1 states:** "~**$1 trillion** from Medicaid."

**Chapter 4 states:** "The OBBBA is projected to cut Medicaid spending by **$1.02 trillion**."

The $1.4T figure in Chapter 2 appears to be a combined healthcare total (Medicaid cuts + ACA subsidy expiration effects + other healthcare provisions), while the $1T/$1.02T figures refer specifically to Medicaid. Both can be correct simultaneously, but they create confusion when used without clear attribution.

**What needs to change:** Chapter 2 should clarify "The OBBBA took $1.4 trillion from healthcare broadly — including $1.02 trillion from Medicaid, the expiration of ACA enhanced subsidies affecting 5 million people, and other healthcare provisions." This resolves the apparent inconsistency without changing any underlying figure.

### A.4 The Wealth Tax Revenue Cliff (Structural Problem)

**The problem:** The wealth tax ($2T+, 10-year estimate) is correctly sequenced as a Year 3-4 initiative in Chapter 5. But the revenue scorecard in Chapter 2 treats it as part of the baseline 10-year total used to justify fiscal balance. Since the wealth tax is also constitutionally uncertain (genuine legal risk of being struck down), relying on it for the plan's fiscal balance creates a structural vulnerability: if the wealth tax is delayed, enjoined, or struck down, the plan's revenue-spending balance is off by $2T+.

Chapter 2 does acknowledge this in B.6: "If the wealth tax is delayed or struck down, the plan must find ~$2T in replacement revenue or reduce spending." But the main fiscal table doesn't show this scenario at all — it includes wealth tax revenue in the conservative baseline as if it were certain.

**What needs to change:** Add a third column to the B.6 fiscal bottom line table showing the scenario without the wealth tax, using alternative revenue sources (higher capital gains rate, expanded FTT, broader IRS enforcement). This is the plan's true conservative floor and should be shown explicitly.

---

### A.5 The "Building Bill" Size Problem (Sequencing)

**Chapter 5** proposes that the FY2030 reconciliation bill (Year 1, fall 2029) contains: AmeriCare public option + Medicare dental/vision/hearing expansion + Long-term care insurance + Climate/IRA restoration + Housing investment. Rough total: $2.2–2.8T in spending, offset by financial transactions tax and capital gains reform revenue.

This is a very large reconciliation bill. While there's no legal size limit, the political challenge of holding a 50-51 vote majority together for a single bill of this scope and cost — in Year 1, before AmeriCare has demonstrated success — is substantially harder than the chapter acknowledges. The OBBBA itself was this large, but it was assembled by a party with more ideological cohesion and political discipline.

**What needs to change:** Chapter 5 should acknowledge this risk explicitly and consider whether housing and long-term care belong in a separate FY2031 bill, reserving the FY2030 bill for the highest-priority items (AmeriCare + climate). This would also give housing its own political moment rather than being buried in a sprawling bill.

## SECTION B: PROCEDURAL INCONSISTENCIES

### B.1 The PRO Act Timeline Conflict

**Chapter 1 (initial framework)**, "Days 100–365: The Building Agenda," lists:
> "American Worker Protection Act (PRO Act + overtime restoration + wage theft enforcement)"

This implies the PRO Act is achievable in Year 1 as regular legislation.

**Chapter 5 (coalition math)** correctly identifies:
> "The PRO Act in full is unlikely to pass in the first two years without a broader filibuster carve-out... Year 3 more realistic."

These are in direct conflict. The initial framework is over-optimistic. Chapter 5's analysis is correct — the PRO Act faces both a 60-vote filibuster hurdle and a Byrd Rule obstacle to reconciliation. Year 1 passage is not realistic.

**What needs to change:** Chapter 1's Days 100–365 section should be amended to note that "introducing and holding a floor vote on the PRO Act is a Year 1 action; passage requires filibuster reform and is more realistic in Year 3." The vote itself is worth holding even if it fails — it puts senators on record.

---

### B.2 The "Protect Your Vote" Campaign Promise Tension

**Chapter 1** includes "Protect your vote" as one of the "Seven for the First Hundred Days" campaign commitments.

**Chapter 5** correctly identifies that the filibuster carve-out needed for voting rights legislation "may not have the votes" within the Democratic caucus, and describes it as "the single greatest uncertainty in the entire governance plan."

The campaign promise implies passage within 100 days; the governing analysis says passage is uncertain. This is the most politically consequential inconsistency in the document — if Democrats run on this and then can't deliver due to an internal filibuster deadlock, it will be a significant credibility failure.

**What needs to change:** The "Protect your vote" campaign commitment should be explicitly defined as a two-part promise: (1) hold a vote on the filibuster carve-out within the first 30 days, putting every senator on record; and (2) if the carve-out passes, enact the John Lewis VRA immediately; if not, pursue every available alternative pathway. The honest formulation is: "We will fight for your vote on Day 1 and every day. We will hold that vote. And if any Democrat blocks it, America will know who they are." That is deliverable. "Pass voting rights in 100 days" is not guaranteed.

**Chapter 2** ends with: *"Next: Chapter 3 — Healthcare: Full Governance Plan | Chapter 4 — Immigration: Full Policy Chapter"*

But the actual writing order was: Chapter 3 = Immigration, Chapter 4 = Healthcare. These labels are swapped.

**What needs to change:** Update Chapter 2's closing reference to correctly reflect: "Next: Chapter 3 — Immigration: Full Policy Chapter | Chapter 4 — Healthcare: Full Governance Plan."

### B.4 Day One EO Volume — Uncoordinated Across Chapters

Chapters 3, 4, and 6 each contain their own standalone "Day One Executive Actions" sections, with no cross-referencing or prioritization across chapters. The combined count across these three chapters alone exceeds 50 executive actions — plus Chapter 1 mentions 50–100 "pre-reviewed" Day 1-30 actions in the transition planning section.

While the total is not unreasonable for a Day 1-30 window, the lack of a master consolidated list creates real operational problems:

- Some EOs in different chapters will require the same agencies (e.g., both healthcare and immigration have EOs directing CMS and DHS)
- No prioritization: which 10 EOs are signed publicly on Day 1 for political visibility? Which 40 are signed quietly in the Oval Office?
- No conflict-check: an EO in Chapter 4 restoring Planned Parenthood to Medicaid and an EO in Chapter 6 restoring IG independence both involve HHS — they should be coordinated

**What needs to change:** A Master Day 1–30 EO Inventory is needed as a separate planning document that consolidates all EO actions across chapters, resolves redundancies, sequences for political visibility, and assigns drafting responsibility by agency.

## SECTION C: FACTUAL CLAIMS REQUIRING VERIFICATION FLAGS

Several specific factual claims in the chapters carry meaningful uncertainty. These are not necessarily wrong — but they are specific enough that they need verification before the document is shared, and they should be flagged as "to verify" in the working draft.

### C.1 The SCOTUS IEEPA Tariff Ruling (High Stakes)

**Where it appears:** Chapter 1: *"SCOTUS struck down IEEPA-based tariffs in February 2026"*

**The issue:** This is a highly specific factual claim about a Supreme Court ruling that occurred between the knowledge cutoff (August 2025) and the document's stated date (March 2026). The legal challenges to IEEPA tariffs were active and moving through lower courts as of mid-2025. A definitive SCOTUS ruling by February 2026 would require: lower court ruling → emergency appeal → cert grant → oral argument → decision, all within approximately 8-9 months. This timeline is possible (SCOTUS has moved quickly on emergency matters) but is not certain.

**Risk:** If this ruling didn't happen as described — if the tariffs were instead struck down by a lower court (leaving SCOTUS to potentially reverse), or if the legal status is more ambiguous — then the Chapter 1 framing around tariff policy needs adjustment. The trade policy implications also vary significantly depending on the actual legal status.

**What needs to change:** Flag this claim in Chapter 1 with a bracketed note: "[VERIFY: SCOTUS IEEPA tariff ruling status as of March 2026 — confirm ruling scope, date, and whether cert was granted or lower court ruling]." Do not remove the reference, but do not build heavy policy architecture on it until verified.

---

### C.2 The OBBBA Signing Date and Exact Title

**Where it appears:** Every chapter: *"The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025"*

**The issue:** The "One Big Beautiful Bill" was a real legislative effort advancing through Congress in spring-summer 2025. The House passed its version in late May 2025; the Senate was negotiating through June-July 2025. Whether the final bill was signed exactly on July 4, 2025 (a symbolically loaded date Trump would have favored) and whether the enacted title is precisely "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" is specific enough to require verification. The knowledge cutoff of August 2025 means this may be accurate but should be confirmed against reporting.

**Risk:** If the bill was signed on a different date, or with a slightly different official title (e.g., "Fiscal Responsibility and Tax Relief Act of 2025" with "One Big Beautiful Bill" as a colloquial name), the repeated references throughout all chapters look imprecise.

**What needs to change:** Add a bracketed verification note the first time this appears in Chapter 1: "[VERIFY: Exact signing date and official enrolled bill title]." If confirmed, remove the bracket. If the title differs, update consistently throughout.

---

### C.3 The "700,000 Deaths" USAID Estimate

**Where it appears:** Chapter 1, Appendix A: *"USAID dismantled (700K+ deaths est.)"*

**The issue:** This figure appears in reporting from early 2025 as an estimate by researchers projecting deaths over several years from the interruption of USAID-funded HIV treatment, malaria prevention, tuberculosis programs, and food aid. It is a projection over an extended timeframe, not a confirmed body count. Presenting it in a damage inventory table without a timeframe or "projected" qualifier makes it look like a confirmed casualty figure, which it is not.

**Risk:** This figure will be attacked aggressively by opponents. Without proper attribution and timeframe qualification, it becomes a credibility liability.

**What needs to change:** Change the Appendix A entry to read: "USAID dismantled (est. 500K–700K+ deaths projected over 3-5 years from interrupted HIV, malaria, TB, and food programs — researchers' projections as of early 2025)." Alternatively, footnote the specific research paper or reporting source.

---

### C.4 The PhRMA Lobbying Figure

**Where it appears:** Chapter 6, Part VII: *"The pharmaceutical industry spent $374 million lobbying Congress last year."*

**The issue:** This is a very specific figure. PhRMA and its member companies are consistently among the top lobbying spenders in Washington — the broader pharmaceutical and health products sector regularly spends $300M-$400M+ annually in total lobbying. The $374M figure is plausible but should be sourced. If it refers to 2025 calendar year spending, it would be based on early reporting since full-year figures typically aren't published until mid-year following.

**What needs to change:** Add a citation: "[Source: OpenSecrets / SOPR lobbying disclosure data — verify figure and year]." This protects the document if the exact number differs slightly from final reported figures.

---

### C.5 Coverage Loss Figures — Minor Internal Inconsistency

**Chapter 1** states: *"10-15 million Americans"* losing coverage from OBBBA.
**Chapter 2** states: *"10+ million from Medicaid cuts; 5 million additional from ACA subsidy expiration"* — implying 15M total.
**Chapter 4** states: *"11.8 million people losing health insurance by 2034...combined...could reach 17 million."*

The range jumps from 10-15M to 17M across chapters. All three figures can be defensible depending on: which provisions are included, which timeframe is used, and whether ACA subsidy expiration (a separate policy action, not directly in OBBBA) is counted. But the inconsistency looks like a research error.

**The most defensible framing:** "The CBO estimates OBBBA provisions alone will cause 11.8 million to lose coverage by 2034; combined with the expiration of ACA enhanced subsidies (a separate but related policy decision), the total reaches approximately 15–17 million." This reconciles all three figures by using CBO's specific number for OBBBA and adding the subsidy expiration explicitly.

**What needs to change:** Standardize the language across all three chapters using the above framing.

## SECTION D: GAPS RELATIVE TO GUIDING PRINCIPLES

### D.1 "Promote Peace" — Not Yet Addressed

The user's first message explicitly lists "promote peace" as a governing goal. The foreign policy chapter has not been written. But more importantly, **none of the existing chapters makes any connection between the domestic agenda and peace** — as if these are entirely separate domains.

They are not. The connection should be made explicit in the framework:
- Defense spending reform (Pentagon has never passed an audit; $800B+/year budget) is directly connected to the fiscal agenda
- The USAID restoration agenda is itself a peace-building initiative (foreign aid prevents conflicts)
- Immigration from Central America and the Caribbean is partly driven by violence that U.S. foreign policy helps cause and could help resolve
- The climate agenda is a national security and global stability agenda — climate displacement drives conflict

**What needs to change:** Add a bridging paragraph to Chapter 1's framing section connecting the domestic agenda to peace; flag the foreign policy chapter as a priority for next development.

---

### D.2 Transportation — Mentioned but Undeveloped

High-speed rail, transit investment, and transportation electrification appear in Chapter 2's spending scorecard ($300B for "high-speed rail + intercity transit") but with no policy architecture. The user's guiding principles explicitly include "build affordable... transportation... systems and infrastructure."

Transportation has specific legislative architecture (Amtrak, FRA, FTA, BUILD grants, IIJA successor) that needs to be developed. It also has strong rural political appeal (rural transit, freight rail) that connects directly to the coalition strategy.

**What needs to change:** Flag as an explicit priority for the Economic Fairness chapter or a dedicated infrastructure chapter.

---

### D.3 Reproductive Rights — Partial Treatment

The user's guiding principles include "protect rights and freedoms." Chapter 4 includes reproductive health in the healthcare governance plan (Title IX), but this is framed primarily as a healthcare access issue. The broader constitutional and legal strategy for protecting abortion access post-Dobbs — including what federal legislation is possible, the state shield law strategy, and the constitutional amendment path — is not addressed.

This is a politically sensitive gap: any Democratic platform that doesn't explicitly address reproductive rights post-Dobbs will face legitimate criticism from core constituencies.

**What needs to change:** Flag for explicit treatment — either an expansion of Chapter 4's Title IX section or a standalone rights chapter that addresses reproductive rights, LGBTQ+ rights, and civil rights enforcement together.

---

### D.4 Trade Policy — A Major Omission

The tariff war with China, Canada, Mexico, and allies has been one of the most economically disruptive features of Trump's second term. Yet no chapter addresses the post-tariff-war trade reconstruction. Chapter 1's initial framework lists this as a gap but the subsequent chapters haven't filled it.

Trade policy connects directly to:
- The manufacturing and industrial policy agenda (what replaces CHIPS and IRA's supply chain provisions?)
- The agricultural constituency (farmers were hurt by retaliatory tariffs)
- The small business audience (tariff costs raised input prices)
- The anti-monopoly agenda (trade policy shapes which corporations can compete)
- The worker power agenda (labor and environmental standards in trade agreements)

**What needs to change:** Trade policy should be a dedicated section of the Economic Fairness chapter or a standalone chapter. It should address: rebuilding WTO engagement, allied trade architecture, labor and environmental standards, and the political framing for trade (neither free-trade globalism nor Trumpian protectionism — "fair trade with rules that work for workers").

---

### D.5 "Reckon with Systems and Norms No Longer Serving Us"

The user's first message includes this phrase, which is the broadest and most open-ended of the guiding principles. It points toward several areas not yet developed:
- **Criminal justice reform** (sentencing, policing, incarceration rates, drug policy) — listed as a gap in Chapter 1 but not developed
- **Electoral system reform** (beyond what's in Chapter 6 — specifically, Senate malapportionment, Electoral College, and the fundamental question of democratic legitimacy)
- **The gig economy and labor market transformation** (how does worker power apply to 1099 workers, platform economy participants, AI-displaced workers?)
- **Mental health system architecture** (beyond insurance parity — the actual system of community mental health, crisis services, and inpatient care)

These are areas where "the system is no longer serving us" is viscerally true but where the policy response requires careful development. Flag for the remaining chapters.

## SECTION E: STRUCTURAL AND ORGANIZATIONAL ISSUES

### E.1 No Master Timeline / Gantt-Style Governance Calendar

Each chapter has its own timeline (Day 1 EOs, Year 1-2 legislation, Phase 1-2-3). But there is no master governance calendar that synthesizes across all chapters and shows the president's full agenda on a single timeline. Without this, the chapters feel like parallel plans rather than a unified governing strategy.

A single governance calendar — showing which EOs happen in which weeks, which legislative vehicles are in which months, which agency rebuilding actions run on which timelines — is the most important missing structural element for the document to be a true governing plan rather than a collection of policy chapters.

**What needs to change:** Create a Master Governance Calendar document as a standalone deliverable, synthesizing across all chapters.

---

### E.2 No Defined "Win Conditions"

The governance plan is rich in proposals but thin on defined success metrics at the overall level. Each chapter has some accountability measures (the healthcare dashboard, immigration KPIs) but there's no document-level answer to: "How will we know if Project 2029 succeeded?"

The framework should define, at the document level, what the country looks like in 2033 if the governing plan has been well-executed:
- Uninsured rate: X%
- Poverty rate: X%
- Real median wage growth: X%
- Carbon emissions trajectory: on track for Y% reduction
- Corruption index: improved by Z
- Voter turnout: X%

These become both accountability measures and campaign messages.

---

### E.3 The "What Democrats Don't Do" Section Is Missing

Every governing plan defines itself partly by what it won't do. This framework is missing an explicit "what we won't do" section that would build credibility with skeptical voters:
- We won't raise taxes on households earning under $400,000
- We won't cut Social Security or Medicare benefits
- We won't open the borders (enforce immigration law fairly)
- We won't tell you what kind of car to drive or what to eat
- We won't punish success — we'll ensure success doesn't rig the system

This section would directly counter Republican attack lines before they land. It belongs in the campaign platform chapter (Chapter 1) or a standalone messaging document.

## SECTION F: WHAT IS SOLID AND SHOULD NOT CHANGE

To be balanced: the following elements of the framework are correct, well-developed, and should not be substantially revised:

1. **The "rigged rules" framing synthesis** (Chapter 2): The anti-oligarchy vs. moderate debate is resolved correctly. "Rigged rules" over "class war"; specific villains over abstract categories; "fair share" over "punish the rich." This is the right call and it's well-argued.

2. **The three-phase healthcare architecture** (Chapter 4): Restore → Public Option → Universal is the right sequential strategy. The honest treatment of Medicare for All (right long-run goal, wrong immediate vehicle) is politically and analytically correct.

3. **The comprehensive immigration framework** (Chapter 3): The synthesis of enforcement + legal pathway reform + earned legalization is right. The E-Verify inclusion as part of a genuine exchange is politically necessary. The Reagan 1986 anchor is an underused political asset. The economic offense (deportation shrinks GDP) is powerful and should lead.

4. **The reconciliation sequencing** (Chapter 5): Restoration Bill first (Day 60); Building Bill second (Fall 2029); Fairness Bill third (FY2031) is the right order. The bill-by-bill vote count analysis is the most rigorous element of any chapter.

5. **The IG massacre as the central corruption story** (Chapter 6): This is the right narrative anchor. The connection between fired watchdogs and taxpayer money lost is devastating and underused. The framing of Schedule F as a reconstituted Gilded Age spoils system is correct and should be maintained.

6. **The filibuster-as-prerequisite argument**: The pre-election commitment strategy — make the filibuster carve-out a condition of DSCC support in 2026 and 2028 — is the most operationally important single recommendation in the framework. It is correct and should be amplified.

7. **The "governing philosophy in one sentence"** from Chapter 5: *"Pass what you can pass, embed it so it's durable, build the coalition to pass the rest — and never sacrifice a real win for a symbolic position."* This should be the lodestar of every governance decision.
