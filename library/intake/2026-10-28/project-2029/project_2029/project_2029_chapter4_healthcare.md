## PART I: THE DAMAGE — A CLEAR-EYED ACCOUNTING

Before building the governing plan, the scope of what must be reversed must be stated plainly — in human terms, not just budget numbers. This is the contrast material that drives both the campaign and the first year of governance.

### I.1 What the OBBBA Did to Healthcare

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, is by any measure the largest rollback of healthcare coverage in American history. Its effects were designed to take years to fully materialize — papering over the immediate political damage while the real harm accumulates slowly in rural emergency rooms, insurance desks, and Medicaid offices.

**Coverage losses:**
- CBO estimates OBBBA provisions alone will cause 11.8 million people to lose health insurance by 2034, with the majority losing Medicaid coverage; combined with the separate expiration of ACA enhanced tax credits (a related but distinct policy action), the total number losing coverage reaches approximately 15–17 million people
- With 71.1 million Americans enrolled in Medicaid as of April 2025, approximately 10 million individuals are expected to lose eligibility for Medicaid services specifically through the bill's work requirements, eligibility restrictions, and administrative barriers; the 11.8M CBO figure reflects the total Medicaid coverage loss including eligibility and enrollment effects

**The mechanisms of harm:**
- The lion's share of Medicaid savings comes from two changes: strict new "community engagement" work requirements for certain adults, projected to save $325 billion over a decade; and caps and gradual reductions in provider taxes that fund state Medicaid programs, cutting a combined $375 billion
- The OBBBA imposes pre-enrollment verification requirements that will effectively end automatic re-enrollment for ACA Marketplace patients, and does not address the scheduled expiration of enhanced tax credits at the end of 2025
- Starting January 2026, OBBBA eliminates enhanced federal funding for states choosing to expand Medicaid for the first time, deterring additional expansion and leaving low-income adults without a coverage pathway
- The OBBBA blocks implementation of national minimum staffing requirements for nursing homes designed to improve quality of care, and carves "orphan drugs" out of Medicare's drug price negotiation process, limiting Medicare's ability to control costs for some of the most expensive medications

**What it triggered in Medicare:**
- Because the OBBBA is projected to increase the deficit, it will trigger automatic spending cuts reducing Medicare funding by approximately $500 billion between 2026 and 2034
- These cuts include a 4% reduction in payments to hospitals and other providers, unless Congress takes action to circumvent them

**The rural hospital catastrophe:**
- The OBBBA is projected to cut Medicaid spending by $1.02 trillion, and the $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program designed to blunt the impact would amount to only $4.5 million per rural hospital per year for five years — then disappear
- More than 300 rural hospitals are at "immediate risk" of closure; as of May 2025 approximately 2,086 rural hospitals received $12.2 billion annually in Medicaid revenue
- Rural hospitals face a cascade of problems: hospital closures, more uninsured residents, and reduced access to care — the temporary rural health fund doesn't address the fundamental problem of uncompensated care when millions lose insurance

**It's already happening:**
- In northeast Georgia, a hospital closed its maternity ward; in rural New Hampshire, a community health center shuttered; in Iowa, a Des Moines hospital system laid off dozens of employees and closed a clinic — all citing the OBBBA
- A rural New Hampshire health center serving nearly 1,600 patients in the rural White Mountains closed, citing a $500,000 annual revenue loss from the bill's work requirements reducing Medicaid participation
- Less funding for Medicaid is projected to decrease approximately 500,000 healthcare jobs over the next 10 years

**The workforce damage:**
- OBBBA removes medical students' ability to receive certain Federal Direct Stafford and PLUS loans, caps borrowing for medical school, and limits new borrowers to only two repayment options — directly constraining the physician pipeline at the moment rural America most needs new doctors

**The double hit on the dually eligible:**
- About 13 million Americans qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid — typically older adults or people with disabilities with very low incomes. They face a double impact: Medicaid cuts reduce help with daily living and cost-sharing, while Medicare becomes more expensive and harder to access

### I.2 What Was Already Wrong Before OBBBA

The OBBBA made a broken system dramatically worse. But the pre-existing conditions matter for understanding the scope of what genuine reform requires:

- 25–30 million Americans were uninsured even before OBBBA, in the world's wealthiest nation
- The U.S. spends approximately 17-18% of GDP on healthcare — roughly twice the OECD average — with outcomes that rank in the bottom third of wealthy nations on most metrics
- Americans pay 3x–10x what citizens of peer countries pay for the same prescription drugs
- Administrative overhead consumes an estimated 25-34% of all healthcare spending — the highest in the world, driven by the complexity of multi-payer systems
- Healthcare costs are the leading driver of personal bankruptcy, medical debt is the largest single cause of debt in collections, and over 100 million Americans carry medical debt
- The healthcare workforce crisis — nursing shortages, rural physician deserts, mental health provider gaps — predates OBBBA but will be catastrophically worsened by it
- Racial and geographic health disparities are extreme: a Black woman in Mississippi has a maternal mortality rate 3-4x that of a white woman in Massachusetts

## PART II: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

### II.1 The Overarching Goal — Stated Plainly

The Project 2029 healthcare governance goal is **universal coverage by 2039** — every American having access to comprehensive, affordable healthcare regardless of employment, income, age, or geography.

This is not a slogan. It is a policy commitment that shapes every decision in this chapter. It means:
- No American loses coverage because they lose a job
- No American skips a diagnosis because they can't afford a copay
- No American goes bankrupt from medical bills
- No rural community loses its only hospital
- No chronic condition goes untreated for lack of insurance

The path to universal coverage in this framework is **sequential, building, and realistic** — not a single leap to single-payer that triggers a political backlash that reverses everything. It is designed so that each step makes the next step easier, more popular, and harder to reverse.

### II.2 The Three-Phase Architecture

**Phase 1 (2029–2030): Restore and Stabilize**
Reverse OBBBA damage; restore coverage to everyone who lost it; stabilize rural hospitals; rebuild gutted agencies; extend and expand ACA with permanent subsidies. Goal: *reduce uninsured to pre-OBBBA levels and below, within 18 months.*

**Phase 2 (2030–2033): Expand and Compete**
Launch a robust Medicare public option available to all Americans; expand Medicare benefits (dental, vision, hearing); cap drug prices; strengthen Medicaid and CHIP permanently; create long-term care program. Goal: *reduce the uninsured to under 5% and give every American a meaningful public option by end of term.*

**Phase 3 (2033 onward): Move to Universal**
Use demonstrated success of the public option — enrollment, cost savings, satisfaction — to build political case for universal coverage; move toward auto-enrollment for the uninsured; pursue single-payer or near-universal public coverage as the natural successor to a public option that has already insured most of the previously uninsured. Goal: *universal or near-universal coverage as the durable, legislative norm.*

### II.3 The Framing Argument: Public Option as the Honest Path

The internal Democratic debate between Medicare for All and the public option is real, but it is often framed as a values debate when it is actually a strategy debate. This framework's position:

**Medicare for All is the right long-run goal. The public option is the right immediate vehicle. This is not a contradiction.**

The evidence:
- 68% of voters support a public health insurance option, including 80% of Democrats and 56% of Republicans; by contrast, Medicare for All polls at 55% support overall but faces strong partisan opposition
- More Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents would prefer voting for a candidate who wants to build on the ACA to expand coverage and reduce costs rather than replace the ACA with Medicare for All; there is robust support among Democrats, and even support among four in ten Republicans, for a government-run health plan — the public option
- Even the Koch-funded Mercatus Center found that Medicare for All would save around $2 trillion over a 10-year period, and most families would spend less on healthcare than they do on current premiums, copays, and deductibles — the system-level economics favor single-payer — but the politics require a demonstrated transition
- The public option, if well-designed, will naturally enroll the most of the uninsured and underinsured; as enrollment grows and the system demonstrates lower costs, the political foundation for expanding public coverage broadens organically

**What to say when asked "Is this a path to Medicare for All?"**

> "I support every American having good, affordable healthcare. The public option means you get to choose: keep what you have, or choose the public plan. If the public plan is good — and it will be — more people will choose it. If it works, we'll expand it. My goal is simple: no American should go without healthcare because they can't afford it. If that eventually looks like Medicare for All, I'm not going to apologize for that."

## PART III: CAMPAIGN MESSAGE ARCHITECTURE

### III.1 The Core Message

**"They took your healthcare to pay for billionaires' tax cuts. We're taking it back — and making sure no one can ever take it from you again."**

This message works because:
- It names the villain (the OBBBA trade: healthcare for tax cuts)
- It promises restoration (taking it back)
- It promises durability (never again)
- It is not abstract — every voter either lost coverage or knows someone who did

### III.2 Specific Messages by Audience

**For rural voters** (the highest-impact persuadable audience):
> "Your hospital is closing because Republicans in Washington cut $1 trillion from Medicaid to pay for tax cuts that mostly go to millionaires. In [state], [X] hospitals have already cut services or closed since the big beautiful bill passed. We're going to restore that funding, stabilize those hospitals, and make sure your community doesn't lose its only emergency room."

**For working-age uninsured** (the largest coverage-loss group):
> "You lost your Medicaid coverage because you couldn't fill out enough paperwork proving you worked enough hours while juggling two jobs. That's not catching waste — that's just bureaucratic cruelty designed to take coverage away from people who earned it. We'll cut that red tape and get you covered again."

**For people on Medicare** (older voters, mobilizable):
> "The Republicans' bill will automatically cut $500 billion from Medicare — not because Medicare is broken, but because their tax cuts blew up the deficit and federal law requires automatic cuts. We're going to stop those cuts, expand Medicare to cover dental and vision, and cap what you pay for prescriptions at $2,000 a year."

**For small business owners** (crossover persuadable):
> "You're paying 18% of your revenue in employee healthcare. A public option means your employees have a real alternative — you can offer it instead of expensive private plans, or let employees who want it choose it themselves. Either way, you save money and they get covered. That's good for you and good for them."

**For parents** (broad cross-demographic):
> "1 in 10 young adults lost Medicaid coverage under this bill. Not because they don't work — because the paperwork to prove it is designed to make them fail. We will make sure every child and every young adult in this country has access to healthcare, period."

### III.3 The Attack-Response Toolkit

**Attack: "Government-run healthcare means worse care"**
> "Every country that does better than us on health outcomes — and most wealthy countries do — has more government involvement in healthcare, not less. Japan, Germany, France, Canada — they all live longer and pay less. We're not trying to be Canada. We're giving you a choice. Keep your current plan if you like it. But you'll have a public option that competes for your business. Competition improves quality. Ask any Republican economist."

**Attack: "You can't afford to expand healthcare"**
> "We can't afford not to. Americans pay twice what Germans or Japanese pay for healthcare with worse results. We spend $4 trillion a year on a system that leaves 30 million people uninsured and drives another 100 million into debt. The public option will save the federal government money, save employers money, and save families money. The question is who benefits from the current waste — and the answer is insurance executives and pharmaceutical CEOs."

**Attack: "Medicare for All would eliminate private insurance"**
> "We're not proposing Medicare for All. We're proposing a public option — the same thing that 68% of Americans including a majority of Republicans support. You keep your private plan if you prefer it. The public option gives everyone else a choice they don't have today. If private insurance is so great, it should be able to compete."

**Attack: "Work requirements are reasonable — why should taxpayers pay for able-bodied people not to work?"**
> "In Arkansas, when they tried Medicaid work requirements, they found the people who lost coverage were already working — they just couldn't fill out the paperwork while working two jobs. No one gained employment. 18,000 people lost coverage. Then a federal court struck it down as illegal. The evidence is clear: work requirements don't increase work. They increase paperwork and decrease coverage. If you want to support working families, you make healthcare available to workers — not add red tape that punishes them for being busy."

## PART IV: THE LEGISLATIVE AGENDA

### IV.1 Phase 1 Legislation — The Healthcare Restoration Act of 2029

**Introduction**: Day 1 of the 119th Congress. Passed via budget reconciliation (healthcare provisions are budgetary). This is the single highest-priority legislative vehicle of the first 100 days.

#### Title I: Medicaid Full Restoration

**Sec. 1.1 — Repeal OBBBA Medicaid Work Requirements**
- Repeal OBBBA Section [X] community engagement requirements in their entirety
- Restore continuous enrollment protections eliminated by OBBBA
- Restore annual (rather than semi-annual) eligibility redetermination
- Direction: CMS must notify all states within 30 days of enactment to cease implementation; states must restore enrollment within 120 days

**Sec. 1.2 — Restore Provider Tax Authority**
- Repeal OBBBA restrictions on state provider taxes that fund Medicaid programs
- Restore state-directed payment authority
- Eliminate the staged cuts to provider tax percentages that would have reduced state Medicaid capacity over time

**Sec. 1.3 — Restore Medicaid Expansion Incentives**
- Restore the enhanced federal match for newly expanding states (eliminated by OBBBA)
- Create a 3-year enhanced match bonus (100% FMAP for 3 years) to incentivize the remaining 10 non-expansion states to expand
- As of 2026, Alabama, Florida, Georgia, Kansas, Mississippi, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Wisconsin, and Wyoming remain non-expansion states; millions of low-income adults in these states fall in the coverage gap
- Estimated new coverage in expansion holdout states if all expanded: 3–4 million people

**Sec. 1.4 — Restore Reproductive Health Provider Funding**
- Repeal OBBBA's one-year moratorium on Medicaid funding for nonprofit providers offering family planning and reproductive services
- Restore Planned Parenthood and FQHC eligibility for Medicaid reimbursement
- Restore Title X family planning funding and eligibility rules

**Sec. 1.5 — Restore Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS)**
- Restore HCBS eligibility standards reversed by OBBBA
- Fund HCBS waitlist elimination: $50 billion over 5 years to eliminate state HCBS waiver waitlists (currently 700,000+ people)
- Restore nursing home staffing requirements blocked by OBBBA

#### Title II: ACA Permanent Stabilization

**Sec. 2.1 — Make Enhanced ACA Subsidies Permanent**
- The ARP-enhanced premium tax credits that expired at end of 2025 enrolled 5+ million additional people; make them permanent
- Expand income threshold for subsidies (currently cuts off at 400% FPL) — move to no income cap, sliding scale to 600% FPL
- Eliminate the "subsidy cliff" that creates coverage gaps at income transitions

**Sec. 2.2 — Restore and Expand Automatic Enrollment**
- Restore automatic re-enrollment for current marketplace enrollees
- Create streamlined enrollment for people losing Medicaid who are now marketplace-eligible (continuous eligibility bridge)
- Direct CMS to implement outreach to newly uninsured populations

**Sec. 2.3 — ACA Marketplace Stabilization**
- Repeal OBBBA pre-enrollment verification requirements that disrupted marketplace access
- Restore 90-day grace periods for premium payment
- Require CMS to maintain and fully fund HealthCare.gov and state exchange systems

**Sec. 2.4 — Restore and Expand Cost-Sharing Reductions (CSRs)**
- Permanently appropriate CSR payments (originally defunded by Trump in 2017, resulting in silver-loading that increased costs)
- Expand CSR eligibility to all silver plan enrollees up to 300% FPL

#### Title III: Rural Hospital Emergency Program

**Sec. 3.1 — Rural Hospital Stabilization Fund**
- $75 billion over 10 years (replacing OBBBA's insufficient $50 billion 5-year program) for rural hospital financial stabilization
- Structured as: direct operating support for critical access hospitals; capital improvement grants; workforce recruitment subsidies
- Eligibility: rural hospitals with >30% Medicaid patient share or operating margins below 2%
- Unlike OBBBA's fund, this funding is permanent and indexed to inflation

**Sec. 3.2 — Medicaid Uncompensated Care Relief**
- $20 billion emergency fund for hospitals carrying uncompensated care burden from the OBBBA coverage-loss gap
- Distributed based on documented uncompensated care costs 2025–2029

**Sec. 3.3 — Rural Health Workforce Program**
- Expand National Health Service Corps by 50,000 placements over 5 years
- Loan forgiveness for physicians, nurses, and mental health providers serving in rural health shortage areas
- Expand Conrad 30 waiver (international medical graduates) and make permanent
- Fund rural residency programs: $3 billion for 2,000 new rural medicine residency slots

#### Title IV: Medicare Automatic Cuts — PAYGO Override

**Sec. 4.1 — Prevent OBBBA-Triggered Medicare Cuts**
- Offset or override the statutory PAYGO sequester triggered by OBBBA's deficit increase
- Direct OMB to exempt Medicare from automatic spending reduction under PAYGO while the Healthcare Restoration Act's revenue provisions are phased in
- Estimated protection: $500 billion in Medicare cuts averted over 2026–2034

**Sec. 4.2 — Medicare Physician Payment Reform**
- Replace the OBBBA's temporary one-year 2.5% physician payment update with a permanent annual inflation-adjusted update mechanism
- End the cycle of annual "doc fixes" that have underpaid physicians for decades
- Index Medicare physician fees to the Medicare Economic Index (MEI) going forward

#### Title V: Drug Price Fairness

**Sec. 5.1 — Expand Medicare Drug Price Negotiation**
- Restore orphan drug carveout elimination: remove OBBBA's exemption of orphan drugs from Medicare negotiation
- Accelerate and expand the negotiation process: move from 10 drugs in Year 1 to 50 drugs by Year 3
- Allow negotiated prices to apply to commercial insurance plans (the "most-favored-nation" commercial extension)

**Sec. 5.2 — Out-of-Pocket Cap Expansion**
- Reduce Medicare Part D out-of-pocket cap from $2,000 (IRA) to $1,500 by 2031
- Extend an out-of-pocket cap of $3,000 to commercial insurance plans (beyond Medicare)

**Sec. 5.3 — Insulin and Essential Drug Price Floors**
- Restore and expand $35/month insulin cap for all insured patients (not just Medicare)
- Extend to additional essential drugs: EpiPens, inhalers, naloxone, common cancer medications
- Create CMS authority to cap pricing on any drug where the U.S. price exceeds 150% of the median price in comparable OECD countries

**Revenue provisions** (incorporated into the reconciliation package with Chapter 2 revenue sources):
- TCJA top-rate reversal and corporate tax reform fund the healthcare restoration
- CBO score: Healthcare Restoration Act costs approximately $900B–$1.1T gross over 10 years; fully offset by revenue provisions in the same reconciliation package

### IV.2 Phase 2 Legislation — The American Health Security Act of 2030

Introduced in Year 2; planned for passage by end of Year 2 or early Year 3. This is the structural expansion legislation.

#### Title I: Medicare Public Option

**The Design — Choices That Matter**

The public option design choices determine how transformative the legislation is. This framework adopts the most robust design consistent with Senate passage:

- **Universal eligibility**: Every American can enroll, regardless of current coverage status
- **Payment rates**: Medicare rates + 10% for most providers; Medicare + 25% for rural and safety-net providers (to protect rural hospital finances)
- **Benefits**: ACA Essential Health Benefits plus dental, vision, and hearing (see Title II)
- **Premiums**: Income-scaled; free for households at or below 200% FPL; capped at 8.5% of income for all others — making it systematically cheaper than comparable private plans
- **Employer option**: Employers may offer the public option to employees; federal government picks up subsidy portion for employees who choose it
- **No mandate**: No one is required to enroll; this is a choice, not a replacement

**Key design debates resolved in this framework:**

*Issue: Provider payment rates.* Setting rates at 100% of Medicare creates the most cost savings but risks provider backlash and access limitations. This framework sets rates at Medicare +10% for most providers and Medicare +25% for rural/safety-net providers, balancing cost savings with access preservation. The Urban Institute estimates this design would reduce national median premiums by approximately 20-25%.

*Issue: Employer access.* Allowing employers to offer the public option potentially accelerates the shift away from employer-sponsored insurance. This framework allows it because: employer relief from healthcare costs increases wages; employee choice is expanded; and the natural market evolution toward public coverage is the long-run goal.

*Issue: Medicaid interaction.* Under this design, the public option is available above Medicaid income thresholds. Medicaid is preserved and strengthened (see Phase 1) as the floor; the public option expands coverage above it.

**Estimated impact** (based on Urban Institute public option modeling):
- 12–18 million newly insured within 3 years
- 20–25% reduction in marketplace premiums
- Employer premium costs reduced by 15–25% for firms offering the public option
- Federal cost: approximately $500–$800 billion gross over 10 years, offset by healthcare savings and revenue

**Sec. 1.1 — Establishment of the Medicare Public Option**
- Create the "AmeriCare" plan (branding TBD; must poll test) within the Medicare program
- Available on HealthCare.gov and state exchanges beginning plan year 2031
- CMS administers; benefits standardized nationally; premiums set annually by an independent board

**Sec. 1.2 — Provider Participation**
- All Medicare-participating providers must accept AmeriCare patients
- Explicit anti-discrimination: providers may not steer AmeriCare patients to inferior care
- Quality measurement and reporting requirements tied to participation

**Sec. 1.3 — AmeriCare Trust Fund**
- Establish dedicated AmeriCare Trust Fund within HHS
- Funded by: premiums; employer contributions (where employers offer AmeriCare); federal subsidy for subsidized enrollees; pharma negotiation savings

#### Title II: Medicare Benefit Expansion

**Sec. 2.1 — Dental, Vision, and Hearing**
- Add comprehensive dental, vision, and hearing benefits to traditional Medicare
- Phase in: dental for preventive care in Year 1 (2031); comprehensive dental by Year 3; vision and hearing in Year 1
- Dental coverage to include: preventive (cleanings, x-rays), basic restorative, and major services; $2,000 annual benefit with no cost-sharing for preventive
- Estimated cost: $250–350 billion over 10 years; offset by reduced emergency care costs and drug price savings
- Include these benefits in AmeriCare public option from launch

**Sec. 2.2 — Mental Health Parity Enforcement**
- Establish CMS Office of Mental Health Parity Enforcement with real investigative authority
- Require insurance plans to demonstrate demonstrable parity compliance (current law is widely violated but weakly enforced)
- Extend mental health benefits in Medicare and AmeriCare: 40 outpatient sessions per year; inpatient parity; substance use disorder treatment with no preauthorization

**Sec. 2.3 — Long-Term Care Insurance Program**
- Establish a federal long-term care insurance program modeled on Germany's social long-term care insurance
- Funded by a 0.6% payroll contribution (employer and employee split)
- Provides: home care, adult day care, skilled nursing facility benefits up to $3,000/month for qualifying individuals
- Addresses a critical gap: long-term care is the primary driver of Medicaid spend-down for middle-class seniors; this program moves long-term care out of Medicaid dependency

#### Title III: Insurance Market Reforms

**Sec. 3.1 — Price Transparency and Anti-Gouging**
- Require all hospitals, clinics, and providers to publish prices in machine-readable, searchable format
- Require prior authorization decisions within 24 hours for urgent care, 72 hours for standard care
- Ban "ghost networks" (listing providers as in-network who don't accept the insurance)
- Establish federal limits on out-of-network billing surprise charges
- Create CMS authority to impose civil penalties for price-gouging in healthcare markets

**Sec. 3.2 — Prior Authorization Reform**
- Establish maximum response timelines for all prior authorization requests
- Require peer-to-peer review by physician of same specialty for denied complex care
- Prohibit prior authorization for preventive services, generic drugs, and established chronic condition management
- Mandate electronic prior authorization system (eliminating the fax machine absurdity)

**Sec. 3.3 — Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM) Reform**
- Require PBMs to pass 100% of manufacturer rebates to plan enrollees at point of sale
- Ban spread pricing (PBM charging insurers more than they pay pharmacies)
- Prohibit PBM ownership of specialty pharmacies that serve their own client plans
- Require PBM licensing and transparency reporting to CMS

**Sec. 3.4 — Anti-Monopoly in Healthcare Markets**
- Direct DOJ and FTC to conduct rolling review of hospital, insurer, and pharmaceutical mergers
- Presumptive bar on mergers that would give a single insurer >40% market share in any county

#### Title IV: Healthcare Administrative Simplification Act
*(Addressing the 25-34% overhead problem — the largest structural cost driver in American healthcare)*

American healthcare spends 25-34% of every dollar on administration — billing, coding, prior authorization, credentialing, compliance, and denial management. This is the highest administrative burden of any wealthy nation's healthcare system, driven by the complexity of navigating thousands of different payer rules, billing codes, and coverage policies. A public option alone does not fix this. The administrative cost persists as long as thousands of different payers operate different systems with different rules.

**Sec. 4.1 — Uniform Billing and Coding Mandate**
- Require all payers (commercial insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, and AmeriCare) to adopt a single, standardized billing format and a unified set of billing codes within 3 years of enactment
- Mandate electronic submission and adjudication for all claims over $50; eliminate paper billing from Medicare and Medicaid
- Standardized credentialing: create a national provider credentialing database — physicians and hospitals credential once and are accepted by all payers, ending the current system where each insurer requires separate credentialing
- Estimated administrative savings: $200-400B annually at full implementation

**Sec. 4.2 — All-Payer Reference Pricing**
- Establish national reference prices for the 500 most common medical procedures: based on Medicare rates plus a defined percentage markup (initially 150%, declining over 5 years toward 125%)
- Commercial insurers may not pay more than reference price to in-network providers without specific justification; out-of-network charges capped at 125% of reference price for emergency care
- Providers may not charge commercial patients more than 150% of their Medicare rate for the same service
- This does not mandate single-payer; it creates price consistency that dramatically reduces the administrative burden of price variation while preserving insurance choice

**Sec. 4.3 — Global Budgeting Pilots**
- Fund 10-state pilots of global budgeting for hospitals and health systems: rather than fee-for-service billing for every individual service, hospitals receive a global annual budget based on their patient population and service mix
- Maryland's global hospital budgeting model has successfully controlled costs and improved quality since 2014 — fund similar pilots in rural, urban, and suburban settings to build evidence base for national expansion
- Global budgeting eliminates the billing-and-coding overhead entirely for participating institutions

**Sec. 4.4 — Mental Health System Infrastructure**
*(Beyond parity — building the actual physical capacity that parity requires)*
- Parity enforcement ensures insurance pays for mental health care. But the shortage is not primarily about insurance — it is about the scarcity of providers and facilities.
- Fund a National Community Mental Health Infrastructure program: $20B over 5 years to build and staff community mental health centers in every county, crisis stabilization centers as alternatives to emergency departments, and peer support networks
- Increase reimbursement rates for mental health services to 100% of comparable medical service rates — the current underpayment is why mental health providers disproportionately opt out of insurance networks
- Fund workforce development: scholarships, loan forgiveness, and salary supplements for psychiatric nurses, social workers, and mental health counselors who serve underserved communities
- Break-up authority for hospital systems that demonstrate market power pricing abuse
- Prohibit non-compete agreements for physicians and nurses

#### Title IV: Women's Health and Reproductive Care

**Sec. 4.1 — Reproductive Healthcare Access**
- Restore and expand Title X family planning funding; require coverage of all FDA-approved contraceptive methods without cost-sharing
- Extend ACA contraceptive coverage mandate to all plans (close grandfathered plan loophole)
- Create federal patient protection for people traveling across state lines for legal medical care
- Federal shield law: protect providers and patients from prosecution under state laws for healthcare services legal under federal law

**Sec. 4.2 — Maternal Mortality and Morbidity**
- Fund the Momnibus Act provisions: CDC maternal health research; state maternal mortality review committees; community-based maternal health workers; rural maternal health
- Require Medicaid coverage of postpartum care for 12 months (extended from 60 days)
- Require all hospitals above 100 beds to maintain obstetric units; emergency OB network mandate for rural areas losing maternity wards

### IV.3 Phase 3 Path — Universal Coverage by 2039

This section describes the legislative and political strategy for achieving universal coverage in the second Democratic term (2033–2037) or beyond. It is deliberately framed as a target, not a bill — because the legislative pathway will depend on political conditions shaped by Phase 1 and 2 success.

**The natural progression argument:**

If the public option (AmeriCare) launches in 2031 and enrolls — as modeling suggests — 12–18 million people within 3 years, the political landscape will have shifted materially. At that point, the remaining uninsured (likely 8–12 million people) will be primarily people who have been unable to enroll due to administrative barriers, language, geography, or genuine poverty. The natural legislative step is auto-enrollment for the uninsured into AmeriCare with the ability to opt out — moving from universal access to near-universal coverage without formally enacting "Medicare for All."

**The honest Medicare for All argument:**

Single-payer Medicare for All would save around $2 trillion over a 10-year period; most families would spend less on healthcare than they currently pay in premiums, copays, and deductibles. The case for it is strong. The political obstacle is the disruption to employer-sponsored insurance covering roughly 160 million people — even if they would pay less overall, the change is visible and the savings are diffuse. The public option strategy allows AmeriCare enrollment to grow, employer coverage to gradually shift, and the political coalition for full single-payer to build on demonstrated success rather than theoretical argument.

**Legislative targets for Phase 3 (illustrative, subject to political conditions):**
- 2033: Auto-enrollment of all uninsured Americans in AmeriCare (opt-out allowed)
- 2035: Medicare eligibility age reduced from 65 to 55; Medicare at 55 available as default
- 2037: Formal move to AmeriCare as universal coverage baseline; private insurance continues as supplemental option
- 2039: Target date for universal coverage as the described end state

## PART VI: AGENCY REBUILDING PLAN

The governing capacity to implement this agenda was systematically damaged by DOGE and the Trump administration. This section identifies the institutional rebuilding required.

### VI.1 Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS)

CMS is the largest purchaser of healthcare in the world and the administrative backbone of the entire agenda. It must be:
- Fully staffed within 6 months; target 200+ immediate permanent hires in key positions vacated by DOGE
- Data systems secured after DOGE's access and the risk of data compromise
- Regional office capacity restored (DOGE gutted many regional offices)
- State Medicaid technical assistance capacity restored; states implementing OBBBA changes in good faith need help unwinding them

### VI.2 FDA

- Restore FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research independence; dismiss any political appointees who override science-based reviews
- Rebuild the drug approval pipeline for generics (currently backlogged); speed to market for generic drugs reduces prices
- Restore the Food Safety and Applied Nutrition programs; address DOGE cuts that reduced food safety inspections
- Rebuild relationships with international drug regulatory authorities for data sharing

### VI.3 CDC and NIH

- Restore CDC budget to at least pre-OBBBA/DOGE levels (~$9B/year)
- Reconstitute all disbanded advisory committees (ACIP, etc.)
- Rebuild CDC's Prevention and Public Health Fund programs eliminated in OBBBA
- Restore NIH grant programs canceled for "DEI" or political content; restore grant evaluation independence
- Fund a National Emergency Preparedness Review to assess gaps revealed by COVID and subsequent DOGE cuts

### VI.4 HRSA (Health Resources and Services Administration)

- HRSA administers the National Health Service Corps, rural health programs, and FQHCs — all of which were targeted by DOGE
- Restore all HRSA cuts; accelerate FQHC grant cycle; restore maternal and child health programs

## PART VII: THE POLITICAL STRATEGY

### VII.1 The Coalition and the Risks

**Natural allies of this agenda:**
- Organized labor (union healthcare costs are enormous; public option provides relief)
- Nurses and frontline healthcare workers (workforce crisis alignment)
- Rural communities (hospital closures make this a survival issue)
- Small business owners (healthcare cost reduction is a direct economic benefit)
- Seniors (Medicare benefit expansion and drug price caps are highly popular)
- Young adults (coverage continuity, mental health, reproductive health)
- Disability community (HCBS restoration and long-term care)

**Opponents and how to neutralize them:**
- Insurance industry: Will fight the public option hard. Counter: the public option doesn't abolish private insurance; it competes. Run the "competition" argument back at the industry's Republican allies.
- Pharmaceutical industry: Will fight drug price reform. Counter: drug prices are a supermajority issue across party lines; any Republican who votes against drug price caps is choosing Pharma over their constituents.
- Hospital systems (large, profitable): Will argue the provider payment rates are too low. Counter: the agenda restores Medicaid funding (their biggest pain point), stabilizes rural hospitals, and reduces uncompensated care. Large hospital systems will oppose; community hospitals and rural hospitals will support.
- Physicians: Split. Primary care and rural physicians (underserved by current system) will broadly support. Specialists in high-reimbursement procedures will oppose. The physician payment reform (permanent inflation-adjusted Medicare rates) is a direct positive to buy physician coalition support.

### VII.2 Making Healthcare Reform Durable

The ACA's durability lesson: it survived 50+ repeal attempts because once people were enrolled and receiving benefits, the political cost of taking it away became too high. Every healthcare reform action should be designed to maximize enrollment speed, because insured voters are the strongest protection against reversal.

**Design for durability:**
1. Get people enrolled fast — every day of the ACA enrollment period, active outreach; every Medicaid-eligible household gets a letter, not just a website
2. Make the benefits visible and immediate — dental benefits, drug price caps, and cost-sharing reductions should be perceptible at the individual level within the first year
3. Build healthcare identity — "AmeriCare" should become a brand people identify with, like Medicare; brand loyalty is political durability
4. State-level partnerships — work with Democratic governors to build state-level healthcare infrastructure that embeds reforms at the state level and makes federal rollback more difficult
5. Automatic triggers — build automatic enrollment expansions into the legislation so that coverage expands as revenue allows, without requiring separate legislation

### VII.3 The Accountability Dashboard

The governance plan includes a public-facing real-time dashboard tracking:
- Number of newly insured Americans per week/month since enactment
- Rural hospitals stabilized vs. at risk
- Drug price reductions from Medicare negotiation (monthly)
- AmeriCare enrollment by state
- Uncompensated care burden on hospitals (quarterly)
- Coverage by race, income, and geography (annual equity report)

This accountability infrastructure serves three purposes: (1) it demonstrates that the government can competently deliver; (2) it creates political facts that are hard to deny; (3) it surfaces implementation problems early before they become political liabilities.
