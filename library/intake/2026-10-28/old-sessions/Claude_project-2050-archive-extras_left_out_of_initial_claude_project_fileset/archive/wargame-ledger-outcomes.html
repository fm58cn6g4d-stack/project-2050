A 2029 Trifecta War Game · Outcomes Instrument

The Ledger

What the twenty-seven-bill agenda would actually change — domain by domain, metric by metric, with the confidence each claim deserves.
Companion to Floor Time (passage), the Bill Book (provisions), and Nineteen Months (narrative). Every row pairs a baseline, a projected direction and magnitude, the mechanism, and an evidence grade: HIGH — strong causal literature or direct policy precedent (e.g., the 2021 CTC actually happened); MED — good research, real design or implementation risk; LOW — directionally defensible, honest people disagree on magnitude. All figures are illustrative war-game estimates keyed to the modeled program, not forecasts.

The ROAD Act changed the housing baseline — settled law, July 2026The bipartisan 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act — passed 85–5 and 358–32, and enacted July 11, 2026 without the President's signature after Trump neither signed nor vetoed it within the constitutional 10-day window — is the deregulatory, supply-enabling half of housing policy with no money attached: zoning and permitting reform, manufactured-housing rules, small-balance mortgage access, an institutional-investor limit, but no HUD funding. It is the near-perfect complement to our capital-injection bill three years later. The housing rows above already assume ROAD is in force and partially implemented by 2029; the compounding is why the units estimate rose from a cold-start ~3–4M to ~4–7M and its confidence from LOW to MED. The risk to this: if ROAD stays under-implemented because HUD is never funded, some of the "reformed ground" our capital is supposed to land on won't have materialized — which is exactly the gap our bill's HUD capacity is built to close.
The inflation caveat, stated plainlyInjecting this much purchasing power into childcare, housing, and health while supply responds more slowly is the 2021 lesson in miniature: expect sectoral price pressure in years one and two, manage it with pacing and the revenue offsets, and judge the program on year-five effective costs, not year-one sticker prices.

The honest bottom lineReading the ledger whole

Three patterns organize everything above. First, the highest-confidence rows are overwhelmingly direct program parameters — checks issued, caps set, records expunged, states admitted — which is exactly why the agenda's political theory front-loads them. Second, the lowest-confidence rows cluster where federal law must work through other actors: local zoning boards, state police agencies, hospital systems, a hostile Court. Those aren't reasons to retreat; they're the map of where implementation capacity and evaluation money must go. Third, the two caveats that recur — sectoral inflation while supply catches up, and litigation before this Court — are the program's genuine strategic risks, and both are managed by design choices already in the bills (pacing and offsets for the first; statutory redundancy and the diversified revenue base for the second).

The ledger's deepest claim is the one no single row can show: these effects compound. The parent who gains a childcare slot joins the labor force the immigration bill is expanding, in a town the housing bill is building, on wages the labor bills are lifting, under a democracy the franchise bills are repairing. Score the rows separately and you undercount the program; that is either its greatest promise or its greatest modeling hazard, and an honest analyst holds both.
