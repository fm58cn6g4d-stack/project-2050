# Revenue channels and the full menu

Federal money moves through separate channels, and each channel has its own claims and its own revenue options. There is no single ceiling a 2029 governing plan must fit under. Social Security, Medicare hospital insurance and the Highway Trust Fund are financed by dedicated taxes and face their own shortfalls; the general fund carries everything else, and its menu of options runs well past $10 trillion over ten years before any broad-based tax.

The real constraints are different: which taxes a party will defend, how much total tax the economy and electorate will bear, and the procedural rules that decide which vehicle each change can ride.

**Units and windows.** Sourced estimates keep the window their source used. The "2030-39" column scales them for a 2029 enactment, by about 1.2 for 2026-35 figures and 1.23 for 2025-34 figures, reflecting nominal growth in the tax base. Scaled figures are my estimates. Many CBO options were scored before the 2025 tax law, which changes some of them, as flagged below.

## The channels at a glance

| Channel | What it pays for | Dedicated revenue | Status | Procedure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Social Security (OASDI) | Retirement, survivor and disability benefits | 12.4% payroll tax up to $184,500 (2026); tax on benefits | Combined funds depleted in 2034; 83% of benefits payable then. 75-year deficit 4.42% of taxable payroll | Barred from reconciliation; needs 60 votes or a filibuster change |
| Medicare Hospital Insurance (Part A) | Hospital, skilled nursing, hospice | 2.9% payroll tax plus 0.9% above $200,000/$250,000; tax on benefits | Depleted in Q2 2033; 89% payable then. 75-year deficit 0.56% of taxable payroll | Reconciliation allowed |
| Medicare SMI (Parts B and D) | Physician, outpatient, drugs | About three-quarters general fund, one-quarter premiums | Funded automatically each year; no depletion, but a growing general-fund draw | Reconciliation allowed |
| Highway Trust Fund | Highways and transit | 18.4¢ gas and 24.4¢ diesel taxes, truck fees | Depleted in 2028; about $340B short over 2026-35 | Reconciliation allowed |
| General fund | Medicaid, ACA credits, family benefits, housing, climate, defense, interest | Income, corporate, estate, excise, tariffs | About $2.1T deficit in FY2026 | Revenue changes allowed in reconciliation |

Two consequences follow.

- **Social Security needs its own deal and its own vote.** It cannot ride a reconciliation bill, so it either waits for 60 votes or becomes the strongest case for changing the filibuster. Its revenue comes from payroll-type taxes credited to the trust fund, not from the general-fund pool.
- **Medicare spans two channels.** Hospital insurance has a dedicated tax that can be raised. New Part B benefits, such as dental, vision and hearing, are paid about 75% from the general fund and 25% from premiums automatically, so part of their cost is self-financing through premiums.

## Social Security

The annual gap at depletion is roughly $400 billion to $450 billion, the 17% cut applied to benefits that were $1.6 trillion in 2025 and will be about $2.6 trillion in 2034, my estimate.

| Option | Sourced estimate | Share of the 4.42% gap | Who pays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apply the 12.4% tax to earnings above $250,000, no added benefits | $1.6T, 2025-34 ([CBO via CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/cbos-options-improve-social-security-solvency)); about $1.9T in 2030-39 | Closed about 70% of the gap measured in 2024; about half of today's larger gap, my estimate | Earners above $250,000 |
| Same, with a small added benefit for the new taxes ([SSA actuaries, E2.16](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/solvency/provisions/charts/chart_run424.pdf)) | Improves balance by 2.35% of payroll | About 53% | Earners above $250,000 |
| Raise the 12.4% rate | Trustees: closing the gap after a 2034 depletion takes a 17.3% rate | About 4.9 points combined if delayed to 2034; less if phased in sooner | All covered workers and employers |
| Apply a Social Security tax to investment income above $400,000 and credit it to the trust fund | Unscored here | Depends on rate | High-income households |
| Cover newly hired state and local workers | Modest gain | Small | New public employees |

An illustrative package: the $250,000 threshold with benefit credit (2.35%) plus a payroll rate increase of about 2 points phased in over 20 years would roughly close the 75-year gap, by my arithmetic. Most Democratic proposals also raise benefits, which widens the gap the revenue must cover.

## Medicare hospital insurance

The gap is smaller: about $85 billion in 2033, when outlays of $775 billion meet income of $690 billion. The Trustees put the fix at a payroll rate of 3.46% instead of 2.90%.

| Option | Sourced estimate | 2030-39 | Who pays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extend the 3.8% net investment income tax to active pass-through income and credit it to HI | $440B, 2026-35 ([CRFB summary of CBO](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/cbos-revenue-savings-options)) | About $0.5T | Pass-through owners above $200,000/$250,000 |
| Raise the Medicare and investment income tax rates to 5% above $400,000 | $412B, 2025-34 ([Treasury via CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/breaking-down-proposals-presidents-fy-2025-budget)) | About $0.5T | Households above $400,000 |
| Redirect existing investment income tax receipts to HI | Shifts general-fund revenue | Revenue-neutral overall | None directly |
| Medicare Advantage payment reform | CRFB attributes $550B of 2027-36 overpayment to the HI trust fund | $0.2T to $0.35T captured, my estimate | Medicare Advantage insurers |
| Raise the HI payroll rate by 0.56 points | Closes the 75-year gap per the Trustees | About $0.9T, my estimate | All workers and employers |

The first two rows together raise roughly $100 billion a year by the early 2030s, about enough to close the HI gap without touching the general fund or most workers' payroll taxes.

## Transportation

The Highway Trust Fund runs dry in 2028. Congress has transferred about $275 billion from the general fund since 2008 to keep it going.

| Option | Sourced estimate | 2030-39 | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Raise fuel taxes 15¢ and index them | $215B, 2026-35 (CBO) | About $0.26T | First increase since 1993; falls as vehicles electrify |
| Federal electric-vehicle registration fee | $12B to $17B over ten years ([CBO via Bond Buyer](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/despite-user-fees-surface-transportation-bill-deepens-highway-trust-fund-deficit-cbo)) | Small | Mostly symbolic as a revenue source |
| Per-mile road user charge | Unscored | Could replace fuel taxes over time | Requires new collection system |
| Dedicate part of a carbon fee | See general fund | Any share | Ties transport to climate revenue |
| Continue general-fund transfers | Any amount | Competes with other claims | Status quo approach |

A larger passenger rail or transit program would add to this channel's claims, or to the general fund's, unless paired with its own source.

## Cautions on the arithmetic

- **Options overlap.** A higher top rate, taxing gains at death and a minimum tax on large fortunes all reach the same households; together they raise less than the sum of their scores. The employer exclusion cap and the hospital price cap both lower the cost of employer coverage, so their revenue partly overlaps too.
- **Shrinking bases.** Carbon, fuel and tobacco revenue all fall as the policy works. They suit transitional or capital spending better than permanent benefits.
- **Pre-2025 scores.** Several CBO options predate the 2025 tax law. Itemized deduction limits in particular would raise much less now.
- **Conventional versus dynamic.** These are conventional scores. Critics of high-end and corporate increases argue that behavioral and growth effects would cut collections; supporters point to IRS enforcement returns of up to $12 per dollar on high-income audits ([Yale Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/revenue-and-distributional-effects-irs-funding)).
- **Total tax burden.** Each channel can be filled separately, but voters and markets judge the combined increase. Revenue was about 17% of GDP in recent years; the full menu above would push it well past 20%.

## What research says about total tax burden

Economists do not identify a fixed ceiling on how much a country can tax. The evidence says more about how revenue is raised than about how much, and the disagreements center on high-end rates and corporate taxes.

| Question | What the evidence shows | Where it is contested |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How high do other rich countries go? | The OECD average tax-to-GDP ratio reached a record 34.1% in 2024. Denmark was highest at 45.2%, France 43.5%, Austria 43.4% ([OECD Revenue Statistics 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025_07ca0a8e.html)). The US, counting all levels of government, is roughly a quarter of GDP. | Comparisons depend on what counts as a tax; US employer health premiums, for example, are private spending elsewhere financed by taxes. |
| Does a bigger tax share slow growth? | Lindert finds that higher tax-financed social spending does not correlate with lower GDP levels or growth across OECD countries. He attributes this partly to pro-growth tax mixes in high-tax states ([NBER w9869](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9869.pdf)). | Critics, notably Bergh on Sweden, argue the "free lunch" framing overstates the case and depends on how spending is classified ([Econ Journal Watch](https://econjwatch.org/file_download/288/ejw_com_may06_bergh.pdf)). |
| Which taxes do least harm? | An OECD working paper ranks corporate taxes as most harmful to growth, then personal income taxes, then consumption taxes, with recurrent property taxes least harmful ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/taxation-and-economic-growth_241216205486.html)). | Later work by other researchers confirms the property-tax result but finds weaker evidence for the rest of the ranking. |
| How high can top rates go? | Diamond and Saez put the revenue-maximizing top rate at about 73%, combining federal, state, payroll and sales taxes ([summary](https://conversableeconomist.com/2011/12/09/should-the-top-income-tax-rate-be-48-or-73/)). | Mankiw, Weinzierl and Yagan argue for about 48%. Critics say the 73% figure rests on a low assumed responsiveness of top incomes; higher estimates imply peaks near 40% or below ([Cato Journal](https://www.cato.org/cato-journal/fall-2019/optimal-top-tax-rates-review-critique)). |
| Who bears corporate taxes? | JCT and CBO assign 25% of the long-run burden to workers and 75% to capital owners; Treasury uses 18% to workers ([Tax Foundation summary of JCT](https://taxfoundation.org/jct-corporate-tax-falls-partly-labor/)). | Some studies put labor's share at 50% to 70% or more. |

Three implications for a 2029 plan.

- **There is room, but the mix matters.** Taking US revenue toward the OECD average is within the range other democracies sustain. Most of them do it with broad payroll and consumption taxes rather than high top rates, which is why a top-end-only strategy eventually runs into the contested part of this literature.
- **The binding limits are practical.** Avoidance capacity at the top, IRS enforcement, capital mobility, voter tolerance and debt dynamics constrain what can be collected sooner than any growth threshold the research has found.
- **Each channel still has its own limit.** A payroll increase for Social Security and a corporate increase for the general fund draw on different bases, but voters and markets judge the combined burden.
