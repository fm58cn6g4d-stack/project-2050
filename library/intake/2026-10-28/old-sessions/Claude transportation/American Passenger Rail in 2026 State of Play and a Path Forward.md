# American Passenger Rail in 2026: State of Play and a Path Forward

Sep 21, 2026 · @espen

Congress is nine days from the September 30 expiration of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act's surface transportation title, and it has already punted: a stopgap signed September 2 extends authority only to December 11 and drops $36.8 billion in advance appropriations, cutting passenger-rail investment by roughly 83 percent in the interim. The House has moved its five-year successor, the BUILD America 250 Act, out of committee 62-2; the Senate has not released text. Underneath that fight, Amtrak is mid-restructuring into a holding company, California's flagship HSR project is within 16 months of running out of money, and the most credible progress anywhere in the country is happening in Nevada, Virginia, and North Carolina. This document maps that landscape, then argues the realistic path runs in two stages: a scaled-up BUILD America 250 passed in the December lame-duck session, followed by a structural framework, the American Rail Competitiveness Act (ARCA), enacted as the passenger rail title of the next reauthorization in 2031 and running to roughly 2051, built to make passenger rail genuinely competitive with driving and short-haul flight.

## The 2026 reauthorization fight

The IIJA's surface transportation title (five years, $1.2 trillion, including Amtrak's $75 billion ConnectUS ask) expires September 30, 2026. Rail programs ride in the same reauthorization; they have been folded into the highway bill since the FAST Act in 2015 rather than authorized separately.

Congress missed the deadline. On September 1-2 it passed the Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027 (H.R. 6500), extending most programs to December 11 on pro-rated FY2026 contract authority. That extension does not restore the $36.8 billion in IIJA advance appropriations, which had been designated emergency spending outside normal budget rules. The gap falls hardest on programs that were never formula-driven: transit investment drops roughly 20 percent and passenger rail investment roughly 83 percent through December 11, per transportation-industry estimates. It is the 29th short-term surface transportation extension since the 1990s; this process essentially never finishes on time.

### BUILD America 250 Act (H.R. 8870): the House's opening bid

House T&I Chairman Sam Graves (R-MO) and Ranking Member Rick Larsen (D-WA) introduced BUILD America 250 on May 17 and moved it through committee May 22 on a 62-2 vote after 15 hours of markup and over 100 amendments. It authorizes $580 billion over FY2027-31: $474.4 billion in guaranteed Highway Trust Fund contract authority, with the remaining roughly $106 billion subject to annual appropriation, since unlike the IIJA it carries no general-fund advance appropriations of its own.

### IIJA vs. BUILD America 250: the guaranteed-versus-authorized split matters more than the headline totals

The raw numbers point in different directions depending which IIJA baseline you use. On direct Amtrak grants, BUILD America 250 authorizes $31.1 billion over five years, more than the $22 billion IIJA actually guaranteed through advance appropriations. But IIJA also carried a separate, non-binding authorized target of $19.2 billion in additional Amtrak funding subject to normal annual appropriations, bringing its full authorized envelope to roughly $41.2 billion against BUILD America 250's $31.1 billion, a real decrease of about a quarter. The more consequential change, per CRS, is structural rather than nominal: IIJA's $22 billion could not later be clawed back by a stopgap, the exact mechanism that just cut passenger rail 83 percent through December 11 (see above). BUILD America 250 provides zero advance appropriations for Amtrak, so its entire $31.1 billion depends on winning the annual appropriations fight five years running.

On the Federal-State Partnership program and its two smaller siblings, Restoration and Enhancement and Interstate Rail Compacts, which BUILD America 250 merges into the new $18.5 billion National Intercity Passenger Railroad Partnership Program, the comparison is a clear decrease either way: those three programs guaranteed roughly $36-37 billion combined under IIJA, so the merged successor is a cut of close to half. Rail advocacy analysis puts the whole rail title's authorized total about 38 percent below IIJA's combined $102 billion in rail funding, and treats the removal of all guaranteed funding, not just the dollar reduction, as the more significant step back.

Rail Passengers Association, which had engaged cautiously on the bill, withheld its endorsement over the funding levels and the Corridor ID sunset. It warns the cuts jeopardize 69 corridors already in the FRA pipeline, including Albany-Boston, Baton Rouge-New Orleans, and Phoenix-Tucson, and that FTA transit authorization falls about 23 percent and $24 billion short of matching IIJA levels in real terms. The bill has not yet reached the House floor.

### The Senate: no bill yet, real jurisdictional friction

The Senate has not released reauthorization text. Jurisdiction is split in a way the House's is not: EPW (Capito, chair; Whitehouse, ranking) handles highways, Commerce (Cruz, chair; Cantwell, ranking) handles rail and safety, and Banking and Finance hold pieces too. Capito has held hearings since 2025 around three stated principles: cut duplicative programs, speed project delivery, and preserve state flexibility against what she calls top-down mandates. No committee has marked up a highway or rail title as of this writing. Commerce's most concrete recent action has been safety-adjacent: reintroducing the Cantwell-Husted Bipartisan Railway Safety Act (a response to the East Palestine derailment) and confirming FRA and Amtrak board nominees, not a rail-title bill.

Expect the eventual Senate product to diverge from BUILD America 250 in three predictable ways. Capito's preference for consolidating grant programs and preserving state flexibility points toward folding rail funding further into block-grant-like mechanisms rather than keeping discrete competitive programs. DOT Secretary Duffy's July letter to Congress pushed proposals, like eliminating the Mass Transit Account from the Highway Trust Fund, that go further right than the House bill and that Democrats on both committees are likely to resist. Whitehouse and Cantwell will push to restore IIJA-level rail and transit funding and to add the Railway Safety Act, which stalled on the floor last Congress over freight-industry opposition. The timing is likely to resolve inside the lame-duck session itself, not after it, and the reason is as personal as it is procedural. Graves is retiring, and would have lost the T&I gavel to House Republican term limits even had he run again and Republicans held the majority, so this reauthorization is genuinely his last chance to close a deal bearing his name. That gives him a strong incentive to move a bill before the new Congress is seated in January, while he still holds the gavel and Larsen, his counterpart of two decades, is still the ranking member across the table. If House Democrats flip the chamber in November, the incentives on both sides point the same way: Republicans have reason to lock in a deal now, before losing the majority that lets them write the baseline text, and Democrats have reason to take a real, if incomplete, win in the lame duck rather than gamble that a fresh Congress in 2027 reopens the whole negotiation on more favorable terms, since a partisan rewrite could just as easily produce another year of extensions as a better bill.

The realistic outcome is a "BUILD America 250 Plus": Graves and Larsen's basic structure intact, funding levels nudged up from BUILD America 250's toward IIJA's using whatever Senate leverage a stronger post-midterm Democratic minority, or majority, brings to the table, and a handful of policy tweaks, but not the structural overhaul, a dedicated trust fund, permanent regional commissions, expanded RRIF authority, that ARCA proposes below. That overhaul is a bigger lift than one lame-duck session, even a well-timed one, can absorb; it is a better fit for the next reauthorization, in 2031, an idea this document returns to in the phasing section.

## Amtrak's own plan, and the fight over what Amtrak even is

### ConnectUS, five years on

Amtrak released its Corridor Vision, branded ConnectUS, in 2021: $75 billion over 15 years to add or restore service on 39 new or improved corridors across 46 states, plus the separate $117 billion Connect NEC 2035 plan for the Northeast Corridor spine. Projected impact: $8 billion a year in net economic benefit by 2035, 20 million additional annual riders, and up to 55 percent lower emissions than driving on routes outside the NEC. The IIJA funded a down payment, not the whole vision: about $66 billion for intercity rail overall, $22 billion of it to Amtrak directly, versus the $75 billion Amtrak had asked for just for national-network expansion. Every corridor now moving forward, including North Carolina's S-Line and Georgia's Atlanta-hub concepts, traces back to this 2021 document and the Corridor ID Program it helped create.

Five years later, ConnectUS is best understood as a wish list that shaped the FRA's Corridor ID pipeline rather than a funded program. Most of its 39 corridors remain in early planning phases; only a handful (North Carolina's Raleigh-Richmond S-Line, Virginia's Long Bridge, a handful of Midwest and Southeast corridors) have real construction dollars behind them.

### How far along are the original 2021 asks?

Neither ConnectUS nor Connect NEC 2035 maps cleanly onto how Congress actually appropriates rail money; FRA has never published a single dashboard tracking obligations against those specific 2021 dollar targets. The table below is a reconstruction from public IIJA appropriations and Notice of Funding Opportunity data, not an official Amtrak or FRA figure, and should be read as approximate.

| Original ask | IIJA (FY22-26) toward it | BUILD America 250 (FY27-31) toward it | Rough cumulative by FY31 | Rough share funded | Loan-backed capital (outside these totals) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Connect NEC 2035: $117B | $6B direct Amtrak NEC grants, plus up to $24B in Federal-State Partnership-NEC awards (its full authorized ceiling: $16.4B awarded in the FY22-23 round, about $2.0B more in FY24, up to $4.75B pending in the FY25-26 round) | $10.4B in direct NEC grants, plus an unspecified NEC share of the new $18.5B National Intercity Passenger Railroad Partnership Program that replaces FSP | Roughly $40-50B | Roughly a third to 40 percent | $4.06B RRIF loan closed July 2024, funding the local share of Gateway's $16B Hudson Tunnel Project on top of its federal grants |
| ConnectUS National Network: $75B | $16B direct Amtrak National Network grants, plus at least $12B in Federal-State Partnership-National awards (its authorized floor; the first NOFO round alone made $4.6B available) | $20.7B in direct National Network grants, plus a share of the same $18.5B partnership program | Roughly $55-60B | Roughly 70-80 percent | None directly against this ask; Brightline West's $6.0B RRIF loan (closed September 2025) sits outside ConnectUS entirely since it's privately developed, not an Amtrak-led corridor |

The National Network ask looks closer to fully funded, in percentage terms, than the far larger NEC ask, which is a real and slightly counterintuitive finding given how much attention Gateway gets. Two things explain it. Gateway alone is a roughly $16 billion outlier that consumes a large share of whatever NEC-designated money exists, and a meaningful piece of its financing is a RRIF loan rather than a grant, so it barely shows up against the 2021 target at all. And the Connect NEC 2035 ask, $117 billion for about 150 projects over 15 years, was always a far larger and more capital-intensive request than ConnectUS's corridor-by-corridor national asks. Either way, both original visions were 15-year plans; by the end of BUILD America 250's FY27-31 window, the country will be 10 years into that clock with the NEC vision well under half funded and the National Network vision closer to, but still short of, three-quarters funded.

Two more caveats worth a gut check. First, loans: the grant totals above are not the whole federal capital picture. RRIF loans do not count against either 2021 target since they are debt, not grants, so Gateway's $4.06 billion loan is real federal capital that the table understates, and RRIF's total lending authority is $35 billion, most of it still unused. Second, obligated versus authorized: the $22 billion in Amtrak's direct IIJA funding was fully guaranteed by statute rather than subject to annual appropriations, but guaranteed is not the same as spent. Amtrak's own FY2026 grant request states that roughly 80 percent of its combined IIJA and annual-appropriations funding had been obligated under an executed grant agreement as of that report, not the full 100 percent. The FSP-NEC figures above are mostly real awards too, though the FY22-23 round's $16.4 billion included some multi-year "anticipated obligations" for FY24-25 rather than money fully disbursed at announcement.

### The restructuring fight

In February 2026 the FRA revealed it was directing Amtrak to reorganize as a holding company sitting atop three subsidiaries: infrastructure (track, stations, dispatching, capital projects), fleet (locomotives, cars, leasing, potentially pooled with state equipment), and operations. Amtrak's board approved a preliminary framework in late July and plans to bring a full structure back to the board in December 2026, with implementation still undated; Rail Passengers Association expects a multi-year process.

DOT has repeatedly denied this is a step toward privatization, but the politics around it are genuinely contested. Rail unions, led by the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers and Trainmen, have warned members it could erode labor protections and open the door to contracting out state-supported routes to operators like Herzog. Rail Passengers has taken a measured position: structural reform can help if executed well, but international experience (it points to the UK's 2024-25 renationalization into Great British Railways, reversing three decades of privatized operation) cuts the other way, and the organization's central worry is that restructuring proceeds independent of, and possibly as cover for, the Trump administration's request to cut Amtrak's FY2027 appropriation. The timing is pointed: this reorganization is unfolding in the same year Congress is supposed to decide how much money Amtrak gets for the next five years, and no restructuring proposal so far specifies how funding would flow to the new subsidiaries.

Notably, this isn't a clean partisan split on the board itself. Amtrak's board is bipartisan by statute, no more than five of eight presidentially appointed seats from one party, and several current members, including Chair Anthony Coscia, a 2010 Obama appointee reappointed in 2015, were nominated well before this administration; the board approved the preliminary framework on July 30 without public dissent reported. Some observers note Amtrak management floated a similar breakup once before, under former CEO Richard Anderson in 2018-19, suggesting the appetite for restructuring predates this administration's push even if the FRA is now setting the timeline. There are real potential upsides worth naming alongside the risks: separating NEC infrastructure into its own entity could let it run on an open-access basis with multiple operators competing for slots, an idea many rail economists favor; it could depoliticize the recurring fight over NEC-versus-National-Network capital dollars by giving each its own accounting; and a dedicated fleet subsidiary could pool equipment more efficiently across states, the way European infrastructure managers already do. Rail Passengers Association's own members have raised these upsides even while pressing for safeguards, particularly around keeping the fleet entity from becoming a profit center that squeezes service quality.

For a build-out plan, the restructuring matters for one practical reason: whatever legislative vehicle expands passenger rail over the next decade will be writing into a corporate structure that may look nothing like today's Amtrak by the time it takes effect. Any financing mechanism this proposal creates needs to be structured to survive that reorganization, not assume today's org chart persists.

## The vanguard: where actual dirt is moving

The most useful signal for a build-out plan is not what Congress authorizes but what is physically under construction. Four projects and one emerging corridor tell most of the story.

### Brightline West: the one true high-speed line under construction

Brightline West's 218-mile, fully electric, up-to-200-mph line between Las Vegas and Rancho Cucamonga is the closest thing the U.S. has to a true high-speed rail project actively being built. It runs mostly in the I-15 median, is backed by a $3 billion federal grant, a $6.0 billion RRIF loan closed in September 2025 (the largest single RRIF commitment on record besides Gateway's), and roughly $3.5 billion in private activity bonds, and broke ground in April 2024. As of 2026 it is in civil construction at both the Las Vegas station and along the corridor, with train manufacturing underway. The original target of the 2028 LA Olympics has slipped to late 2029. Its business case rests on the specific 400-550 kilometer distance band where AECOM's research finds high-speed rail beats both driving and flying on total trip time; that distance logic, more than any federal program, is the model worth generalizing.

### California HSR: a cautionary tale on financing discipline

California's high-speed rail project is the clearest warning in this landscape. The FRA terminated roughly $4 billion in federal grants in mid-2025 after a compliance review found the authority had missed obligations in nine areas, including a $7 billion shortfall on the Initial Operating Segment and a blown 2024 deadline to select a trainset vendor. The state dropped its lawsuit over the funding in December 2025. The Authority's own 2026 draft business plan, reviewed by the Legislative Analyst's Office and the project's Inspector General, shows a roughly $9.5 billion funding gap over five years, current resources exhausted as soon as December 2027 absent new financing, and a schedule that has slipped from a 2032-33 opening to as late as September 2034 for the Merced-Bakersfield segment alone. Total system cost is now estimated near $126 billion against original 2008 voter authorization of about $33 billion, with a full-system funding gap near $90 billion and no identified path north of Merced or south of Bakersfield. None of that is purely a story of California's own making. Trump administration officials, including Rep. Kevin Kiley, pushed publicly and explicitly to pull the project's funding well before the compliance review concluded, and DOT conducted that review under an administration openly hostile to the project's survival. Both things are true at once, and a federal build-out plan has to design against both: a project that gave critics real ammunition through missed deadlines and non-transparent reporting, and a federal partner willing to use that ammunition as pretext to withdraw support entirely.

### Virginia: the highest-leverage single project in the country

Virginia's $5 billion Transforming Rail in Virginia program, run by the Virginia Passenger Rail Authority since 2020, is quietly the most consequential passenger-rail investment underway. Its flagship, the Long Bridge Project, is adding a second two-track rail bridge over the Potomac (plus five bridges total and two pedestrian/bike bridges) to relieve a 120-year-old, 98-percent-capacity bottleneck that CSX itself identified as the single biggest constraint on East Coast passenger rail. Estimated at $2.7 billion, under construction since 2024 with completion targeted for 2030, Long Bridge is a prerequisite for nearly every other Southeast and Mid-Atlantic corridor expansion: VPRA's executive director has said plainly that once it is done, "you'll see a lot more movement...up and down the I-95 corridor, but at least out to Broad Run if not further." Virginia's 2026 legislative session also eased land-acquisition rules for VPRA, a small but telling sign that a state government is actively building institutional capacity for sustained rail expansion, not just funding individual projects.

### North Carolina: the S-Line and interstate execution

North Carolina's Raleigh-to-Richmond (R2R) project, restoring the long-dormant S-Line that CSX once operated between Richmond and Tampa, received the largest grant in NCDOT history: nearly $1.1 billion under the IIJA in December 2023. It is a genuine interstate execution model: NCDOT leads, working with VPRA, Amtrak, CSX, and the FRA, and construction (two grade separations at Durant Road and New Hope Church Road in Raleigh) is visibly underway in 2026, with an additional $65 million awarded in June 2026 toward purchasing the S-Line corridor itself from CSX. Completion will cut over an hour off Raleigh-Richmond travel time and, combined with Long Bridge, meaningfully strengthens the entire Southeast Corridor concept Amtrak's ConnectUS plan envisioned running from D.C. through Charlotte to Atlanta.

### Cascadia: the Pacific Northwest's slow-build option

Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia's long-studied ultra-high-speed corridor linking Vancouver, Seattle, and Portland (projected speeds to 250 mph, sub-hour city-to-city trips) formally entered the FRA's Corridor ID Program and is now developing a Service Development Plan through 2028, funded partly by Washington's Climate Commitment Act revenue and a federal Corridor ID grant. It remains firmly in the planning phase, with WSDOT's own 2019 analysis assuming a 2040 operational date, and it faces a genuine strategic fork that advocacy group Solutionary Rail has pressed publicly: build a wholly new high-speed alignment, or spend those dollars upgrading the existing, already-operating Amtrak Cascades service to 110 mph first. Rep. Suzan DelBene, whose district anchors the Washington end of the corridor, has pushed for pursuing new-build high-speed rail and continued Amtrak Cascades upgrades in parallel rather than sequentially, the approach this proposal adopts below.

## Legislation beyond the highway bill

### The American High-Speed Rail Act (H.R. 9036)

Reps. Seth Moulton (D-MA) and Suzan DelBene (D-WA) reintroduced this bill in 2026, as they have in every Congress since 2020. It would authorize up to $41 billion a year for five years ($205 billion total) in FRA grants dedicated specifically to high-speed (186-plus mph) and higher-speed (110-186 mph) rail, require at least $38 billion in matched non-federal funding, direct tax incentives for freight railroads that make right-of-way available for HSR projects, and direct DOT to write safety rules based on internationally proven technology rather than starting from scratch. It has 47 co-sponsors across 20 states, all Democrats; Rep. Suzanne Bonamici was among the original 2024 co-sponsors, and the bill's Pacific Northwest orientation (DelBene has cited Cascadia directly) tracks the corridor described above.

Its legislative fate is not encouraging: the 2024 version died in the House Subcommittee on Railroads, Pipelines, and Hazardous Materials without a hearing, and nothing in the current Republican-controlled House's posture suggests 2026 will differ. Its real function is as a marker bill, not a live vehicle: it stakes out what a dedicated, well-funded HSR program could look like and gives reauthorization negotiators language to borrow from if Democrats gain leverage after the midterms. Any framework built on the assumption that H.R. 9036 passes as written would be building on sand; a framework that treats it as a source of provisions to graft onto BUILD America 250 or its Senate counterpart is realistic.

### Other vehicles worth tracking

The Railway Safety Act of 2026 (Cantwell-Husted, S. and companion H.R. 928) is a freight-safety bill, not a passenger-capacity bill, but it has already been folded into BUILD America 250 by amendment in the House and remains a live Senate priority; its wayside-detector and inspection mandates matter for passenger rail because Amtrak and state-supported trains run on freight-owned track for most route-miles outside the Northeast Corridor. The Nationally Significant Rail Station Modernization Act, added to BUILD America 250 by amendment, would let Amtrak acquire, lease, and develop station-adjacent property and enter joint public-private partnerships there, plus block state and local property taxation of that Amtrak-owned development; it is a real, if narrow, tool for value-capture financing at major stations (Union Station in D.C. and NEC hub stations are the obvious first users). RRIF, the Railroad Rehabilitation and Improvement Financing program, continues to matter as the loan vehicle actually financing pieces of Gateway; it survived the 2026 funding freeze specifically because RRIF debt service is not contingent on federal appropriations, which is a structural lesson this proposal builds on directly.

## The proposal: the American Rail Competitiveness Act

The evidence above points to a specific diagnosis. The U.S. does not lack corridors worth building (ConnectUS identified 39 of them) or state-level will (Virginia, North Carolina, and Washington are executing). It lacks three things: a financing structure that survives annual appropriations politics, an accountability structure that catches a CAHSR-style failure before four billion dollars and a decade are sunk, and a permanent institutional home for interstate corridor planning once a grant cycle ends. The American Rail Competitiveness Act (ARCA) is built to fix those three things, not to re-litigate whether rail is worth funding, which the bipartisan 62-2 BUILD America 250 vote suggests is no longer the live question.

Everything below describes ARCA as the passenger rail title of the 2031 surface transportation reauthorization, not the interim bill Congress is likely to pass this December. The Timeline section near the end of this proposal lays out how the two relate.

### Three corridor tiers, sorted by what actually works at each distance

Brightline's own siting logic, that high-speed rail wins the 400-550 kilometer (250-350 mile) band against both driving and flying, is the single most useful empirical fact in this landscape. ARCA uses it to sort the national network into three tiers rather than funding every corridor the same way.

| Tier | Speed / mode | Distance logic | Candidate corridors | Financing tool |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: True HSR | 186+ mph, dedicated right-of-way | 250-450 miles, weak existing rail geometry, strong city-pair demand | Texas Triangle (Dallas-Houston-San Antonio-Austin); Cascadia (Vancouver-Seattle-Portland); a rebuilt Nevada-California link if Brightline West extends; Atlanta-Charlotte | Trust Fund advance-appropriated or competitive grants, plus RRIF loans and private capital (Gateway and Brightline West models) |
| 2: Higher-speed rail | 110-186 mph, upgraded shared or semi-dedicated track | 150-400 miles, existing freight or Amtrak corridor to build on | Southeast Corridor (DC-Richmond-Raleigh, extending to Charlotte and Atlanta); Chicago hub network (to St. Louis, Detroit, Twin Cities, Cincinnati); California's Merced-Bakersfield segment, reconceived as a Tier 2 anchor rather than a stranded Tier 1 fragment | Trust Fund formula and competitive grants, cost-shared with states |
| 3: Frequency and reliability | Existing speeds, more trips, better on-time performance | Any distance; the ridership-building layer | Most of ConnectUS's 39 corridors; long-distance route feeder connections; grade separations and station modernization nationwide | Formula funding through CRISI and the National Intercity Passenger Railroad Partnership Program |

The point of sorting this way is discipline about which pot of money a project draws from, not a mandate that every Tier 1 corridor prove itself as a slower service first. That second rule does not actually work: true HSR needs its own grade-separated, geometrically distinct alignment almost everywhere, so most Tier 1 corridors, Texas Triangle chief among them, have no legacy line capable of hosting a meaningful Tier 2 or 3 version at all. Requiring one before Tier 1 money can move would block exactly the corridors this framework most wants to build, and it is not how Brightline West itself got built: it went straight to construction on demand modeling and financing, not a prior slow train's revealed ridership.

The better rule, and the one Rep. Suzan DelBene has pushed for Cascadia specifically, is to pursue new-build Tier 1 planning and existing-track Tier 2 or 3 upgrades in parallel wherever a legacy corridor exists worth upgrading, rather than treating one as a precondition for the other. Where no legacy corridor exists at all, Tier 1 eligibility rests on the accountability gates described later in this proposal, secured non-federal match, completed procurement, independently reviewed ridership and revenue modeling, not on waiting for proof that a slower version already draws riders. California is the cautionary case for a narrower reason than sequencing: CAHSR's failure was a financing and accountability failure, missed deadlines, an undisclosed funding gap, a blown procurement date, not a failure to prove itself first at a lower tier, since no such lower-tier version of the SF-LA corridor ever existed to prove out in the first place.

### Financing architecture: advance-appropriated grants, formula and competitive funding, and loans

The 2026 experience argues for not depending on any single financing mechanism, not for replacing grants with loans. Long Bridge, the S-Line, and most of Gateway itself were built with federal grants, and grants have the strongest track record of anything in this document. The specific lesson from Gateway's own funding freeze is narrower: the piece of Gateway financed by an RRIF loan kept moving because loan debt service is not contingent on annual appropriations, while the grant-funded portions of the broader portfolio went dark instantly when the December 2026 stopgap hit. ARCA's answer is to diversify rather than pick a side: a genuine advance appropriation at enactment, recurring dedicated revenue that grows over time, and loan authority reserved for the specific projects where debt service fits the capital structure best.

First, it creates a permanent Intercity Passenger Rail Trust Fund with four legs, the most important of which is the one grants have always depended on:

- A genuine multi-year advance appropriation at enactment, sized at roughly $150-200 billion over FY2031-36, deliberately larger than IIJA's guaranteed $66 billion even after adjusting for a decade of inflation, sized to actually build the network described in this proposal rather than incrementally extend it. This is not a new financing philosophy, it is the mechanism that actually built everything succeeding in this document, Long Bridge, the S-Line, most of Gateway, and it is guaranteed the same way IIJA's money was: written directly into the reauthorization statute rather than appropriated annually, so a future stopgap cannot claw it back the way it clawed back $36.8 billion in December 2026.
- A dedicated two-tier aviation fee, described in full below, providing durable, growing recurring revenue that reduces, without eliminating, how large the next advance appropriation at the 2036 reauthorization needs to be.
- A freight capacity-benefit fee tied to federally funded capacity upgrades that measurably reduce freight dispatching delay, a modest, recurring, beneficiary-pays supplement, not a fee for hosting Amtrak, which freight railroads already get paid for under 49 U.S.C. 24308.
- A statutory mandatory floor beneath the advance appropriation: a smaller fixed annual transfer written into the statute as mandatory rather than discretionary spending, insulated the way Social Security's trust fund is, so guaranteed funding continues even after the initial advance appropriation is spent down, without requiring another five-year fight.

### How the Trust Fund actually pays for projects

Collecting the money differently from IIJA does not mean spending it differently. ARCA's Trust Fund disburses through the same three channels the Highway Trust Fund and FRA's existing grant programs already use, not through loans alone:

- Formula apportionments to the four regional commissions, and to states outside their footprint, a predictable, non-competitive multi-year allocation for Tier 2 and 3 work, the same planning certainty state DOTs already get from Highway Trust Fund formula money, so commissions can commit to multi-year capital programs instead of re-competing for every dollar.
- Competitive, discretionary grants for larger or novel Tier 1 and Tier 2 projects, continuing the Federal-State Partnership and CRISI model, now funded by a Trust Fund insulated from the annual appropriations process rather than by vulnerable annual appropriations.
- RRIF loan guarantees and interest-rate buy-downs specifically for the most capital-intensive Tier 1 projects, where debt backed by future farebox and value-capture revenue fits the capital structure better than an outright grant, the model Gateway and Brightline West already use.

Most of the country's rail money, under this design, still arrives as a grant, formula or competitive. Loans are the tool for a specific kind of project, greenfield Tier 1 corridors with credible future revenue to borrow against, not a replacement for the broader grant system.

### Sizing the whole package against IIJA

IIJA's $102 billion in total rail funding over FY2022-26, $66 billion guaranteed plus $36 billion authorized, is the wrong baseline for a proposal meant to seriously build the network described here rather than sustain it. Run the numbers: $102 billion in 2021 dollars is roughly $135-145 billion by 2031 after a decade of inflation alone, and ARCA is deliberately sized well above even that inflation-adjusted floor.

| Financing leg | FY2031-36 total | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advance appropriation | $150-200 billion | Guaranteed at enactment, the primary lever for scale |
| RRIF loan authority (expanded) | $150 billion | Debt capacity, not appropriated spending; backed by future revenue |
| Aviation fee, both layers | Roughly $30-35 billion cumulative | Recurring, still growing at the end of the window |
| Freight capacity-benefit fee | Roughly $2 billion cumulative | Modest and recurring |
| Mandatory transfer floor | Roughly $18 billion cumulative | Continues past 2036 without another fight |
| Total appropriated and fee revenue | Roughly $355-405 billion |  |
| Total capital mobilized, including RRIF | Roughly $505-555 billion |  |

Even the conservative, appropriated-and-fee-only total is two and a half to three times IIJA's inflation-adjusted equivalent; including RRIF's expanded loan capacity, ARCA mobilizes roughly three and a half to four times as much capital as IIJA did in real terms. That scale is not arbitrary: Brightline West cost about $12 billion for one 218-mile corridor and Gateway about $16 billion for one 10-mile bottleneck, so a serious national buildout of 3-5 genuine Tier 1 corridors plus a real Tier 2 and 3 network nationwide, ConnectUS's own $75 billion National Network ask alone would be roughly $100 billion in 2031 dollars, is a several-hundred-billion-dollar undertaking, not a $100 billion one. Sizing ARCA this way is what makes the difference between a program that funds a handful of demonstration projects and one that can actually finish what this document's own vanguard chapter describes as still, five years on, mostly unbuilt.

This does not eliminate political risk, nothing fully does, but it converts an annual fight into a decennial one.

### The dedicated aviation fee: a two-tier design

Short-haul flights and rail compete directly for the same trips, so the aviation piece of the Trust Fund's blended base gets its own two-tier structure, with a clean precedent: it mirrors how highway funding already works, a universal gas tax into the general Highway Trust Fund, plus tolls on specific facilities that flow directly to the agency that built them.

Layer one, universal, feeds the general Trust Fund: a flat fee on all domestic enplanements, not just short-haul, starting at $4 a ticket in 2031 and stepping up by roughly $1 a year to $8 by 2036, then indexed to CPI so it does not quietly erode the way the unchanged-since-2001 September 11th security fee has. That raises roughly $3.4 billion a year at the outset and about $6.9 billion a year once fully phased in. A flat fee was chosen over a percentage of fare for administrative simplicity, one number everyone understands, at the cost of needing that explicit inflation adjustment a percentage fee would have provided automatically; the sensitivity table below shows what alternative structures and levels would have raised instead.

Layer two, corridor-specific, flows directly to that corridor's own regional commission rather than the general fund: a $10-15 surcharge on flights competing with a specific Tier 1 or Tier 2 corridor. Scoping matters as much as the revenue here. A blanket national short-haul tax would hit Honolulu-Maui or a rural hub feeder exactly as hard as Las Vegas-Los Angeles, even though only routes like the latter have any real prospect of a rail alternative, bad policy, taxing people with no substitute good available, and bad politics, since it would alienate the rural and hub-dependent members of Congress this proposal needs. France's actual rule, often cited as the model, is narrower than its reputation: it bars domestic flights only on routes where a sub-2.5-hour train already exists. ARCA can't copy that trigger directly, since it needs this revenue while a corridor is still under construction, not after; tying the surcharge to an operating train would collect nothing on exactly the greenfield corridors, Texas Triangle above all, that most need the money. The fix is to trigger the surcharge on Corridor ID Program selection with a funded Service Development Plan instead: once FRA and the state have formally committed real institutional resources to a city pair, flights competing with that future corridor start paying into the fund building it, years before track opens, exactly the window when the fund needs the money most. That keeps the list bounded and defensible, it rides on an existing federal selection process rather than route-by-route congressional discretion, and it is not a new idea structurally: highway users have always paid into the gas tax for roads not yet built. The honest cost is that an early flier on, say, Dallas-Houston in year two of construction pays a tax for a train that will not open for years; ARCA's own accountability gates are the safeguard, since a corridor that misses its milestones and loses its Corridor ID status loses the surcharge automatically too.

Ballparking three example corridors, using a $10-15 per-ticket surcharge and treating these as rough orders of magnitude, not scored figures:

| Corridor | Air passenger base | Rough annual surcharge revenue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Southern California-Las Vegas (Brightline West's corridor) | 1.34 million on LAX-LAS alone in 2024; plausibly 4-5 million across all Southern California airports serving Vegas (Burbank, Ontario, Long Beach, Orange County, San Diego) | Roughly $40-75 million a year |
| Texas Triangle (Dallas, Houston, Austin, San Antonio combined) | A dense short-hop network across six city pairs; a reasonable current estimate is 3-5 million annual passengers combined | Roughly $30-75 million a year |
| Cascadia (Seattle-Portland-Vancouver) | A smaller market; combined Seattle-Portland and Seattle-Vancouver traffic is plausibly 1-2 million annual passengers | Roughly $10-30 million a year |

Even added together, three of the strongest candidate corridors plausibly raise somewhere in the neighborhood of $80-180 million a year, real, durable revenue well suited to backing loan debt service, but a small fraction of what any single Tier 1 corridor costs to build. That confirms the point made above: this mechanism is a meaningful supplement to the Trust Fund's other legs, not a primary construction funding source on its own.

For context, a blanket national version with no Corridor ID scoping would raise far more: U.S. domestic airlines carried roughly 860 million enplanements in 2025, and at the ICCT's estimate that short-haul trips are about half the domestic market, a $10-15 surcharge on all of them would raise something like $4.3-6.5 billion a year. That is real money, but it is also the version this proposal argues against: it would tax Honolulu-Maui and rural hub feeders at the same rate as Las Vegas-Los Angeles. The scoped, Corridor ID-triggered version starts far smaller and grows only as more corridors actually enter the federal pipeline, a deliberate trade of less money now for a tax base that expands in lockstep with genuine rail buildout and gives opponents no rural-fairness argument to make.

### Sensitivity: flat fee versus a percentage, at several levels

For reference, here is what alternative structures and levels for the universal layer would have raised, against 860 million annual domestic enplanements at an average one-way fare of about $280-300.

| Structure | Level | Annual revenue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flat, per enplanement | $1 | About $0.9 billion |
| Flat, per enplanement | $2 | About $1.7 billion |
| Flat, per enplanement | $3 | About $2.6 billion |
| Flat, per enplanement | $5 | About $4.3 billion |
| Flat, per enplanement | $7.50 (matching the existing September 11th security fee) | About $6.5 billion |
| Percentage of fare | 0.5% (about $1.40 a ticket) | About $1.2 billion |
| Percentage of fare | 1% (about $2.80 a ticket) | About $2.4 billion |
| Percentage of fare | 1.5% (about $4.20 a ticket) | About $3.6 billion |
| Percentage of fare | 2% (about $5.60 a ticket) | About $4.8 billion |
| Percentage of fare | 3% (about $8.40 a ticket, roughly double the existing 7.5% federal ticket tax's effective add) | About $7.2 billion |

The two structures behave differently over time in a way that matters for a fund meant to last decades. A flat fee is fixed in nominal dollars, so it erodes with inflation exactly like the gas tax has unless indexed by statute, which is why ARCA ties its flat, stepped fee to CPI after 2036 rather than leaving it fixed the way the September 11th security fee has sat since 2001. A percentage fee would have scaled automatically with fare growth instead, at the cost of less predictable annual revenue, and would have been closer to proportional (more from a $500 last-minute fare than a $150 advance-purchase one) where a flat fee takes the same dollar regardless. ARCA's choice of a flat fee trades that automatic indexing for administrative simplicity, one number everyone understands, a trade worth making given how directly it strengthens the political case for the fee in the first place.

Second, it substantially expands RRIF's loan authority, from its current $35 billion statutory ceiling, most of it unused, to roughly $150 billion, with a federal interest-rate buy-down for state and interstate rail authorities explicitly modeled on the Gateway Development Commission's financing. That is enough headroom for several Tier 1 corridors to each draw debt at Brightline West or Gateway scale ($4-9 billion apiece) simultaneously, rather than competing against freight short lines and transit-oriented development projects for a shared $35 billion pool. Tier 1 projects get capitalized with debt backed by future farebox and value-capture revenue rather than depending entirely on grants that can be frozen or zeroed out in a single continuing resolution.

Third, it makes the Nationally Significant Rail Station Modernization Act's station-area development authority, already added to BUILD America 250 by amendment, the default tool for every Tier 1 and Tier 2 hub station, so major stations become revenue-generating assets (offices, retail, housing above and around the station) the way Hudson Yards development already functions around Gateway, rather than pure cost centers.

### Accountability: catching the next California before the money is gone

CAHSR's inspector general found what mattered too late: an undisclosed schedule slip, an unmet funding-gap disclosure requirement, and a missed trainset procurement deadline the authority swore to a federal judge it would hit. Notably, the inspector general did not fault the authority for having match dollars that were merely authorized instead of obligated; it faulted the authority for representing its funding situation to a federal court as more secure than it actually was. ARCA writes that actual lesson into law as mandatory, public, milestone gates for every Tier 1 and Tier 2 corridor: no second tranche of federal capital releases until an independent reviewer publicly certifies the prior tranche's non-federal match at its true legal status, authorized, appropriated, obligated, or contingent on the federal award itself, rather than requiring every dollar to already be spendable before releasing more. That distinction matters because the non-federal match is often itself public money from another level of government, a state matching a federal grant, and a state legislature often cannot or will not fully obligate its own share until the federal award is secured, while the federal program often will not finalize its own award until the state match looks secure. FRA's own Federal-State Partnership program already builds around exactly this problem, using what it calls "contingent commitment authority" to pledge multi-year federal funding conditionally rather than requiring it all in hand at once; ARCA's gates make two-sided contingent commitments, federal and state funding each conditionally pledged pending the other's finalization, with conditions and timeline disclosed publicly, the standard rather than the exception. The gate also requires the rolling stock procurement to be under contract and updated ridership and revenue projections reconciled against the original business case with public variance reporting. This is not a punitive measure aimed at California; it is a standing process that would have flagged CAHSR's problems in 2019 rather than 2026, while non-Amtrak operators bidding on Tier 1 or Tier 2 corridors (which BUILD America 250 already permits) face the same gates so private capital cannot game looser oversight than public authorities face.

### Institutions: permanent corridor commissions, not five-year sunsets

BUILD America 250 sunsets the Corridor ID Program's new-corridor selections after October 1, 2031. ARCA makes Corridor ID permanent and replicates the Northeast Corridor Commission model, the one multi-state rail governance body that has actually functioned continuously since 2015, into four new permanent regional bodies: a Southeast Corridor Commission (VA, NC, SC, GA, FL, plus Amtrak and FRA, building directly on the VPRA-NCDOT partnership already coordinating Long Bridge and the S-Line), a Cascadia Corridor Commission (WA, OR, BC, plus FRA, formalizing the existing multi-jurisdictional planning process), a Midwest Hub Commission (IL, WI, MI, IN, OH, MN, MO, around Chicago), and a Texas Triangle Commission. Each gets a small, permanent formula allocation for planning staff so momentum does not reset every time a grant cycle or a presidential administration changes, which is precisely what has repeatedly stalled Cascadia and the Southeast Corridor for a decade at a time.

On Amtrak's own restructuring, ARCA takes no position on whether the holding-company model is correct, that is an operational question for Amtrak's board, but it statutorily guarantees that (1) existing collective bargaining agreements and union representation transfer intact to any successor entity, foreclosing the privatization-by-reorganization path unions fear, and (2) the National Network subsidiary, whatever it is ultimately called, retains statutory obligation to serve every current long-distance and state-supported route, so restructuring cannot become a backdoor for route discontinuation.

### Timeline: an interim bill now, ARCA in 2031

The realistic sequence has two distinct stages, not one continuous build-out starting in 2027.

| Phase | Years | Primary goals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interim: BUILD America 250 Plus | FY2027-2031 | The December 2026 lame-duck deal described above passes and governs the current cycle; Gateway, Long Bridge, and the S-Line complete on their existing schedules; Brightline West opens (targeted late 2029); a right-sized Central Valley segment of CAHSR opens as a going concern; Corridor ID keeps operating on its existing 2031 sunset while ARCA's case builds |
| ARCA Phase 1: Foundation | 2031-2033 | Enact ARCA as the passenger rail title of the 2031 reauthorization; stand up Trust Fund revenue collection and RRIF's expanded authority, both statutory and administrative changes usable within about a year, not a from-scratch institution; finalize accountability-gate regulations through standard FRA rulemaking; whichever corridor already has a complete, funded Service Development Plan by 2031 clears the gates and moves into final design and procurement. Realistically that favors an extension of the Southeast Corridor over Texas Triangle or Cascadia: NEPA review has historically taken years even with money and political will behind it, and the Southeast Corridor already has a cleared 2015 Tier II Environmental Impact Statement for the Raleigh-Richmond segment to extend from, while Texas Triangle has no comparable head start and Cascadia's own planning assumes a 2040s service date. Genuine permitting reform, a stated bipartisan priority in both BUILD America 250 and DOT's own reauthorization requests, could compress this further, but reform has been promised in every reauthorization cycle without fully delivering, so treat it as upside, not a baseline assumption |
| ARCA Phase 2: First groundbreakings | 2032-2036 | The lead corridor or corridors break ground within a year or two of clearing the gates above, not after a full five-year wait; the four regional commissions finish forming and staffing in parallel with that construction rather than as a precondition for it; Southeast and Cascadia's Tier 2 upgrades come online on their own, generally faster, timelines; integrate ticketing and scheduling across Amtrak, state authorities, and private operators |
| ARCA Phase 3: National maturity | 2036-2051 | Most of ConnectUS's 39 corridors operating at Tier 2 or 3 standard; 3-5 operating Tier 1 HSR lines; passenger rail genuinely time-competitive with driving and short-haul flying across the core 100-500 mile national travel market |

This pushes ARCA's own horizon out to roughly 2051, a quarter-century from today, deliberately slower than Amtrak's original 15-year ConnectUS ask and far slower than Moulton-DelBene's five-year, $205 billion version. Both of those timelines assumed a funding and institutional stability that neither this reauthorization cycle nor Amtrak's own restructuring has shown exists. Treating the December 2026 deal as a bridge, not the finish line, and building the structural reforms into a reauthorization both parties will have five more years to actually want is worth more than pretending this Congress will do in one lame-duck session what it has not managed in any of the last four reauthorization cycles.

## Political strategy: why this clears the Senate

The honest starting point is that ARCA is not this cycle's bill. The lame-duck session after the November 3 midterms is likely to deliver "BUILD America 250 Plus," described above, not a structural overhaul: Congress rarely absorbs more than incremental change in a single reauthorization, and a bipartisan committee product with a retiring chairman's name already on it is not the vehicle for a new trust fund and four new interstate commissions. ARCA's real target is the reauthorization after that, due in 2031, by which point the interim bill's own limits, still-vulnerable annual-appropriations funding, an unextended Corridor ID Program, no dedicated revenue stream, will have had five years to make the case for something more durable on their own.

Three things make ARCA's core pieces more winnable than a straight funding fight. First, the RRIF-and-trust-fund financing model answers Capito's stated preference (consolidate programs, avoid top-down mandates, give states flexibility) better than BUILD America 250 does on rail: a trust fund with permanent regional commissions is a states-empowering structure, not a new Washington bureaucracy, and loan-based financing is the kind of fiscal instrument that has historically drawn Republican support even when direct grants do not, TIFIA and RRIF both passed with bipartisan votes for exactly this reason. Second, Tier 3 frequency and reliability money reaches red and purple states directly. Virginia and North Carolina's projects are the proof: this is not a coastal-blue-state-only coalition, and a Southeast Corridor Commission gives Republican-held Virginia and North Carolina delegations a direct stake in defending the framework in conference. Third, bundling accountability gates into the bill answers the strongest good-faith Republican objection to rail spending, that it becomes CAHSR, with a mechanism rather than a talking point; a member skeptical of rail spending has less to object to in a bill that makes another CAHSR statutorily harder to happen.

The real risks are less about the policy's merits than about timing and institutional churn: whether Amtrak's restructuring is far enough along by 2027 that funding mechanisms can attach to a stable structure, whether the midterms produce a Congress willing to write a real five-year bill instead of another extension, and whether California's continued struggles become the dominant public narrative about rail generally rather than the specific accountability failure this framework is built to prevent. None of those risks are reasons to wait; they are reasons the financing and accountability architecture needs to be locked into statute now, while the political coalition (Graves and Larsen's bipartisan committee vote, Virginia and North Carolina's demonstrated execution, Brightline's private-capital model) is more aligned than it has been in a generation.
