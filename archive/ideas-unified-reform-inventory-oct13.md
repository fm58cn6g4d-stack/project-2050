## 17. Amendments, in ratification order

*Sequenced by coalition breadth, not importance. The first ratification is the proof of concept.*

3. **National popular vote** — Bayh-Celler lineage; real opposition is seven swing states, not twenty-five small ones.

5. **Affirmative individual right to vote.**
6. **23rd Amendment repeal.**

**Ratification operations ⟳**
- Name the 38 target states; sequence by coalition rather than geography
- Model legislation and an interstate legislator coordination body

# Assessment

## Scale

**Roughly 190 items.** For calibration: a productive first Congress with a trifecta and rules reform passes perhaps twenty-five to thirty significant bills. Tier 0 alone — the ① statutory items — runs to about ninety.

This is not a two-year agenda. It is a **twenty-year program with a two-year opening**, which is the correct frame and also the one that requires saying out loud. Project 2025 was the product of an iterative exercise begun in 1980. The equivalent here is a Project 2050 whose first Congress does perhaps a quarter of the list and whose principal output is a durable movement infrastructure.

## What the compiled list reveals that the pieces did not

**The proportions are wrong, and diagnostically so.** Roughly 60% of these items are statutory, 20% state-level, 12% amendments, and under 5% executive or international. That distribution reveals a bias I built in without noticing: I reached for Congress because Congress is where reform *ought* to happen, not because it is where change is most available. Executive action is fast, cheap, and reversible — which makes it wrong for durable reform and right for the interim, and I have almost nothing in that column.

**Section 5 should have been section 1.** The executive-power reforms are the ones most directly demanded by recent experience, most likely to attract residual institutionalist Republican support, and closest to what actually broke. Building an agenda about representation and courts while omitting emergency powers, impoundment, and the Insurrection Act was a serious misallocation of attention, and it happened because representation reform is the more intellectually interesting problem.

**Capacity is the hidden prerequisite everywhere.** Enlargement without staffing makes the House worse. Delivery without civil service reform means benefits arrive after the election. Permitting reform gates every physical-world outcome. These items poll at nothing, excite no one, and gate roughly half the list.

**The anti-corruption cluster is the most underpriced thing here.** It is the highest-polling democracy category in the country, the most legible to non-ideological voters, the easiest to legislate, and the natural rhetorical frame for reforms that lose when framed as good government. Alaska's system survived a repeal attempt on exactly this framing. It should probably lead the public argument even though it is not the most structurally consequential cluster.

## The four real constraints

1. **Floor time.** Under reformed cloture there are perhaps 148 discretionary floor days. The ① statutory items exceed 300. The cut happens whether or not anyone plans it.
2. **Reconciliation eligibility.** What moves money rides free. What changes a rule takes the floor. This sorts the agenda more powerfully than ideology and produces the submerged-state pathology as a byproduct.
3. **Attention.** More binding than floor time. A president can make perhaps three things salient in a year. Ninety bills is one thing plus eighty-nine invisible ones — which is survivable substantively and dangerous politically, since backlash tracks volume while reward tracks salience.
4. **Ratification.** Six amendments is more than the country has ratified since 1971. Realistically this list contains one plausible amendment in twenty years, and it should be chosen as such rather than pursued in parallel.

## Where the list is still thin

**Federalism.** There is no theory here of what states should do versus the federal government, and the conditional-funding mechanism is doing quiet load-bearing work in several items that *NFIB* made shakier than I have acknowledged.

**Implementation and sequencing within domains.** The list is an inventory, not a plan. Which twenty-five items constitute the actual first Congress is the next question and it is a harder one.

**The opposition's response.** Every item here has a countermeasure, and several — commissions, RCV, statehood — have already generated pre-emptive state-level bans. An agenda that does not model the counter-move is a wish list.

**Cost and financing.** Section 10 and 11 have real fiscal weight and I have not scored any of it. The wealth-taxation item is doing enormous unexamined work as the funding base for everything.

**Failure modes.** What happens if the Court strikes the FRA, the term-limits statute, and the commission mandate in a single term? There is no contingency architecture, and the pre-committed-expansion trigger is the only item that even gestures at one.

## The honest bottom line

The list is comprehensive enough to be useful and long enough to be dangerous. Its principal value is as an inventory to select from, not a program to attempt.

The selection criteria that the modeling supports: **highest structural yield per floor day, sequenced so that recurring and legible delivery is flowing before the midterm, with every structural item paired to a material one, and every item passing the reciprocity test.** By those criteria the first Congress is closer to twenty-five items than ninety, and the correct next exercise is choosing which twenty-five — knowing that the rest is a twenty-year program whose first job is surviving 2030.
