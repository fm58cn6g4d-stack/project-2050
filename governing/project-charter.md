## 1. What could be different

*Imagine a parent walking a child to school in 2050. They pass apartments, a grocery store and a library that opens early enough for people working the first shift to use it. The family has enough room in its budget to plan an outing and put something aside. Missing a shift would still be difficult, but it would no longer threaten to set off a succession of unpaid bills, lost child care and possible eviction.*

*An older neighbor’s daughter is starting a business. Until recently, much of her time went toward arranging her mother’s care, filling the gaps between services and worrying about what would happen if she became unavailable. Her mother now has dependable support at home. Both of them have more say in how they spend their days.*

These are ordinary changes, but their implications extend well beyond the households experiencing them. A parent who can plan beyond the end of the week has more freedom to pursue an education, look for better work or become involved in their community. A person who receives dependable care can exercise choices that would otherwise exist largely on paper. Their relatives gain time as well, while the people providing that care gain the opportunity to make a sustainable living from work on which others depend.

Project 2050 begins with the ambition to make this kind of freedom more widely available. I want an America that is fundamentally more fair, connected and hopeful, and in which people can see meaningful progress being made. That requires institutions and goals sized to the challenges of the twenty-first century: meeting the climate crisis, preparing for an economy changed by AI, caring for an aging population and renewing a democracy whose integrity and capacity to deliver are under strain.

The project asks how a government could undertake these tasks while remaining accountable to the people whose lives it affects. A more capable state should be accompanied by a more engaged citizenry, with meaningful opportunities to shape public decisions, scrutinize the use of power and challenge mistakes. The government’s ability to act and people’s ability to influence that action belong together in the design.

This is also an argument about a renewed American dream. Everyone should have a fair shot at a good life, including the security to make plans, the opportunity to contribute, the time to maintain relationships and the power to take part in decisions about a shared future. The project’s purpose is to connect that ambition to a coherent governing program and explain what would make it possible.

The scenes in this charter are fictional illustrations of that purpose. They are not forecasts or modeled outcomes, and some details imply choices the adopted program has not settled. They help identify what the analysis needs to explain. In the morning described above, for example, it matters whether a family receives the benefits for which it is eligible, whether care and housing are actually available, and whether new costs absorb the gains. FAM-1, FAM-2 and the education and housing agendas provide starting points for answering those questions; their presence in the program does not establish that every result in the scene follows from them.

## 2. The commitments behind the program

The project’s first commitment is to give everyone a fair shot at a good life. Coming as close to eradicating child poverty as possible is a defining part of that ambition. Success should therefore be assessed partly by what happens to the children and families who remain excluded, including those facing the deepest deprivation or the greatest difficulty obtaining assistance. An improvement in the average household’s circumstances would be valuable, but it would leave this central question unanswered.

A second commitment is to renew the social pact through which we care for people who are sick, elderly, disabled or otherwise vulnerable. A promise of coverage needs a system capable of providing care, and that system depends on the people doing the work. The project should examine affordability, quality, workforce conditions and family preferences together, including the burdens carried by unpaid caregivers. The aim is to reinforce these systems so that people can rely on them for decades.

These material commitments depend on a capable and accountable democracy. Public institutions need the authority, expertise, resources and continuity to deliver, while people need effective ways to influence their decisions and obtain remedies when they fail. Professional competence, credible statistics and independent scrutiny should survive changes in political control. A useful test of a structural reform is whether we would accept its exercise by a government we opposed, consistent with the project’s governing rule of reciprocity.

The project also seeks a more connected society, in which people have opportunities to participate in work larger than themselves. Climate preparation, public investment and the care of shared places can provide such opportunities, but participation requires institutions that make it worthwhile and possible. People with little spare time, limited income, a disability or reasons to distrust government should still have meaningful ways to take part. Libraries, public spaces, workplaces and civic organizations belong in this account because they can help create the conditions for cooperation.

Finally, Project 2050 should help society prepare for change and govern with a longer view. Climate mitigation and adaptation require sustained decisions about investment, land, infrastructure and risk. AI raises questions about productivity and employment, and also about bargaining power, ownership and who shapes the use of new technology. In both cases, early improvements need to be connected to institutions capable of maintaining progress, recognizing failures and changing course.

These commitments explain what the instruments are for. They should allow us to reconsider an instrument when evidence suggests a better way to serve the same purpose. Any change to an adopted policy still requires Espen’s decision and incorporation into the authoritative project record.

## 3. A possible future, and the choices that would produce it

The annotated bibliography provides substantial resources for moving from a high-level governing agenda toward more specific designs. Its strongest contribution is to identify methods for assessing what institutions would need to do: how to estimate costs, construct schedules, plan a workforce, organize participation and evaluate results. It also helps distinguish findings about the world from possibilities that depend on choices we have yet to make.

Climate research, for example, gives us reason to examine both the benefits of acting and the harms that action may not prevent. The IPCC assessments describe increasing hazards with additional warming, available responses and limits to adaptation. U.S. assessments and resilience tools help connect those questions to particular regions and communities. A credible public vision can therefore explore cleaner energy, more comfortable homes and better-prepared places, while also showing why some losses, difficult decisions and continuing adjustments remain. A successful U.S. program would contribute to the global response; it would not determine global warming on its own. [R4]

### Preparing for a difficult season

*A river community has spent years preparing for a flood it hoped would never come. Warnings reach residents in languages they understand, and people who need assistance know whom to contact. A school can provide cooling and shelter when the grid fails. Some homes have been raised. Other households chose to move, with support that allowed them to remain near work and family.*

*There are still losses. In the following months, residents can inspect how rebuilding money is allocated and challenge a decision. Upstream, a rural town has a different plan because its roads, employers and hazards are different. Both plans depend on years of maintenance and on people whose work is rarely visible until something goes wrong.*

This example makes clear why the climate agenda needs to connect housing, energy, transport, health and local finance. Preparing a community requires decisions about where to build, how to maintain essential services, who can afford insurance and what happens when staying becomes unsafe. The analysis must identify who bears concentrated costs and disruption, what support and influence they have, and which authority is responsible for the final decision. CLI-6 through CLI-8 are relevant starting points, though the scene does not establish that their present designs or budgets are sufficient.

The same attention to place should shape the public work more generally. Metropolitan neighborhoods, suburbs, rural towns and coastal communities face different opportunities and constraints. Tribal sovereignty and government-to-government responsibilities require distinct treatment. An appealing neighborhood can illustrate a possibility without standing in for the country as a whole.

AI presents a different uncertainty. Workplace studies identify productivity gains in particular settings, while task-exposure studies help describe which activities might change. Those findings do not establish a single trajectory for national employment through 2050. Moving from an effect in a workplace to a claim about wages, labor demand or economic growth requires additional assumptions. [R5–R6]

### Working with new tools

*A worker uses a tool that now handles a routine part of the job, leaving more time to work through a difficult problem with a colleague. They helped negotiate how the tool would be used, and they can request human review of a disputed evaluation. Across town, someone whose occupation has contracted is entering a new field through a paid placement. There is an employer involved and a credible opening at the end of the training.*

*Other workers have had a harder transition. Some have lost income or a sense of purpose, and some employers have used the same technologies to increase control over the people who remain.*

The difference between these experiences is part of what the project needs to explain. Training is more useful when it connects to actual opportunities, and increased productivity does not settle how its gains will be distributed. The analysis should compare entry-level hiring, displacement, wage replacement, job quality and worker influence across several possible futures. AI-4 and AI-5 provide relevant policy anchors. The paid placement and negotiated arrangement in this scene remain illustrative; they do not adopt AI-4E, AI-4F or another unchosen instrument.

Care and housing raise a related question about the relationship between a benefit and the thing it is meant to make available. The bibliography’s care sources identify workforce, payment and state-delivery constraints, while its housing sources identify financing, regulation, construction and differences among local markets. These findings direct attention to the systems through which a commitment becomes an experience in someone’s life.

### Having care people can rely on

*An older adult chooses to remain at home. Their care worker has dependable hours, enough time for the visit and a reason to stay in the profession. When the person’s needs change, someone helps them and their family understand the available options. Their daughter still provides care, but spends less time repairing gaps between services and more time simply being with her parent.*

For this arrangement to work, the plan needs an account of the care worker’s pay and training, the availability of providers, reimbursement, geography and the family’s preferences. HLT-4 and the wider care agenda offer starting points. Service coordination remains a design question, and a coverage expansion cannot by itself establish that people will be able to obtain care.

These examples suggest a common approach to the future we describe. We should ask what people could gain, explain the choices and mechanisms that might produce those gains, and examine the conditions under which the result would be different. That allows the public work to be ambitious while giving the professional analysis questions it can investigate.

## 4. Democratic participation and a government people can see working

The project’s interest in visible progress follows from a concern with people’s actual experience of government. A benefit that arrives on time, an appeal that is resolved and a transport connection that can be relied upon all change what people can do. Public records should make these improvements inspectable, including the cases that still fail and the groups that continue to be treated unequally.

It is important, however, to distinguish the delivery of a service from the legitimacy of the institution providing it. An agency can become faster without giving people greater influence, and a person can receive a benefit while continuing to distrust the government. The project should investigate the relationships among delivery, experience, fairness, political influence and trust rather than assuming that improvement in one establishes improvement in the others.

Research on deliberative processes offers concrete ways to think about public influence. Recruitment, facilitation, accessible evidence and a defined remit affect who participates and what they can contribute. A clear response from the responsible authority matters because it establishes what participation can actually change. The OECD’s principles provide a useful starting point, while their application to U.S. institutions requires further analysis. [R7]

### Taking part in a public decision

*Residents meet about a regional investment proposal. Someone who would otherwise lose wages is compensated for their time, and care and accessibility needs have been addressed. Participants can examine competing evidence, question the people presenting it and understand which decisions remain open. The elected authority has explained what it must do with their recommendations.*

*Several residents oppose the eventual outcome. They can organize and challenge an unlawful decision. Months later, the public record explains which recommendations were accepted, which were rejected and what happened afterward.*

The value of this process depends on the authority attached to it, the rights it protects and the people it reaches. Participation should complement representative institutions and remain possible for people who cannot make public deliberation a second job. DEM-18’s planned pilots provide a place to test these questions. Compensation and care support in the example are proposed design features to examine, rather than newly adopted entitlements.

Evaluation should be equally open to unwelcome findings. Measures need enough independence that an agency or governing coalition cannot define success simply by choosing what is easiest to count. Surveys and qualitative research can help establish which changes people value and perceive, including among people outside the governing coalition. The public work should make room for that scrutiny, and for the possibility that experience challenges the project’s expectations.

## 5. What Project 2050 should produce

The project has two connected levels. The public level should synthesize the analysis into bold, creative and sometimes provocative accounts of a possible future, presented through narrative work and an interactive explorer. The professional level should provide the governing blueprint and analytical resources through which readers can examine how those possibilities might be achieved.

Politically engaged, policy-curious readers are the initial public audience. Resources for people working in government and policy should develop alongside that work, early enough to shape its claims. A reader ought to be able to begin with a household or community experience and follow it into the proposed policy, evidence, funding and delivery requirements. Someone preparing legislation should be able to enter directly at that more detailed level.

| Product                                                           | Intended use                                                                           | What it needs to contain                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Public vision and explorer**                              | Help readers imagine possibilities, understand choices and consider how to participate | Narrative chapters or essays, maps, household examples and selected comparisons, with accessible explanations and visible assumptions                         |
| **Governing blueprint and professional resources** | Help readers assess, adapt, legislate, cost and plan the program                       | Policy dossiers, legislative specifications, implementation playbooks, evidence records and reproducible models, each with a stated purpose and review status |
| **Focused policy contributions**                              | Answer a concrete question for an office, organization, researcher or other audience   | Briefs, papers, presentations and, eventually, advice drawn from maintained analysis and checked against the context of the request                           |

These products should develop through an exchange between imagination and investigation. A future scene can expose an unmet need, and an implementation review can reveal an opportunity the original agenda overlooked. A prototype can also show whether readers understand an explanation or want to inspect a different part of the evidence.

The explorer will need to be clear about what its interactions do. Navigating a proposal, illustrating an alternative and calculating an outcome make different claims on the underlying analysis. A numerical control needs a defined relationship to a model and a range within which the result is meaningful. Qualitative exploration can be useful before those calculations exist, provided the distinction is visible.

Over time, the professional foundation should be able to produce focused policy contributions when called upon. An advising role would grow from components that have been tested and maintained, with their limitations understood. It should be possible to use a well-developed part of the project before every domain reaches the same depth.

## 6. Building the analytical foundation

The review of the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) exposed a gap between allocating resources to a goal and explaining how existing institutions would deliver it. The bibliography provides methods for addressing that gap, but much of the connective analysis still needs to be done. For each major proposal, we need to understand the problem and causal mechanism, the current institutional setting, the proposed change, who would carry it out and what that work would require.

Policy dossiers should bring these questions together at a depth appropriate to their consequences and intended use. They should identify affected populations, alternatives, existing law and programs, responsible organizations, enactment routes, resources, delivery requirements, distributional effects and uncertainty. The roles of federal, state, local and Tribal governments, as well as private organizations, need to be explicit where they matter.

Some of the most important work will cross policy boundaries. Eligibility, identity, payments and tax administration can support several benefits; care and construction programs can draw on the same workers; climate and housing investments can depend on the same grid. We cannot assess each proposal independently and assume that adding the results gives an adequate account of the whole agenda. Shared systems may create opportunities to reuse resources, but they may also create bottlenecks or a point of failure for several programs at once.

### Connecting required work to existing resources

A government resource ledger should help us compare proposed work with the institutions and resources already in place. Research into federal practice provides methods for making that comparison. Agencies use budget submissions and execution reports, cost-accounting methods, workforce plans, acquisition plans and investment reviews for different parts of this task. Project 2050 should connect the evidence from those processes while retaining the distinctions among them. Appendix C documents the sources and the development specification in more detail. [L1–L9]

The first version should be a small, reviewable planning workbook. It would describe a policy’s delivery requirements, trace relevant existing resources, compare feasible implementation alternatives and explain the remaining cost and funding questions. Its purpose would be to support a reasoned proposal, with a clear record of what public evidence can establish and what still needs agency or specialist verification.

| Linked record | What it would contain | What it contributes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Programs, organizations and funding accounts** | Stable project IDs and official identifiers where available; legal purpose; responsible organizations; account and funding-period information; changes in organizational scope | A map connecting policy requirements to government structures without assuming that a program and an account are the same thing |
| **Budget and spending observations** | Amount, unit, fiscal year or reporting period, source vintage, request/enacted/actual/estimate status, budget measure, reporting scope and non-additive components | Comparable financial evidence, kept separate from assumptions about availability |
| **Required work and implementation alternatives** | Existing policy and work-package IDs, deliverables, workload, service standards, alternatives, schedule, dependencies and proposed responsibility | A defined basis for estimating the work and comparing ways to deliver it |
| **Workforce, contracts and systems** | Skills and workload assumptions; headcount versus full-time equivalents (FTE); contract identifiers and scope; system functions; existing commitments; migration, maintenance and acquisition needs | Evidence about capability and constraints, rather than an interchangeable pool of resources |
| **Cost assignments and funding bridges** | Links among work, resources and cost estimates; allocation drivers; federal/state shares; costs included elsewhere; incremental costs; candidate funding routes and unresolved restrictions | A traceable account of shared costs, additional requirements and possible double counting |
| **Evidence, review and decisions** | Source passages or table locations; retrieval dates; definitions; mapping confidence; discrepancies; reviewer role; decisions and revision history | A record another analyst can inspect and challenge |

The financial records need to preserve the difference between a budget request, enacted funding, an operating-plan estimate and recorded spending. They should also preserve whether a figure is an annual flow or a balance measured at a date. A subtotal, its components and a later report of the same spending must not become three separate costs. Changes in agency structure or reporting categories need an explicit bridge before historical figures are compared. CMS’s published documents provide concrete examples of why these safeguards matter. [L2–L4]

Costing and funding should then be assessed separately. Federal managerial cost-accounting standards provide a useful order of preference: trace a cost directly where practicable, use a causal relationship where supported, and otherwise document a reasonable, consistent allocation. That can help us estimate the cost of providing a service. It does not establish which appropriation can pay for it. A separate funding assessment must examine purpose, period, existing commitments and required approvals. The incremental resource cost should be measured against an explicit, comparable without-proposal case; the amount of new funding required needs its own explanation. [L5]

The same discipline applies to people and systems. OPM’s workforce method starts with mission requirements and examines gaps in skills and capacity; staffing totals alone cannot show who is available for additional work. Acquisition planning considers requirements, alternatives, risk, funding and delivery schedules. HHS’s multi-program advance-planning process provides a particularly relevant model for documenting shared system costs and the contributions of different programs. These methods should inform the workbook without being treated as identical requirements for every policy. [L6–L9]

Public budget documents, spending data, workforce datasets and program inventories can support the initial research. Internal staffing assignments, contract performance information, detailed system capacity and proposed repurposing will often require further verification. Each resource relationship should therefore distinguish a documented observation, a proposed reuse, a restriction and an unresolved question. Neither an unobligated balance nor a listed employee establishes spare capacity for Project 2050. Unpriced work remains unpriced.

**Development task:** Build the specification and a bounded CMS prototype using the existing implementation cost register. Start with CMS-IMP-02’s federal-state eligibility work and its links to shared federal systems, preserving the existing IDs. Test whether the workbook can reconstruct a comparable baseline, explain a shared-cost allocation and show a cost/funding bridge with unresolved items visible. A wider application should follow only if those records help a reviewer answer the actual questions; a searchable interface is a later option. The existing fiscal models would continue to calculate aggregate budget effects from supported, reviewed inputs.

### Cost structure and proportionality

Cost estimates need a corresponding structure. Benefits and transfers should be distinguished from federal administration, state and local implementation, capital, IT development and migration, and continuing operations and maintenance. Shared costs should have one accounting owner, with links to the policies that depend on them. Authority, budget authority, obligations and cash outlays answer different questions and should be recorded separately, as should federal budget effects and broader household or social benefits. GAO’s cost and schedule guidance provides methods for documenting assumptions and examining how risks and delays affect estimates. [R1–R3]

The method should remain proportionate to the proposal. A tax provision needs an account of behavioral responses and revenues; an election reform needs legal and institutional specificity; a construction program needs credible assumptions about building and operating the resulting assets. A common framework is useful insofar as it helps us ask the right questions about each mechanism.

### What the evidence establishes

The project should distinguish values and aspirations from observed findings, projections, conditional scenarios, proposals, estimates and model results. A source record needs enough information to explain the relevant baseline, date, population and limits of application. These distinctions matter because the same source can be authoritative for one purpose and insufficient for another. A statute establishes legal requirements, an audit diagnoses particular failures and a causal study identifies an effect under its study conditions. None automatically supplies the evidence required for every use of a proposal.

Readiness also has several dimensions. A policy may be well specified but poorly costed, or have a credible fiscal estimate while lacking an implementation plan. Assessing design, fiscal analysis, legal specification, delivery and external review separately will make those limits easier to see. A legislative specification can be useful to counsel while still requiring legal review, and an internal estimate can support comparison without becoming an official score.

Likewise, a calculation can be reproducible without its assumptions being sound. Numerical consistency, computational reproducibility and substantive validity require different checks. Agreement among AI assistants does not substitute for independent evidence or specialist review. Where data or methods are restricted, the project should explain what can be inspected and what remains beyond its ability to validate.

### Working within the adopted decisions

The governing decisions and standing design rules continue to constrain this work. These include D3’s distributional test, D4’s fiscal conditions, reciprocity, supply with demand, capacity first and payment continuity. Broader benefits or productivity gains should remain visible in the analysis without being converted into dynamic revenue that D4 does not permit us to count.

The decision on CAP-7S already makes the full surge available immediately on enactment as multi-year funding, targeting R1. Spending should then follow agency delivery needs. Milestones can guide implementation without postponing the funding’s legal availability. FY2029 cash outlays, overlap and fiscal validation of the accelerated spending path remain unresolved; the old cash profile does not establish that the new path meets D4.

## 7. From preparation to a maintained body of work

The project needs to distinguish its own development schedule from the governing sequence it describes. Preparation before 2029 concerns what must be ready for legislative and transition decisions. The 2029–30 program concerns enactment, early benefits and the beginning of delivery. Analysis of 2031–40 needs to address scaling, permanent operations, maintenance, the expressly deferred 2033 democracy package and fiscal maturity. The long-range plan’s broader 2029–32 horizon describes early implementation; it does not extend D1’s 2029–30 window for first-trifecta regular-order bills or create a general 2033 queue. The longer view to 2050 should examine outcomes, remaining harms and the ability of institutions to adjust.

D1’s political scenario remains the adopted planning framework. Its assumptions are stipulations, rather than forecasts or evidence that the reforms will secure subsequent electoral victories. Alongside that framework, the project should examine a small number of coherent alternatives: coordinated implementation, constrained or interrupted delivery, and faster technological change with uneven gains. More adverse climate conditions can provide an additional stress test where relevant. These are analytical scenarios for investigation, without assigned probabilities.

Separating external conditions from policy choices and implementation will help identify what remains useful when an assumption fails. AI adoption, labor demand, hazards, interest rates, state cooperation and reversals may matter in different ways to different proposals. The purpose of considering alternatives is to improve the strategy and identify options for adjustment.

Preparation should work backward from actual decision points. The bibliography’s proposed calendar places some drafting, legislative route review and integrated modeling during or after early enactment. Those dates cannot serve as the readiness schedule for R1. Initial specifications and financing analysis need to exist before enactment, even though subsequent work will refine them. This preparation principle retains D5’s governing schedule.

### Carrying the program through 2050

The long view should help us ask whether the changes described in the public work could reach people, last and improve as circumstances change. It should also reveal needs and possibilities the present agenda has missed. The repository’s long-range plan contributes useful questions about sustainability, later policy choices and institutional continuity. Its proposed benchmarks and tool-building sequence should be assessed through the charter’s agenda review, substantive research and public experimentation. They do not establish the research priorities by themselves.

Begin with a small outcomes record alongside the agenda assessment and pilot dossiers. Connect the project’s commitments to dated baselines, meaningful measures, affected populations and the mechanisms through which policy might make a difference. Child poverty, dependable care, practical freedom, connection and public influence should remain visible alongside fiscal, climate and administrative measures. Quantitative indicators will answer some questions; accounts of people’s experience, participation and access to remedies will be needed for others. Record aspirations, candidate targets, adopted commitments, observed conditions and conditional model results separately. A favorable trend does not establish that a policy caused it, and several good indicators do not establish that every community shares the gains.

Use this record to develop pathways between early changes and a possible 2050. For a family receiving a new benefit, for example, examine what happens as children grow older, care needs change and housing or work becomes more expensive. Identify intermediate conditions, delivery requirements, remaining harms and choices that could change the result. Public scenes and prototypes can help expose these questions while research tests the mechanisms and limits. Some pathways will justify further modeling; others will first need better evidence, institutional analysis or a clearer account of what people value. A comprehensive scorecard is not required before this work can begin.

The repository already has substantial analytical machinery: a policy ledger, household incidence and long-term fiscal models, transportation and carbon models, dedicated-fund analysis, floor-time and vote simulations, and a common rebuild process. Some calculations already use outputs from other models. This is a foundation to examine and improve, with different levels of coverage and validity. Fiscal paths extend to 2056 and the carbon model to 2050, while the household model’s annual profiles cover 2030–39; Social Security and summary-level Medicare Hospital Insurance analysis use a 75-year horizon. Those differences matter when presenting a household’s future or comparing long-run results. Appendix D records the current capabilities and outstanding work checked for this revision.

Choose upgrades according to the claim or decision they would improve. Common package definitions, stronger distributional evidence and legislative-vehicle checks remain useful roadmap items. The initial assessment should determine their priority alongside substantive research and the unresolved CMS costing work. CAP-7S’s FY2029 cash path and reconciliation eligibility still require analysis, and the fiscal results retain the old spending proxy. Consistency flags need investigation; matching numbers and a successful rebuild cannot establish that assumptions or legal routes are sound. A material limitation should constrain the claims that depend on it, while unrelated research and appropriately qualified public work continue.

Examine long-run interactions without assuming their direction. Aging, health costs and changes in work may affect care, staffing, revenues and household security together. Lower emissions reduce a fee’s taxable base, but the fee path and other assumptions also affect receipts and dividends. The current carbon model reports receipts rising after 2039 because the fee rises faster than emissions fall; that conditional result needs comparison with other pathways, including faster decarbonization. AI’s implications for earnings, ownership and the revenue base likewise require evidence and alternatives. Develop focused revenue, care, social-insurance and climate analyses where these interactions could change a choice, including options beyond the current package when its instruments appear insufficient.

Further model integration should answer a defined question that existing tools cannot adequately address. Start by checking policy scope, dates, units, baselines and the relationships between outputs; a shared runner does not make independently specified assumptions coherent. A limited connection between two models may be sufficient. A larger scenario engine, macroeconomic module, durability simulation or dashboard should follow a demonstrated need and an assessment of evidence, validation and maintenance costs. Additional simulation can explore uncertainty where assumptions are supportable; it cannot assign credible probabilities to every political or technological future. Extending fiscal analysis to 2075 remains an option to justify, distinct from the 75-year fund tests. Economic feedback may inform scenarios without becoming dynamic revenue counted toward D4.

Durability should mean maintaining useful capabilities and protections while retaining the ability to correct error. Major dossiers should examine relevant changes in political control, legal interpretation, funding and state cooperation, together with maintenance, workforce continuity and access to remedies. Ask which rights and recurring services should remain dependable, which designs permit revision when evidence changes, and whether the same rules would be acceptable under a government we opposed. Independent scrutiny and meaningful public influence are part of that assessment. Keeping a program in place is insufficient evidence of success if it continues to cause harm or prevents legitimate democratic change.

The immediate outputs should therefore be a small outcomes record, long-run questions within the pilot dossiers and a prioritized account of evidence and tool gaps. These should inform the structured governing-decision review and the development of public and professional products. Maintain one working queue for source updates, research, model improvements and decisions, including the post-election D1 re-baseline and relevant fiscal and Trustees updates. Revisit priorities when findings or circumstances change. New instruments, numerical targets, formal governing tests and revisions to D1–D9 remain separate decisions for Espen.

### Reviewing the governing decisions

The project should include a structured review of D1–D9 and the standing design rules once the initial agenda-wide assessment, contrasting pilot dossiers and first checks of shared resources and costs provide a useful body of findings. That is an opportunity to assess the governing framework before investing heavily in more detailed specifications. Public prototypes and research can continue alongside the review; a consequential contradiction or newly identified harm should be raised as soon as it becomes apparent.

The review should ask how well the adopted designs serve the goals they were intended to advance, and whether their interactions support the project’s wider commitments. The outcomes record, sustainability analysis and durability reviews should inform that assessment as they become available. It should identify strengths worth preserving as well as weaknesses, including where new evidence strengthens an earlier judgment. A decision’s purpose, its chosen instrument and the assumptions on which it depends should be examined separately. The assessment should distinguish a weakness in the design from a limitation in our evidence or model, and make any tension among goals explicit.

For each decision or design rule, prepare a concise record of its current wording and source version; intended purpose; original basis and material assumptions; relevant new findings; strengths, weaknesses and confidence; alignment with project goals; and effects across other decisions and policies. Where reconsideration is warranted, set out feasible alternatives and their implications for outcomes, distribution, resources, legal and delivery requirements, sequencing and uncertainty. Recommend whether to retain the decision, revise it, reopen a specified question or defer judgment pending named evidence. Include the consequences of leaving it unchanged.

The output should be a review matrix and a short set of decision briefs for Espen, concentrating discussion on choices that could materially change the program. Repeat the review before a major public or professional release, and undertake a focused reassessment when a material finding or change in the planning scenario undermines an important assumption. These reviews should use dated evidence and explain what changed since the previous assessment.

Existing decisions remain authoritative while they are being reviewed. Espen’s approval is required for any revision, followed by incorporation into the GitHub governing record and corresponding domain and model updates. Approval of this review process does not approve any particular change to D1–D9, the design rules or the project’s goals.

### Targeted research additions

The bibliography also needs targeted additions. Its methods for oversight and implementation are valuable, but its nine domains do not cover all of the project’s policy areas equally. Priorities include child-poverty mechanisms and benefit take-up; social-insurance solvency and care beyond Medicare; legal and constitutional pathways; stronger causal housing and labor evidence; and political economy, administrative burdens and distribution across communities. The National Academies’ *A Roadmap to Reducing Child Poverty* is one useful addition whose methods and findings need substantive review and updating before use in this project. [R8]

### Evaluation, correction and accountability

A specific research question should guide this work: **how can institutions make discovering and correcting error part of responsible governing, while preserving consequences for negligence, concealment and refusal to act?** We should investigate when evaluation creates professional or political risk, rather than assume that the incentive operates identically everywhere. The review should distinguish elected officials, career administrators and other actors, and examine how policy type, potential harm and political context affect their incentives.

The aim is to explore a general expectation that policies will be evaluated and adjusted, with room to acknowledge reasonable decisions made under imperfect information. Accountability should consider the evidence and alternatives available when a decision was made, as well as what officials did when new information became available. The analysis should examine whether this distinction can encourage disclosure and correction without excusing avoidable harm or making accountability depend entirely on officials’ own descriptions of their intentions.

Candidate designs include routine evaluation requirements established in advance; independent evaluation funding and publication; records of assumptions and uncertainties; public duties to respond to findings; and authority and resources to revise implementation. These are options to investigate, not adopted instruments. The research should examine whether they improve learning and correction, how they might be manipulated, and when stronger safeguards are needed before a policy begins. It should also distinguish an inconclusive evaluation from credible evidence of harm and ask who decides when action is required.

The research should also examine adjustment rules and the treatment of pilots. A pilot can have a defined end and evaluation point without imposing a sunset on a permanent benefit. Any automatic adjustment needs an account of its trigger, distributional effects, legal authority, opportunities for review and protection against interrupting recurring payments.

The concrete output should be a comparative evidence review and an institutional-options memo, tested against the two pilot dossiers. It should identify arrangements that deserve further development, evidence limitations and decisions for Espen. Grace for policymakers must be considered alongside protection and remedies for affected people: correcting a policy does not by itself address the harm already experienced.

### Continuing the wider agenda

The ambition to approach the eradication of child poverty should continue to guide the work. A specific target and date require defined measures, modeled effects, attention to families who do not receive assistance and Espen’s approval. The current FAM-1 design should be assessed against that ambition rather than assumed to achieve it on its own.

Espen owns Project 2050 and approves its policy choices. This charter establishes the approved purpose and development approach, while the approved governing document remains authoritative. When research suggests a better instrument, the recommendation should explain its evidence, implications for the governing decisions and choices that require approval. Public and professional outputs should identify their source version and disclose material limitations, with specialist feedback and the experience of affected people informing claims of practical readiness.

Returning to the family at the beginning, the significance of the possible future is that they have more room to live, make plans and take part in the world around them. The governing program must explain how that change could reach them, how it could last and who might still be left out. Project 2050 should connect these questions closely enough that its vision gives direction to the analysis, and the analysis makes the vision more credible and more useful.

### Design rules

**Separate observations from interpretations.** Store the original source amount or capacity measure, its scope and date. Keep the analyst’s mapping, normalization, reuse proposal and judgment in linked records. Allow several resources to support one package and one resource to support several packages. A common name is insufficient evidence for a match.

**Construct two explicit baselines.** The financial baseline describes comparable existing spending and funding under the chosen assumptions. The delivery baseline describes services, workload and capability. Neither should silently assume that today’s resources are sufficient or that a future budget request will be enacted. Where continuity to FY2029 is uncertain, show an assumption or alternative, not a claimed future fact.

**Reconcile costs before proposing new funding.** An estimate should explain the change from the without-proposal case to the with-proposal case, including transition and continuing costs. A separate funding bridge should identify what is already included in another score, what existing funding might lawfully cover, what is unsupported and what additional authority or funding is proposed. Attributed overhead is not automatically an additional appropriation; a potential resource is not automatically a valid offset.

**Treat an allocation as a documented method.** Record the cost being allocated, beneficiaries, driver, period, numerator/denominator where relevant, rationale, evidence and reviewer. Direct attribution should be used where supportable. A provisional allocation can be explored with sensitivity analysis without being presented as an approved charging method. A shared cost has one entry in an additive total even when several policies depend on it.

**Use a repeatable decision process.** Define the service and workload; map the comparable baseline; identify gaps; compare implementation alternatives; estimate lifecycle costs and schedules; reconcile funding and overlap; review material judgments; and update the record as evidence changes. Link intended outcomes, evaluation questions, review dates and responses to findings to the separate evaluation workstream. For the prototype these are analytical review stages, not new powers to postpone statutory benefits or withhold immediately available surge funding.

### A bounded CMS test

Use the existing register’s CMS-IMP-02 as the first delivery chain: federal-state eligibility changes, with links to CMS-IMP-13 for shared federal systems and other packages only where their scope requires it. Preserve the register and IDs as the starting work definition rather than create a competing set. This defines the initial prototype scope; it is not a completed costing exercise.

An analyst should be able to select that package and see its required work, affected programs and partners; relevant budget and system evidence; the difference between a federal administrative cost and a federal contribution to state costs; the proposed allocation of a shared component; uncertainty about approvals and available resources; and the resulting additional-cost and funding questions. State administrative spending and federal matching support need separate perspectives so the same expenditure is not counted twice in a combined display.

Start with a small selection of published CMS baseline exhibits and one state’s accessible planning evidence, selected for availability and analytical usefulness rather than national representativeness. Where detailed APD, contract or capacity evidence cannot be obtained, the pilot should demonstrate how the gap is recorded and what would resolve it. It should not invent a completed funding bridge.

The pilot should pass the following checks before expansion:

- A reviewer can reconstruct every imported observation, including its units, source location, fiscal period and request/enacted/actual/estimate label.
- Additive totals exclude identified non-adds, repeated snapshots and parent/child amounts. Cross-source differences are explained or retained as unresolved.
- Headcount, FTE, contract value and workload remain distinct; no generic conversion turns a contractor dollar into a federal position or spare capacity.
- A shared cost has one accounting owner and an explicit allocation basis. Unknown costs or coverage never become zero by default.
- Reuse is marked as proposed until its scope, capacity, restrictions and approvals are supported. Contract ceilings and unobligated balances do not automatically become offsets.
- FY2029 obligations and outlays are separate from legal availability and from later-window displays. The approved CAP-7S availability decision is preserved.
- An export to the existing fiscal-input workflow contains supported changes only, with reconciliation and review status. Existing generated reports are still regenerated through project scripts.

| **Build an integrated scenario engine** | Potentially useful, but the repo already shares data and rebuilds models together; further integration could propagate weak assumptions as readily as improve analysis | Specify the unanswered question, reconcile relevant definitions and test a limited connection before committing to a larger engine |
| **Extend fiscal analysis to 2075** | A possible extension, rather than a self-justifying milestone or a substitute for fund solvency analysis | Explain which decision needs the added horizon and what supports the extrapolation; retain existing governing tests unless separately revised |
| **Test durability** | Survival under adverse control matters, but program persistence alone is a poor proxy for public benefit or democratic legitimacy | Review rights, continuity, maintenance, reciprocity, evaluation, correction and legitimate revision; use qualitative stress cases before considering a numerical test |
| **Create a dashboard and recurring reviews** | Useful views and practices, but they should not duplicate evidence records, project queues or the governing-decision review | Reuse maintained records; add an interface when an audience and use justify it; keep review triggers and decisions in the shared workflow |

### Current analytical foundation and its limits

The [tools readme](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/blob/e15c5c4a711b4cd2b482bac8300de45f2e08721f/index/tools/readme.md), [rebuild script](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/blob/e15c5c4a711b4cd2b482bac8300de45f2e08721f/index/tools/run-all.sh) and relevant scripts support the distinctions below. “Present” means implemented in the reviewed files, not independently established as sufficient for every charter claim.

| Component | Present in the reviewed repo | Limit or next question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ledger, rebuild and dashboard data** | A shared run builds the ledger and several models; incidence amounts use ledger formulas, and dedicated-fund analysis reads ledger, fiscal and transportation outputs | Scenario lists remain separately specified. Shared execution and accounting links do not provide a common behavioral model. Dashboard exports do not automatically update the separate public interface. |
| **Household incidence** | Quintile analysis with the top 1% separated, estimated distribution keys and annual phase-in profiles for 2030–39 | Not a household microsimulation or a model of wellbeing through 2050. Stronger distributional evidence, within-group examples and later horizons require additional work. |
| **Fiscal paths** | Paths through 2056, annual D4 checks, debt and interest calculations and selected sensitivities | Long-run profiles are calibrated to ledger totals and use specified growth assumptions. They are not independently scored annual policy paths; the accelerated CAP-7S cash path is not validated. |
| **Dedicated funds** | Social Security analysis over 75 years, summary-level Medicare Hospital Insurance analysis, and separate leave, transportation, carbon and held education channels | Coverage and methods differ by fund. Joint Social Security interactions and more detailed Hospital Insurance inputs remain research needs; the existence of the tool does not establish solvency. |
| **Carbon and transportation** | A structural carbon model through 2050, model-specific uncertainty bands, fee/dividend calculations, and a transportation model feeding other outputs | Carbon outputs and ledger estimates are compared rather than automatically substituted. Units, policy scope, revenue conventions and baselines must be reconciled before connecting new calculations. |
| **Votes and floor time** | Simulations of the 2029–30 package, holdout behavior, trimming and available floor time | These are conditional on estimated behavior and the current roster. Trimming does not establish Byrd compliance, and the vote model does not certify CAP-7S eligibility. |
| **Surge-funding checks** | A dedicated module preserves immediate legal availability while keeping FY2029 cash unpriced and acceleration validation incomplete | The module guards against unsupported conclusions; it does not supply the missing delivery costs, cash path or legal analysis. |
| **Consistency review** | A generated report flags numerical, identifier and cross-file discrepancies | Flags require substantive interpretation. A clean headline comparison cannot establish causal validity, realistic delivery or legal readiness. |

The [tools roadmap](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/blob/e15c5c4a711b4cd2b482bac8300de45f2e08721f/index/tools/roadmap.md) still identifies common package definitions, correlated D3/D4 uncertainty analysis, incidence upgrades, a reconciliation-vehicle checker and fiscal/fund improvements as queued work. The readme and rebuild script also include the surge-funding module, which the roadmap’s “in place” table omits. Read documentation alongside implementation rather than treating a single list as an exhaustive inventory.

One substantive correction concerns carbon revenue. The [carbon-model script](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/blob/e15c5c4a711b4cd2b482bac8300de45f2e08721f/index/tools/carbon-model.py) reports receipts continuing to rise after 2039 under its assumed fee path, despite falling emissions. That does not establish that revenue will rise in the world or in every policy scenario. It does establish that the charter should investigate the interaction rather than presume shrinking receipts. A change in total receipts also does not alone establish a change in the real benefit per recipient.

### Scope, sequencing and decisions

Begin the outcomes record and long-run inquiry with the agenda assessment and contrasting pilot dossiers. Record which claims existing tools can support, which need correction or extension, and which require evidence or institutional analysis before a model would be useful. A proposed tool addition should identify the decision or public use it serves, existing capability it would reuse, needed evidence, validation method and maintenance burden. Some questions will be better served by a sourced comparison, a qualitative scenario or specialist review.

The plan’s horizon labels do not supersede D1 or D5: first-trifecta regular-order bills remain in 2029–30, with the specifically deferred democracy package in 2033. Later delivery, maintenance and evaluation are distinct from a new legislative queue. Likewise, its candidate targets and instruments remain open choices. A new scorecard cannot silently adopt them.

The [committed consistency report](https://github.com/fm58cn6g4d-stack/project-2050/blob/e15c5c4a711b4cd2b482bac8300de45f2e08721f/index/check-report.md) continues to flag unpriced surge cash, pending eligibility and cross-file issues requiring investigation. The long-range plan’s broad description of a priced package does not remove those limitations. Material findings should inform the existing governing-decision review, with consequences for outcomes, distribution, resources, legal routes and delivery made explicit. Decisions about instruments, spending, numerical targets and formal governing tests remain Espen’s.
