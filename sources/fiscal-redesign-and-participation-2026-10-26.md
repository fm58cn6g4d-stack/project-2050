**Direction.** A decent life should be secure regardless of a person's ability to work or compete, or their family arrangement. Public responsibility also includes helping people participate in work, education, training and community life when they want to and can, with appropriate accommodations. These purposes reinforce each other. A payment can prevent destitution without providing a route out of isolation; a training referral can be useless without rent, care, transport or an actual job.

Espen affirmed this distinction in responding to the [strategic assessment](project-2050-strategic-assessment-2026-10-25.md), supported stronger institutional responsibility and remedies when delivery fails, asked for modeled climate-portfolio development, and directed a wholesale fiscal/financial review. This pass begins that review and develops the connections. It does not adopt the original assessment's three alternatives or its court/AI recommendations, a new tax, a benefit condition, a spending amount or a fiscal-rule change.

## A secure floor with practical routes into participation

The public promise should have two parts: **security people can rely on, and support to pursue a life they value**. Paid work often supplies income, identity, relationships and a sense of contribution. It is not the only source of those goods, and a poor or unsafe job can undermine them. Government should make meaningful participation possible without becoming the judge of whether someone's life has sufficient purpose. Caregiving, civic activity, learning, creative work and recovery matter too.

The likely UK reference is Alan Milburn's review of young people not in education, employment or training, usually abbreviated NEET. Its published interim report diagnoses fragmented institutions, health barriers and weakened entry routes into work. It emphasizes labor demand as well as individual preparation. This supports examining a connected system, not assuming more training alone will suffice. The October 21 parliamentary answer still described the final report as forthcoming that autumn. I have not treated press accounts of potential benefit cuts as final findings or as Espen's preference. The UK's population, institutions and benefit rules differ from those of this project. [1–2]

### An institution responsible for an offer that can actually be used

Develop a participation service through the public workforce system, with the Labor Department accountable for access and quality and local workforce agencies, colleges, unions, employers and disability-service partners delivering it. This is a proposed institutional home, not a claim that current American Job Centers have the authority, funding or capacity to deliver the whole offer. Health and education partners retain responsibility for their services. A named lead must resolve referrals rather than leave the participant to coordinate agencies.

The offer should combine voluntary guidance, supported employment, paid apprenticeships, education and work experience with practical help: transport, child or dependent care, assistive technology, accessible scheduling, benefits counseling and help retaining a job. People with fluctuating conditions need part-time routes, pauses and re-entry. Participation cannot automatically trigger a presumption that someone no longer needs disability or housing support. Earnings rules still require separate design, but people should know the consequences before trying work, with prompt reinstatement when a trial fails.

Existing LAB-8, LAB-9, AI-4 and education/health provisions provide a base. This proposal connects and strengthens them rather than assuming their existing budgets already cover every missing service. Keep the civic purpose of national service, but compare an accessible local track for people whose disability, caring responsibilities or housing situation makes cross-regional placement impractical. The original mixing requirement has a real purpose; enforcing movement on every participant would narrow who can share it. Skilled permanent care work should be regular paid employment with appropriate qualifications, not indefinitely staffed by a cheaper rotating service cohort. [3]

Build employer demand into the offer. Sector partnerships should secure credible paid placements, supervision and advancement routes before buying large training cohorts. Public housing, care and grid procurement can support apprenticeships and entry jobs, with labor standards and safeguards against replacing existing workers. Employers should make reasonable accommodations and contribute supervision and training, rather than receive open-ended subsidies for hires they would have made anyway. A wage subsidy or public-service placement remains a comparator when suitable private jobs are scarce; it is not proof of an unlimited supply of useful, supervised jobs.

The evidence supports this approach with qualifications. MDRC's WorkAdvance follow-up found that sector-focused programs increased earnings at different stages, but only one of four providers improved the prespecified earnings outcomes in year ten. Training quality, sector demand and continuing support matter. A randomized trial of 541 unemployed veterans with PTSD found stronger sustained employment under Individual Placement and Support than under transitional work. That supports integrated, preference-based assistance in that population; it does not establish results for every disability or show that employment treats every illness. [4–5]

**Do not fund this by assuming benefit exits.** Some people will earn more and use less assistance; others will combine support and work, or need continuing services without employment. Any fiscal effects require evidence and appropriate scoring. Employment support has a cost and a human purpose even when it produces no net Treasury saving. Measure sustained good work, learning progress, accessibility, income stability and participants' own goals—not simply departures from a benefit roll. Preserve the basic floor if a service is declined, inappropriate or unavailable.

### Delivery failure must create an obligation for the institution

A guarantee needs an accountable payer, a delivery organization and a usable remedy. These functions can be separate; accountability cannot disappear between them. For each major benefit, specify what happens when an eligible person cannot obtain the promised service. A bounded comparison should test these three cases:

| Failure | Proposed response to evaluate | What must be funded or resolved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A voucher holder cannot find a suitable accessible home | Extend search support and payment validity; provide a project-based or other housing route; offer temporary protection against homelessness | Local supply and actual accommodation cost; a voucher payment alone cannot manufacture a home |
| A participant receives repeated training referrals but no usable opportunity | Named case owner, accessible review and transfer to another provider or supported-employment route; preserve income assistance | Qualified staff, placements and supervision; avoid punishing providers for serving people with greater needs |
| Authorized care hours go unfilled | Rapid provider substitution, recipient-directed options where appropriate and emergency support; independent escalation | Worker pay, workforce capacity and safeguarding; do not presume relatives can fill the gap |

These are design proposals, not certified deadlines or new remedies already in law. Emergency continuity, accessible human review and independent appeal need dedicated operating resources within the responsible program. Legal aid and collective representation can complement them. A dashboard, another referral or a right to sue is not a complete remedy for tonight's housing or care need. Aggregate reporting should identify repeated institutional failures without exposing sensitive participant records.

## The fiscal redesign: change the structure, not just the last pay-for

The existing plan's underlying choices deserve consideration: progressive financing; permanent benefits paid for at maturity; a limited capital envelope; and separate social-insurance accounts. These remain persuasive. The weak point is the accumulation of overlapping, differently dated revenue estimates until the arithmetic narrowly balances. Several instruments also have aims beyond revenue—constraining excessive pay, taxing accrued gains or changing behavior. Their policy merits and their reliability as financing should be judged separately. [6–8]

The first comparison should cover the entire financing structure, including which adopted instruments to replace. I recommend three designs:

| Design | What it retains or replaces | Strongest case | Principal cost or risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Repair the existing progressive architecture** | Retain the current high-income/corporate package, exclusion cap and surtax; jointly rescore overlapping bases; obtain additional durable receipts | Least disruption; concentrated gains finance the agenda | Continued complexity and dependence on correlated top-end receipts and health savings; additional corporate rates can burden investment and workers |
| **B. Progressive core plus a broad income contribution** | Keep the high-income/corporate core; compare replacing C19b's health-exclusion cap and C20's surtax with one contribution across a broader income base, after a protected allowance and refundable credit | More intelligible financing; less dependence on health-plan cost as a proxy for ability to pay; avoids a wages-only base | Workers and some retirees pay; overlaps existing income, Medicare and Social Security taxes; protections can absorb much of gross yield |
| **C. Progressive core plus consumption financing** | The same two-row replacement, financed through a broad consumption tax with advance refundable household protection | A distinct base that does not depend solely on labor earnings; can support a larger service state | Price transition, administration, non-filer access and burdens on existing savings; regressive without effective compensation |

**B is my preferred design to develop, with A as a serious fallback and C as the large-scale comparator.** A contribution collected through withholding and annual reconciliation could cover wages, self-employment income and realized capital income, with pension treatment and an individual allowance compared explicitly. Retain retirement protections and avoid making family formation a condition of receiving the credit. Do not simultaneously count a dollar assigned to Social Security, HI or the general fund. This is a mechanism for development, not a selected rate or an instruction to extend current taxes to every dollar of retirement income.

C19b was selected as the milder alternative to a median-premium cap, paired with expected premium savings from health price reform. That rationale is real: the exclusion subsidizes costly insurance and favors higher tax brackets. But expensive plans also reflect age, location and health risk. The promise of lower premiums may arrive unevenly, while the tax applies to workers. Compare a carefully risk-adjusted cap against replacing it; do not assume either that all expensive plans are waste or that preserving the exclusion is distributionally neutral. C20 is simpler, but a new broad contribution should replace or integrate it rather than add another overlapping income surtax. Their removal costs **$1.33 trillion in existing 2030–39 planning receipts** before funding anything new. [6,8]

The corporate and wealth-income core needs joint reconstruction, including G1/TS1, G3/G9/C18/C39, G6/G11, and the investment-income taxes assigned to different funds. Separate G11's transition receipts from its ongoing yield and score its realization-based fallback. Retain enforcement investment even when its full receipts are not credited to a legislative vehicle, but provide financing that does not depend on the most optimistic enforcement estimate. A successful tax on a harmful practice may shrink its own base; that does not make it bad policy, but it makes it a poor sole foundation for permanent benefits.

### What the first calculations establish

The new tool solves for a recurring **net revenue requirement**, rather than naming an arbitrary tax rate and declaring success. It uses the existing fiscal paths through 2056. Receipt flows phase in over 2030–34. An illustrative margin of 0.1% of GDP is added to the largest maturity gap; this is a reviewer sensitivity, not a new D4 rule. [9]

| Scope, with the original joint low cases | Retain current taxes: new net receipts at maturity | Replace C19b/C20: new net receipts at maturity |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Adopted agenda only | 0.384% GDP | 0.726% GDP |
| Plus the review's two-million-award housing tranche | 0.473% GDP | 0.815% GDP |
| Plus housing and an unallocated service allowance of 0.1% GDP | 0.573% GDP | 0.915% GDP |

The last row corresponds to about **$310 billion or $496 billion in FY2040**, respectively, in nominal dollars. The larger number replaces existing taxes as well as raising additional money; it is not the net expansion of the total tax burden. The service allowance is a way to expose scale, not a cost estimate for participation or adaptation. Neither column includes a complete care entitlement, new housing construction capital or the new grid package. Actual program costing must replace the allowance before a package can be recommended.

The deeper sensitivity moves C38 and G9 to their existing low estimates and halves G11's projected receipts from 2040 to expose the risk of extrapolating a transition-heavy score. It leaves the existing overlap haircut unchanged. Under that case, housing plus the service allowance requires **0.775% GDP** if existing taxes remain, or **1.117% GDP** if the two taxes are replaced, including the same margin. These cases have no assigned probabilities and do not jointly estimate tax behavior. The outyear G11 haircut is deliberately an assumption, not a finding that half its revenue will disappear.

These magnitudes make a payroll-only solution less attractive as the default. The existing menu's roughly $1.6T per additional uncapped payroll point is only a dated planning anchor. It excludes the new compensation needed to protect households and does not resolve stacking with leave, Medicare and Social Security. Similarly, the menu's five-percent narrow-base VAT illustration raises $2.8T **before** a rebate; the replacement-plus-housing/service case calls for $3.36T of **net** new receipts in the same window under the joint low cases. The old VAT illustration therefore cannot simply be pasted in as a sufficient pay-for. A common-baseline score could change both numbers. [6,9]

Financing is also a promise about who bears risk. Under B, lower-income households need advance refundable protection and accessible non-filer receipt; families with no children must be included. Under C, compensation should arrive with or before the price change and account for pensioners and disability-related consumption. Employer remittance does not eliminate worker incidence. D3 still requires the bottom four quintiles to gain and more than half of new general-fund revenue to come from the top 1% and corporations. Neither unidentified contribution has passed that test. Test annual household exposure, not just an average that counts an unavailable service as a cash gain.

### The other accounts cannot be left outside the redesign

General-fund sufficiency does not complete Social Security solvency, finance the leave high-take-up case, or justify taking HI reserves. Develop one coordinated contribution schedule across these systems while retaining their distinct accounts and obligations. The current SS baseline and the SS-5 option both leave gaps in the dedicated-funds model. The leave contribution cap can produce permanently growing Treasury loans. Neither should be treated as an invisible borrowing permission for the general fund. [7]

Classify service staff, training support, care wages, appeals and maintenance as continuing costs where they recur. Public assets may justify time-limited capital borrowing within D4, but loans, grants, equity and procurement guarantees have different risks. Federal ownership does not turn construction into a cheap loan-subsidy line. Carbon receipts already finance dividends and transition purposes; faster decarbonization is not a reason to promise another permanent service from the same receipts. No assumed employment gain, prevention saving, public-equity return or defense reduction finances this pass.

The new calculations carry general-fund debt and incremental interest through 2056 and preserve the existing unified debt comparisons. They demonstrate the consequences of the assumed financing paths; they do not validate the inherited Social Security or other channel paths. Maintaining the full existing borrowing envelope is also not a finding that all its remaining room should be spent. Full D4 review still needs supported cash profiles and capital classification. An economy-wide debt-stabilization layer should remain visible as a distinct choice, as the original decision brief intended, rather than silently consuming the money assigned to this agenda. [7–9]

## Climate: use the model to compare portfolios, then challenge the result

The first new screen compares the selected package with grid delivery, the model's nuclear/hydro/fusion option, both together, removal of the modeled credit bundle, fee repeal and weaker or more expensive grid delivery. It uses identical inherited physical assumptions except for the named changes. There is no new national engineering calibration. [9–10]

The grid option raises modeled 2035 clean electricity from **75.8% to 81.7%** and avoids an additional **1.62 Gt of energy CO2 over 2030–50**. Its net addition to the model's spending accumulator is only **$38B in constant 2025 dollars**, despite an assumed $120B direct grid program: other modeled subsidy outlays change. That offset is a model result, not an available budget saving. Doubling assumed direct grid costs raises the net increment to $158B without changing emissions, mechanically. With weaker delivery, clean power reaches 79.0% and additional avoided emissions fall to 0.96 Gt. The output depends on creating actual capacity, not merely appropriating money.

The nuclear bundle adds about 0.44 Gt of avoided energy CO2 at $131B additional modeled spending. Adding it alongside the grid produces 1.84 Gt in total—less than the sum of their separate effects. These comparisons favor grid development first in this model, but they omit reliability and other system values and do not establish that all nuclear investment is inferior. Ratios in the output use undiscounted modeled spending, not full social costs or a comparable engineering cost estimate.

The more instructive result for redesign is that **grid delivery does not make the credit bundle dispensable**. Turning the modeled credits off and adding grid reaches 80.1% clean power in 2035 but leaves cumulative energy emissions about 0.47 Gt higher than the selected package. Power share alone would conceal that loss. Credits cover transport, buildings and industry as well as electricity. With fee repeal, grid improves performance but still misses the 2035 power target. A resilient bundle needs complementary instruments.

The next comparison should therefore disaggregate the credits, retaining measures that remove household affordability barriers and testing the marginal value of supply-side credits once transmission constraints ease. Compare a limited redirection to public transmission, targeted industrial contracts and low-income retrofits. Assess cumulative emissions, reliability, household bills, delivery under political reversal and distribution alongside fiscal cost. Adaptation needs a separate loss-reduction and vulnerability assessment; tonnes avoided cannot rank cooling, smoke protection or safe relocation adequately. A single aggregate “best use of dollars” ratio would conceal the project's multiple purposes.

Workforce and delivery remedies belong inside these comparisons. Training electricians without experienced instructors or placements will not deliver the modeled grid pathway. A procurement contract should identify responsibility for delays, customer costs and community harms. Adaptation should protect renters and displaced people independently of the insured value of property. These costs must be in the proposed bundle before it competes against tax credits.

## What follows from this first pass

The lead assignment is now the **financing architecture comparison**, reflecting Espen's instruction rather than the original review's proposed housing-first sequence. Develop A and B to a common, reviewable base, keep C as the larger-scale comparator, and report which existing instruments each replaces. Price the participation/delivery floor and a bounded housing expansion together with the required administration; keep a full care guarantee separate until its caseload and workforce implications are credible. The climate screen supplies one connected allocation question, not an instruction to launch independent redesigns across every domain.

The next deliverable should contain a preferred financing structure and fallback; net receipts after household protection; joint tax-base interactions; annual distribution and dedicated-fund implications; and a limited climate reallocation with costs on a common basis. If the evidence cannot support a rate, return a yield range and unresolved base choices rather than a false point estimate. Keep purpose, actual service capacity and remedies alongside the budget. This pass establishes the direction and first numerical constraints; it does not claim the wholesale redesign is finished.
