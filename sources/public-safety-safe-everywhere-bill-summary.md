# Safe Everywhere Act: cost and section-by-section summary

The federal bill would authorize about $12.1 billion a year, or roughly $61 billion over five years. Every dollar figure below is my proposal, sized against current programs. Nothing here is a CBO score.

## What it costs

| Title | Per year | Five years (FY2028 to FY2032) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I. Solving violent crime | $925 million | $4.6 billion |
| II. Police staffing, standards and accountability | $2.125 billion | $10.6 billion |
| III. Safety Compacts | $2.5 billion | $12.5 billion, plus $811 million one-time |
| IV. Safe Blocks | $400 million | $2.0 billion |
| V. Firearms | $600 million | $3.0 billion |
| VI. Public order and treatment | $1.8 billion | $9.0 billion |
| VII. Courts, victims and reentry | $1.9 billion | $9.5 billion |
| VIII. Protecting public servants | $500 million | $2.5 billion |
| IX. Fraud, cybercrime and identity | $1.2 billion | $6.0 billion |
| X. Data and evaluation | $150 million | $0.75 billion |
| **Total** | **$12.1 billion** | **$61.3 billion** |

**For scale.**

- The Justice Department's whole FY2026 budget is about $37 billion, [per the California State Association of Counties](https://www.counties.org/?p=10509).
- President Biden's 2022 Safer America Plan asked for about $37 billion, including nearly $13 billion over five years for 100,000 officers and $15 billion over ten years for prevention grants, [per the White House fact sheet](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2022/07/21/fact-sheet-president-bidens-safer-america-plan).
- Americans reported $20.9 billion in internet crime losses to the FBI in 2025 alone.
- $12.1 billion a year is about $36 per U.S. resident.

**What the total leaves out.**

- State and local spending, estimated separately in the next section.
- Costs to banks, carriers and platforms under Title IX, and to cities under the liability change in Title II.
- Any savings from fewer shootings, which are large per incident but not scored here.
- Revenue, covered under "How to pay for it" below.

These are authorization levels. Actual spending depends on annual appropriations, which often come in lower.

## State and local costs

States would add roughly $3.5 billion a year and local governments roughly $5 billion, for about $8.5 billion on top of the federal $12.1 billion. These are order-of-magnitude estimates built on the assumptions listed beside each line. Treat each total as accurate to within about a billion dollars either way.

| Courts, prosecutors, defenders | State-funded staff beyond the federal match, since most court systems are state-run | $0.8 billion |
| State clearance funds | State detectives embedded with prosecutors, on the Massachusetts and Missouri models | $0.4 billion |

| Shelter and order response | Shelter capacity and outreach needed before enforcement | $1.0 billion |

### All levels together

| Level | Per year |
| --- | --- |
| Federal | $12.1 billion |
| State | About $3.5 billion |
| Local | About $5.0 billion |
| **Total** | **About $20.6 billion** |

For scale, state and local governments spent nearly $135 billion on policing and $87 billion on corrections in 2021, [according to MoneyGeek's analysis of Census data](https://moneygeek.com/living/analysis/state-policing-corrections-spending). The added $8.5 billion is under 4% of that $222 billion.

**Where the estimate is weakest.**

- **Shelter and treatment.** Costs vary enormously by housing market, and these two lines could each be off by a factor of two.
- **Liability.** No good national data exist on what cities pay now, and nobody knows how many more suits would succeed.
- **Sustaining officers.** This line keeps growing after year five unless federal hiring grants are renewed.
- **Distribution.** Costs fall hardest on high-violence cities with weak tax bases, which is why the Compact grants carry a results bonus and a rural track.
- **Savings.** Fewer shootings reduce hospital, court and jail costs. None of that is netted out.

## How to pay for it

A package built mostly on charges tied to the harms being addressed could cover about $11 billion of the $12.1 billion federal cost. The largest piece is an alcohol tax change the CBO has scored before. The other estimates are mine, with the basis shown.

| Source | Per year | Basis | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tax all alcohol at $16 per proof gallon | About $7 billion | [CBO's 2016 estimate](https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/2016/52284) was $70 billion over ten years. A current score would differ. | Medium |
| Fraud-prevention assessment on large banks, payment apps, carriers, platforms and crypto exchanges | $1.2 billion | Set by design to cover Title IX, as financial regulators are funded by the firms they oversee. | Medium |
| End domestic military deployments | About $1.1 billion | [CBO put ongoing deployments at $93 million a month](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61943). Lasts only while deployments would otherwise continue. | Low as a lasting source |
| Dedicated receipts for the Crime Victims Fund | Up to $0.75 billion | Unobligated False Claims Act receipts, as in the House-passed stabilization bill. Recoveries were a record [$6.8 billion in FY2025](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/01/doj-announces-highest-ever-annual-false-claims-act-recoveries-over-6-8-billion-in-fiscal-year-2025) but under $3 billion the year before. | Low; volatile |
| Double the firearms and ammunition excise tax | About $0.6 billion | The tax raised [$801 million in FY2025](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-16_IF12173_5244ba924790fb67cf8f9d683db588d2d4be3c67.html). Assumes sales fall by a quarter. Existing revenue stays with wildlife conservation. | Low to medium |
| $10 fee per background check | About $0.3 billion | Assumes roughly 28 million checks a year, a figure I did not verify. | Low |
| Fees on crypto kiosks and data brokers | About $0.1 billion | Registration and per-transaction fees; rough guess. | Low |
| **Identified** | **About $11 billion** |  |  |
| General appropriations | About $1 billion | The remainder, or more if the weaker lines fall short. |  |

Without the deployment savings and the Crime Victims Fund receipts, which are the least durable lines, the package covers about $9.2 billion and the general-fund share rises to about $2.9 billion.

### With and without cannabis legalization

Adding a federal cannabis tax would close the remaining gap at a middle estimate, but the range is wide. Every other source stays as listed above, including the firearms excise and background-check fee.

| Scenario | Cannabis tax per year | All identified sources | General fund needed | Counting durable sources only |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Without legalization | None | $11.05 billion | $1.05 billion | $2.9 billion needed |
| With, low | $0.8 billion | $11.85 billion | $0.25 billion | $2.1 billion needed |
| With, middle | $2.0 billion | $13.05 billion | None; $0.95 billion over | $0.9 billion needed |
| With, high | $5.3 billion | $16.35 billion | None; $4.25 billion over | None; $2.4 billion over |

"Durable sources only" leaves out the deployment savings and the Crime Victims Fund receipts.

**Where the three estimates come from.**

- **Low** follows the CBO's most recent score of the MORE Act, about [$8.1 billion over ten years](https://www.nemannlawoffices.com/blog/marijuana-legalization-would-generate-millions.cfm) at that bill's modest rates.
- **Middle** is my assumption: a federal tax at rates comparable to the states', which now collect [$3.55 billion a year in cannabis excise taxes](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/09/cannabis-excise-tax.html) from legal markets covering part of the country.
- **High** is the [Tax Foundation's 2016 estimate](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/federal/marijuana-tax-legalization-federal-revenue/) for a 10% federal surtax on a mature national market.

**What a surplus could do.** In the middle and high cases, the extra revenue could lower the alcohol rate, cover part of the $8.5 billion state and local cost, or build the stabilization reserve faster.

**What changes besides revenue.**

- Legalization would free police, court and lab time now spent on possession cases, and expungement fits the reentry title.
- It would need its own rules on public use, impaired driving and potency, or it works against Title VI.
- A federal tax stacked on state taxes raises legal prices and helps the illicit market persist, which limits the rate.
- Earlier legalization bills sent their revenue to communities affected by drug enforcement. Redirecting it to this bill would be contested.
- Opponents would use it to recast a safety bill as a drug bill. One answer is to pass legalization separately and have this bill name cannabis receipts as an eligible source if it becomes law.

**Why these sources.**

- **Alcohol.** Alcohol is present in a large share of homicides and assaults, and the CCJ summit rated falling consumption a contributor to the homicide decline. The federal tax on beer and wine is far lower per unit of alcohol than on spirits.
- **Fraud assessment.** Nearly every scam passes through these firms, and they profit from the transaction volume. An assessment also rewards firms that cut fraud, if it is scaled to losses on their networks.
- **Firearms.** The tax already exists and funds conservation. Only the increase would go to violence reduction.
- **Deployments.** The Niskanen analysis found no effect on violent crime, so this is the rare offset that costs nothing in safety.

**The problems with each.**

- **Alcohol taxes are regressive** and hit moderate drinkers along with heavy ones. Brewers, vintners and restaurants would oppose it in every district.
- **Gun and background-check charges invite constitutional challenges** as burdens on a protected right, and would cost votes the rest of the bill needs.
- **Assessments get passed to customers,** and small banks and carriers will argue the burden is disproportionate.
- **Taxes meant to reduce a harm shrink their own base.** If drinking and gun sales keep falling, so does the revenue.
- **Budget rules complicate dedicating revenue.** Most of the bill is discretionary spending that Congress appropriates yearly. Guaranteeing that these receipts reach these programs would take a trust fund or mandatory funding, which is a bigger legislative lift.

**Other routes, by political starting point.**

- **From the right:** offset within the Justice Department's existing $37 billion budget or other domestic spending, with no new taxes.
- **From the left:** fund it from general revenue raised on high incomes or corporations, and drop the regressive excise taxes.
- **Neither:** leave it unoffset, as most public safety spending has been, and defend it as an investment that reduces costs elsewhere.

**State and local revenue.** The $8.5 billion in state and local costs has its own options: state alcohol and cannabis taxes, opioid settlement funds for the treatment lines, sports-betting revenue, and reinvested savings from lower jail populations. Very rough sizing follows.

| Source | Rough yield per year | How I got there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opioid settlement funds | About $1.0 billion | Settlements total roughly $56 billion over about 18 years, or about $3 billion a year, [per Shatterproof](https://www.shatterproof.org/node/40216). Assumes a third goes to the treatment lines, which fit the settlements' permitted uses. |
| Reinvested corrections savings | About $0.9 billion | Assumes a 1% saving on the $87 billion states and localities spend on corrections. Illustrative only. |
| State alcohol taxes | About $0.7 billion | Assumes a 10% rise on roughly $7 billion in state alcohol excise revenue. I did not verify that base. |
| Cannabis taxes | About $0.5 billion | States collected [$3.55 billion in cannabis excise taxes](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/09/cannabis-excise-tax.html) in the year to June 2026. Assumes 15% is dedicated. |
| Sports-betting taxes | About $0.5 billion | States collected [about $3.2 billion in FY2025](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/us-sports-betting-tax-revenue-2025-state-breakdown). Assumes 15% is dedicated. |
| **Identified** | **About $3.6 billion** |  |
| State and local general funds | About $4.9 billion | The rest of the $8.5 billion. |

Three cautions. Dedicating existing cannabis and betting revenue takes it from whatever it funds now, so those lines are reallocations, not new money. Opioid settlement payments shrink over time and are already committed in many states. And these sources are spread unevenly: states without legal cannabis or sports betting have fewer options.

## Section-by-section summary

### Title I. Solving violent crime

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 101. Clearance grants | Expands the VICTIM Act model: detectives, non-fatal shooting teams, analysts and victim liaisons. Sets national clearance goals of 80% for murder and 50% for non-fatal shootings by 2032. | $500 million |
| 102. Forensic capacity | Clears DNA and ballistics backlogs and funds regional labs. | $300 million |
| 103. Witness protection | Grants for witness relocation and protection in state and local cases. | $100 million |
| 104. Clearance reporting | Requires quarterly clearance data by agency, neighborhood and victim demographics as a condition of funding. | $25 million |

Baseline: the pending VICTIM Act authorizes $60 million a year.

### Title II. Police staffing, standards and accountability

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 201. Hiring | Expands COPS Hiring, with priority for investigators and violent-crime units. Recipients report low-level arrests alongside clearance. | $1.5 billion |
| 202. Civilian response | Funds crisis teams and civilian staff for calls that do not need an officer. | $400 million |
| 203. Standards | National decertification registry, public use-of-force and complaint data, body cameras. | $150 million |
| 204. Employer liability | Amends federal civil rights law so agencies are liable for officers' constitutional violations, and removes qualified immunity as a defense in those suits. | None |
| 205. Licensing | Grants for state licensing boards. Requires independent review after any finding or settlement, and a registry check before hiring. | $75 million |

Baseline: COPS Hiring received $253 million in FY2026. At an assumed federal share of $125,000 to $150,000 per position, $1.5 billion supports roughly 10,000 to 12,000 positions a year.

### Title III. Safety Compacts

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 301. Compact grants | Flexible multi-year grants to cities, counties and regions for a locally written violence plan: intervention, focused deterrence, youth programs, and related staff. | $2.5 billion |
| 302. Evaluation | Sets aside 3% of Section 301 for independent evaluation and technical help. Programs that fail lose funding. | Within 301 |
| 303. Results bonus | Reserves 10% of Section 301 for places that cut shootings, raise clearance and narrow disparities. | Within 301 |
| 304. Restoration | Restores the public safety grants cancelled in April 2025. | $811 million, one time |
| 305. Small and rural track | Simplified regional compacts and shared investigators. | Within 301 |

Baseline: the federal community violence intervention program received about $50 million in FY2026.

### Title IV. Safe Blocks

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 401. Place-based grants | Lighting, vacant-lot remediation and abandoned-building repair on high-violence blocks, with shootings nearby measured before and after. | $400 million |

### Title V. Firearms

| 504. Conversion devices and untraceable guns | Tightens penalties and import controls. | None |

| 602. Diversion | Treatment courts and citation-to-treatment programs for low-level offenses. | $200 million |
| 603. Organized retail theft | Regional task forces and data sharing. | $100 million |

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 701. Swift courts | Prosecutors, public defenders and judges to cut time from arrest to trial. | $600 million |

### Title VIII. Protecting public servants

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 801. Security grants | Matching grants for state threat-assessment units, home security and event security for state and local officials, judges and election workers. | $300 million |
| 802. Threat prosecution | A threats prosecutor in each U.S. Attorney's office and a single reporting channel. | $100 million |
| 803. Privacy | Shields home addresses of covered officials and bars data brokers from selling them. | None |
| 804. Early warning | Restores violence-prevention and election-security programs and funds independent tracking. | $100 million |

### Title IX. Fraud, cybercrime and identity

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 901. National strategy | An interagency anti-scam strategy and one reporting system with rapid fund freezes. | $150 million |
| 902. Fraud units | State and regional cyber-fraud investigators and prosecutors. | $400 million |
| 903. Industry duties | Requires banks, payment apps, carriers and platforms to detect and block scams, with shared liability when clear warning signs are missed. Funds enforcement. | $150 million |
| 904. AI impersonation | Creates a federal offense for fraud by cloned voice or likeness, sets customer-verification rules for cloning services, and funds detection tools. | $100 million |
| 905. Identity | Default credit freezes, optional secure digital identity verification, and a single restoration process for victims. | $300 million |

### Title X. Data and evaluation

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1001. Crime statistics | Monthly national crime data, with fraud and cybercrime added to headline figures. | $150 million |
| 1002. Scorecard | Publishes the six compact measures for every funded jurisdiction. | Within 1001 |

### Title XI. Integrity and durability

This title adds no new spending. It governs how the other titles' money is distributed, checked and sustained.

| Section | What it does | Authorized per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1101. Formula and floors | Distributes Title III by violence burden and local fiscal capacity, with a minimum for every state and a regional track for small jurisdictions. | None |
| 1102. Hiring step-down | Federal share of each Title II position declines over five years. Applicants file a retention plan before hiring. | None |
| 1103. Stabilization reserve | Holds 5% of Title III in reserve. Raises the federal share automatically when a state's revenue falls sharply or shootings spike. | Within Title III |
| 1104. Supplement, not supplant | Requires recipients to maintain their own public safety spending. | None |
| 1105. Data integrity | Random audits of crime classification and clearance, cross-checks against hospital and survey data, and loss of bonus eligibility for misreporting. | Within Title X |
| 1106. Workforce | Sets aside 5% of Titles I, II, VI and IX for training, credentials and loan repayment for investigators, analysts, outreach workers and clinicians. | Within those titles |
| 1107. Civil rights conditions | Makes disparity reporting and a gap-closing plan conditions of funding. Requires public use policies for surveillance technology. | None |
| 1108. Reinvestment | Returns a share of documented federal savings to the compact that produced them. | Indefinite |
| 1109. Authorization and review | Authorizes programs permanently, with a five-year review that conditions renewal on evaluation results. | None |

### Title XII. Revenue

| Section | What it does | Estimated receipts per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1201. Alcohol excise | Taxes all alcoholic beverages at $16 per proof gallon. | About $7 billion |
| 1202. Fraud-prevention assessment | Assesses large financial, telecom, platform and crypto firms, scaled to fraud losses on their networks. | $1.2 billion |
| 1203. Firearms excise | Doubles the firearms and ammunition excise rate and directs the increase to Titles IV and V. | About $0.6 billion |
| 1204. Background check fee | Sets a $10 fee per check. | About $0.3 billion |
| 1205. Kiosk and data-broker fees | Registration and transaction fees. | About $0.1 billion |
| 1206. Crime Victims Fund receipts | Deposits unobligated False Claims Act receipts in the fund. | Up to $0.75 billion |
| 1207. Domestic deployments | Bars funds for routine domestic law-enforcement deployments of the military, with exceptions for emergencies requested by a governor. | About $1.1 billion saved |
| 1208. Safe Communities Trust Fund | Holds receipts from Sections 1201 to 1205 and dedicates them to Titles I through IX. | None |

Optional Section 1209 would direct federal cannabis excise receipts to the trust fund if Congress legalizes and taxes cannabis in separate legislation. It adds between $0.8 billion and $5.3 billion a year depending on the estimate, and changes nothing if no such law passes.

## What a bill cannot do

Several platform commitments are outside federal legislation: party pledges on political violence, state arbitration and licensing law, local prosecution priorities, and city enforcement choices. Titles II, VII and VIII use grant conditions to encourage them.

## How to shrink it

A leaner version would keep Titles I, III, VIII and IX whole, since they have the strongest evidence or bipartisan sponsors, and fund Titles II, VI and VII at about a third. That comes to about $8 billion a year.
