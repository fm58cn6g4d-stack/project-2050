# Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act — Updated Analysis from the Full Bill Text

## Bottom line

The full text is bigger and sharper than the press coverage suggested. It gives clean energy the strongest statutory protection against executive hostility ever written, and it narrows environmental review and litigation more than any law since NEPA itself. My lame-duck enactment estimate falls slightly, to about 22% (range 15–30%), mainly because the signature step is riskier than I first assumed.

Three findings drive the update:

- Title IV is a liability regime, not a promise. Agencies that interfere with a permitted project must prove their case by clear and convincing evidence. Losing costs the Treasury 25–50% of the sponsor's sunk costs, or 50–100% of expected construction cost for a pattern of disparate treatment, paid from the Judgment Fund without appropriation.
- The litigation changes reach every federal permit, not just energy. NEPA and NHPA violations can no longer support an injunction or vacatur, only a remand while the project keeps building. Claims under NEPA, the CWA, the ESA and NHPA get 150 days.
- The transmission title is real but slow. Interregional plans are not due until two years after enactment, and the data-center cost rule takes roughly 31 months to finalize. Most physical effects land under the next president.

The headline judgment holds: on balance the bill helps clean energy more than fossil fuels, because transmission and executive risk are the binding constraints for clean power. But it also makes future fossil approvals nearly irreversible, which matters a great deal for any 2029 climate law.

## Division A (EPW): what the text does

Division A rewrites how every federal permit is reviewed and litigated, not only energy permits. Its effect on a highway, a transit line, a mine or a housing project is as large as its effect on a pipeline.

### Title I — NEPA (Secs. 1101–1133)

- Narrower trigger. "Major Federal action" now requires substantial federal control as determined by the agency itself. It excludes projects whose only federal tie is a loan or loan guarantee, and grants where the agency lacks "predominant control" over how money is used.
- Statutory exclusions. Excluded outright: lifeline-infrastructure repairs that add under 20% capacity (no new highway through-lanes), geothermal test wells, grid work in existing rights-of-way, and gas pipeline "looping, compression, capacity-enhancement" inside an existing corridor. Agencies may also skip review if a state or Tribal review, or another federal law, "serves a similar function."
- Hard clocks. One year for an EA and two years for an EIS, counted from the earlier of the level-of-review decision or a complete application. Every other agency must decide its permits within 90 days of the EIS (120 for non-participating agencies).
- Scope limits. Agencies need not consider research published after the notice of intent. Alternatives that fail the sponsor's own goals are dropped, and a sponsor can force an agency to skip an EA and go straight to an EIS.
- Enforcement. Sponsors can sue over missed deadlines and get a court order within 120 days. Senior career officials must spend half their workweek fixing a late review, and the agency head cannot travel more than 25 miles from the office.
- CEQ referee. The CEQ chair resolves interagency disputes within 44 days. That resolution is unreviewable and counts as full compliance with any consultation or concurrence the dispute concerned.
- Litigation. Claims go straight to the court of appeals within 150 days, only from substantive commenters alleging direct harm. The only remedy is remand without vacatur, with the agency given 180 days to fix the defect. A preliminary injunction requires a likely violation of some law other than NEPA or NHPA.
- Smaller items. Programmatic documents last 10 years instead of 5; agencies can borrow each other's categorical exclusions; a $20 million a year permitting fund is authorized; CEQ must build an "ePermit" system by Dec. 1, 2028.

Real-world example: had this law existed, the 2022–23 Mountain Valley Pipeline vacaturs would have been remands with construction continuing. A highway widening funded mainly by formula grants could plausibly skip NEPA entirely if the state controls design.

### Title II — Clean Water Act (Secs. 1201–1205)

- Section 401, all federal licenses. States get at most one year, set by the federal agency, and silence is a waiver. A denial or condition must be justified by clear and convincing evidence that nothing less would protect water quality, and conditions must be the "least burdensome."
- Section 401, gas pipelines and transmission. States get at most nine months, and certify only a "direct point source discharge." Most construction impacts on streams are dredge-and-fill, so this may leave states little to review.
- Section 404. Discharges into under 2 acres per single and complete project are deemed minimal. Nationwide Permits 12, 14, 43, 57 and 58 (utility lines, linear transportation, stormwater, electric lines, water lines) become mandatory, renewable without ESA or NHPA consultation, and last 10 years.
- EPA veto window. EPA's 404(c) veto works only between a complete application and permit issuance. That bars both Pebble-style preemptive vetoes and Spruce-style retroactive ones.
- Litigation. 150-day limit on 404 challenges; no vacatur unless an "imminent and substantial danger" exists. Challenges to the definition of "waters of the United States" move to the courts of appeals.
- Other. NPDES permit terms double to 10 years. EPA water quality criteria now require notice-and-comment rulemaking.

Real-world example: New York's 2016 401 denial of the Constitution Pipeline and its 2020 denial of Williams' Northeast Supply Enhancement would face a clear-and-convincing burden and a direct-discharge scope. The general rule also reaches FERC hydro relicensing, where Oregon, Washington and California have used 401 to require fish passage and flow conditions.

### Title III — Endangered Species Act (Secs. 1301–1308)

- Formal consultation shortens from 90 to 60 days, extendable to 100 instead of 150. Applicants can sue after 145 days, and consultation must finish when the NEPA document does.
- Projects covered by the MAP-21 operational right-of-way exclusion skip Section 7 consultation entirely.
- States can assume Section 7 consultation within their borders after Interior writes a rule (proposed within 270 days). States must waive sovereign immunity, face four years of annual audits and can be terminated after 120 days' notice. Applicants may still choose federal consultation.
- Biological opinions can be challenged only in the D.C. Circuit within 150 days. "Best available science" gets a statutory definition, and listing decisions must integrate qualifying state data.
- Authorizations: $150 million a year for consultation and state programs (FY2027–31), and $50 million a year for a coastal state fund.

### Title IV — Project certainty (Secs. 1401–1403)

- Sec. 1401, no revocation. For non-federal projects, agencies may not "revoke, rescind, withdraw, terminate, suspend, amend, or alter" any permit in effect on or after Sept. 16, 2026, or otherwise block construction or full operation. Exceptions: a court order, a material breach, fraud, the holder's request, or "specific, urgent, substantial, and proximate harm" based on new information.
- Burden and remedy. The agency must prove an exception by clear and convincing evidence in the district where the project sits. A winning sponsor gets reinstatement, delay costs, fees, and 25–50% of all project costs to date.
- Same-judge rule. If a stop-work order issued within a year before enactment was enjoined, any new interference goes back to the same judge. The Dec. 22, 2025 offshore wind orders stay inside that window only if the bill is signed by about Dec. 22, 2026.
- Sec. 1402, disparate treatment. Developers of 47 listed project types, from offshore wind to coal, LNG and CO₂ pipelines, can sue when a class of projects is improperly denied or delayed more than 60 days past deadline. The pattern must last 180 days (280 with a NEPA document) and be intentional.
- Proof and damages. Statistics make a prima facie case; if the government withholds its data, the court presumes the worst. Damages run 50–100% of expected construction cost, paid from the Judgment Fund, and the developer must build the project or repay.
- Sec. 1403, one-year clock. Permits that need no NEPA document must be decided within a year of a complete application. Courts must rule within 120 days and order a decision within 60.

Real-world example: a Revolution Wind–style stop-work order on a nearly finished $4 billion project could cost the Treasury $1–2 billion in mandatory damages. A 1 GW onshore wind farm held up by an intentional pattern of FAA or Defense Department delays could recover $0.8–2 billion.

## Division B (ENR): what the text does

Division B is mostly a Federal Power Act rewrite. It moves transmission siting from DOE to FERC, makes planning and interconnection reform statutory, and puts the data-center cost rule into law, with ERCOT carved out of the siting and planning pieces.

### Transmission siting and planning (Secs. 2101–2106)

- Backstop siting. FERC "shall issue" a permit for lines of 230 kV or more if it finds the line serves interstate commerce, the public interest, consumers and reliability. DOE's corridor designation step is gone, replaced by a DOE congestion study every three years.
- State clock. The one-year state clock now starts when the developer files with the state. For lines of 345 kV or more, the developer can file at FERC at the same time, but FERC cannot issue a permit until a state has failed to act or lacks authority.
- Automatic qualification. A line selected in an approved regional or interregional plan automatically satisfies the backstop findings. FERC becomes lead agency for federal permits, and state permits issued under federal law, including 401 certifications, are reviewed in the courts of appeals.
- Cost allocation. Backstop and planned lines are paid for by beneficiaries, "roughly commensurate" with six listed benefits; customers with trivial benefits pay nothing. Payments to host communities are recoverable in rates.
- Regional and interregional planning. FERC must issue a rule within 180 days; regions file processes within two years and update them every four. Existing Order 1920 compliance counts. The text requires common assumptions and benefits but no fixed planning horizon or scenario count, and there is no FERC fallback if regions never file.
- Consolidated planning (new FPA §318). Within 18 months FERC must require each region to plan generation and transmission together over 20 years, publish fuel-neutral "planned interconnection locations" with fixed zonal per-MW costs, and run single-decision cluster studies with real withdrawal penalties. This is the SPP-style reform, and it is the provision most likely to shrink the roughly 2,000 GW queue.
- Local transmission and ROFR. States can refer a utility's local planning to FERC, which can cut its return, remove the prudence presumption, or fine it up to $5 million per imprudent facility. Federal right of first refusal is banned for planned lines.
- Existing rights-of-way. Reconductoring, voltage upgrades, added circuits and storage inside existing corridors skip NEPA and §106 entirely.

### Data centers and markets (Secs. 2107–2115)

- FERC pricing. Within 270 days FERC must revise its 1994 transmission pricing policy so "computational load" pays both embedded and incremental costs, with a discount for non-firm service. A proposed rule follows within 270 more days and a final rule a year after that.
- No-subsidy floor (new FPA §228). For data centers of 20 MW or more that connect after enactment, no incremental generation, transmission or distribution cost may fall on other customers, even if the data center leaves early. Utilities must collect financial assurance before building. Existing approved arrangements are grandfathered.
- State tools. States may price data centers above cost and rebate the surplus, auction service through open seasons, require them to bring their own generation, require interruptible service, and treat them worse than factories.
- Distributed resources. Within 18 months, regional markets must admit aggregations of behind-the-meter resources of 100 kW or more, with double-compensation penalties. Distribution utilities must interconnect them, except public power unless state law requires it. DOE will run a voluntary rooftop-solar, battery and charger permitting program.
- Grid hardware. Utilities must deploy advanced conductors and grid-enhancing technologies wherever the benefit-cost ratio exceeds one, or face a return-on-equity penalty.
- Interconnection data. Transmission providers get statutory grid-data rights and must adopt automated and AI-assisted study tools.
- Other. EIA must report data-center load on Form 861, and federal power marketing administrations like BPA get pay flexibility to retain staff.

### Energy permitting, geothermal, hydro, offshore (Secs. 2201–2252)

- 150-day limit for all energy and mineral projects. Any federal-law challenge to an authorization for energy, CO₂ or mineral projects or wildfire-fuel work must be filed within 150 days, with remands capped at 180 days.
- Federal lands. BLM and the Forest Service must act on renewable rights-of-way in 30 days, or defer with a schedule, and create categorical exclusions for low-disturbance renewables, storage, and grid work in existing corridors within 180 days.
- Oil and gas. No federal drilling permit is needed when the well sits on non-federal land and the government owns under 50% of the unit's minerals. EPAct 2005 oil and gas exclusions become outright exemptions from NEPA.
- Mining. Mines can claim as many 5-acre mill sites as needed for waste rock, reversing the Ninth Circuit's 2022 Rosemont ruling. Claim and lease receipts feed a small Abandoned Hardrock Mine Fund.
- Tribal land. Tribes with approved regulations can grant their own rights-of-way.
- Geothermal. Annual lease sales; 30-day completeness and 30-day decision clocks for drilling permits; test wells excluded from NEPA; no federal drilling permit where the government owns under half the resource; lawsuits do not pause processing; royalty relief runs from each plant's in-service date.
- Hydro. Federal license conditions must be "reasonably related to the effects of the project." Routine maintenance and emergency variances need no FERC approval, and small hydrokinetic projects (5 MW or less) get 10–20 year licenses.
- Offshore. The OCSLA test becomes "unreasonable interference with other uses." Interior and DOE must identify preferred cable routes within two years, Interior leads offshore transmission permitting, and export cables may cross national marine sanctuaries.

### Title III — Historic preservation (Secs. 2301–2302)

- Narrower scope. "Undertaking" excludes projects whose only federal tie is financial assistance, unless the money is for preservation. "Adverse effect" excludes visual, atmospheric or audible effects unless they significantly diminish a property.
- Hard deadlines. Consultation runs 90 days, then 60 days of further talks, then 45 days for the Advisory Council's comments and a final agency call within 30 days. It must also finish when the NEPA document does.
- Agency has the last word. Tribes, SHPOs and THPOs keep a statutory seat. But the agency may decide "in its sole discretion" that other considerations outweigh preservation, and that ends its obligations.
- Litigation and funding. Challenges go to the courts of appeals; non-signatories cannot challenge programmatic agreements. The Historic Preservation Fund is authorized at $200 million a year through FY2036, with grants for Tribal records databases.

This is why NATHPO calls the Tribal language paternalistic. The seat at the table is real, but the agency can overrule everyone at the table.

The bill finishes a decade of incremental permitting reform and responds to two shocks: load growth from data centers, and a president willing to halt approved projects. It is sponsored by Capito (EPW chair), Lee (ENR chair), Whitehouse and Heinrich, the ranking members.

### The signature risk is underappreciated

Trump has already withheld his signature on a bipartisan permitting bill as leverage. In June 2026 he refused to sign the ROAD to Housing Act to pressure the Senate on the SAVE America Act. It became law only because the Constitution enacts an unsigned bill after 10 days, excluding Sundays (Argus).

That backstop does not work at the end of a Congress. If this bill reaches the president within 10 days of sine die adjournment and he does nothing, it is pocket-vetoed. Leaders should aim to present it by about Dec. 18–21, which also keeps the offshore wind "same-judge" window open.

### What would raise or lower the odds

- Up: a CBO score showing modest expected Judgment Fund costs; a White House statement on wind and solar "regular order"; attaching a trimmed transmission and data-center title to year-end appropriations.
- Down: a presidential post attacking wind; a CBO score treating Title IV damages as large contingent outlays; Tribal nations and states that use 401 on hydro and pipelines (Oregon, Washington, New York, California) mobilizing against Titles II and III.
- Wildcard: Democrats who win big on Nov. 3 may prefer to wait. The text shows why that is a poor bet: reconciliation can carry a carbon fee or tax credits, but not FERC jurisdiction, judicial review limits or Title IV, all of which need 60 votes.

## If enacted: implementation and effects

The bill's fossil and litigation provisions bite almost immediately, while its transmission and data-center provisions mostly mature after January 2029. That sequencing is the single most important fact for anyone planning a 2029 agenda.

The rules this administration writes arrive first; the plans and the data-center final rule, which decide real-world outcomes, land within months of the next inauguration.

### 2027–2028: the rest of the Trump administration

- Immediate winners. Pipeline looping and compression inside existing corridors skip NEPA and §106 on day one. Drilling on non-federal land with minority federal minerals needs no federal permit, and geothermal test wells and grid work in existing rights-of-way skip review. Every pending EIS starts a two-year clock, and every new lawsuit faces a 150-day limit and remand-only remedies.
- Renewables get a shield, not a tailwind. Sec. 1401 protects every permit in effect on Sept. 16, 2026, including Revolution, Sunrise, Empire, Vineyard and Coastal Virginia offshore wind. Sec. 1402 claims ripen about 180–280 days after enactment, so a blanket anti-wind policy would start accruing damages exposure in late 2027.
- Who writes the rules matters. A Republican-led FERC and a Trump CEQ write the first rules: NEPA procedures in 180 days, regional planning in 180 days, consolidated planning in 18 months, and the data-center pricing statement in 270 days. Expect fast, generous rules for large-load reporting and gas, and narrow, minimal rules for interregional cost allocation.
- First litigation wave. States will challenge FERC backstop preemption and the 401 limits as Tenth Amendment questions. Tribes will challenge the §106 "sole discretion" override and the bar on NEPA review of Tribal trust land decisions. Environmental groups will test the remand-only remedy against the ESA and the CWA.
- Little consumer relief yet. The §228 no-subsidy floor applies only to data centers that connect after enactment, and FERC's final pricing rule lands around mid-2029. PJM capacity prices for 2027–28 are already set.

### 2029–2032: a Democratic administration

- Transmission arrives. Regional and interregional processes are due about two years after enactment (late 2028), so a Democratic FERC approves the first plans in 2029. Any line selected in those plans automatically meets the backstop test, so 345 kV+ lines in PJM, MISO, SPP and the West can go to FERC if a state stalls. First energizations would come about 2032–34.
- Interconnection gets faster. The 20-year consolidated planning and fixed per-MW zonal pricing should take effect in most regions by 2029–30, cutting typical queue time from 5+ years toward 2–3.
- The certainty clause cuts both ways. A Democratic DOE could not repeat the January 2024 LNG export pause without risking damages of 50–100% of each terminal's construction cost. It could not revoke any fossil approval in effect after Sept. 16, 2026, as Biden did with Keystone XL. Agencies would need a court order, a breach, fraud, or clear-and-convincing evidence of new and specific harm.
- Regulatory climate policy collides with Title IV. Sec. 1401 bars any action that would "interfere with or prevent" a permitted project's "operation at full capacity." Plaintiffs will argue that new Clean Air Act limits forcing coal or gas plants to curtail are barred unless a later statute overrides it. A 2029 climate law must say so expressly.

### 2033–2040s: long run

- Grid growth. With statutory planning, beneficiary-pays cost allocation and FERC backstop authority, transmission could grow 2–2.5% a year, the pace REPEAT found necessary to capture most of the Inflation Reduction Act's potential.
- Lock-in. Pipelines, compressor expansions and LNG terminals approved in 2027–32 run into the 2060s–2080s, and Title IV makes them very hard to unwind by regulation.
- Durability. Remand-only NEPA and 150-day limits make project approvals durable across administrations of both parties. That favors whoever builds most in the next decade.

### If the bill fails

- Transmission. Order 1920 survives or falls in the Fourth Circuit, and backstop siting stays dead without DOE corridors. Interregional lines remain the exception.
- Data centers. Costs are fought utility by utility through state tariffs and FERC co-location dockets.
- Renewables. Projects stay exposed to stop-work orders until at least January 2029, defended only through APA litigation, which developers have won so far but slowly.
- Fossil infrastructure. Faces 401 and litigation risk, but also lacks protection against a future Democratic revocation.

## Beyond energy: bridges, transit, rail and airports

For transportation megaprojects the bill shortens federal review at the margins and makes approvals much harder to undo, but it leaves untouched the constraints that usually set the schedule. Money, Section 4(f) parkland and historic-site protection, Coast Guard bridge permits, Clean Air Act conformity, FAA noise and airspace rules, and local politics are all outside its reach.

### What reaches transportation projects, and what doesn't

| Provision | Applies to transportation? | Practical effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NEPA clocks, remand-only remedy, standing limits (Secs. 1106, 1110) | Yes, all projects | Highway and transit cases already had a 150-day filing limit under 23 U.S.C. 139(l), so the real change is that a flawed review no longer halts construction |
| NEPA grant exclusion (Sec. 1102) | Only where the agency lacks "predominant control" | FTA capital grants and FHWA-approved projects usually involve that control, so most large projects still get NEPA review |
| Lifeline rebuild exclusion (Sec. 1102) | Yes, for bridges and roads | Rebuilding a destroyed or failing bridge in the "immediate vicinity," with under 20% added capacity and no new through lanes, needs no NEPA review at all |
| §106 historic review (Sec. 2301) | Narrowed | Applies only to portions under a federal permit and substantial federal control; grant-only projects drop out; visual effects count only if they significantly diminish a property; hard deadlines |
| ESA consultation (Secs. 1302–1303) | Yes; right-of-way exemption for highway and transit only | 60-day consultation for all; FRA intercity rail gets no right-of-way exemption |
| CWA §401 and §404 (Secs. 1202–1205) | Yes | One-year state cap with a clear-and-convincing burden; Nationwide Permit 14 (linear transportation, including rail) covers projects affecting under 2 acres |
| No-revocation rule (Sec. 1401) | Yes, for non-federal sponsors | Protects state DOT, transit-agency and Amtrak records of decision and permits; probably not grant agreements, which are funding, not authorizations |
| Disparate-treatment damages (Sec. 1402) | No | None of the 47 listed project types is transportation |
| Section 4(f), Coast Guard bridge permits, Corps §408, air-quality conformity, FAA airspace and noise | Not amended | Often the real schedule drivers |

### What this means

- Permitting is rarely the binding constraint for transportation. The Key Bridge cleared NEPA quickly and still slipped two years on cost and engineering. IBR's longest delays came from the Coast Guard height dispute and state funding, neither of which the bill touches.
- The biggest transportation effect is durability. Remand-only remedies and Sec. 1401 mean that once a state DOT or transit agency has its record of decision, a lawsuit or a hostile federal agency can no longer easily stop construction.
- Rail gets less than transit. The ESA right-of-way exemption and the existing highway and transit filing limit favor FHWA and FTA projects over FRA ones; see the drafting fixes under Recommendations.
- Funding risk is unaddressed. Sec. 1401 likely does not cover grant terminations like the FRA's 2025 cancellation of about $4 billion for California high-speed rail. If drafters want rail and transit to share in the certainty title, they would need to name grant agreements explicitly.

## Layering a 2029 Democratic climate law

A strong 2029 climate law delivers about half its 2035 potential without this bill and about four-fifths with it. That gap, roughly 0.4–0.7 billion tons a year by 2035, is about what the power sectors of California and Texas emit today.

The four scenarios are A, current policy (Rhodium's post-OBBBA range); B, this bill only; C, a 2029 climate law without this bill; and D, both. Dots are midpoints and whiskers show the range. These are my illustrative estimates, scaled from Rhodium, REPEAT, RFF and RMI modeling, not model runs.

The chart's shape is the argument. Scenarios barely differ in 2030, because nothing enacted in 2029 can clear 5-year queues or 10-year line builds. By 2035 the climate law is worth about 10 points more with the bill, and by 2040 about 14 points, as transmission-dependent wind, solar and geothermal compound.

### What a 2029 law should do because of this bill

- Override Title IV for climate standards. Add a clause stating that emissions limits and compliance obligations under the new law are not actions that "amend or alter" a permit under Sec. 1401.
- Pair money with planning. Tie transmission funding to lines selected in FERC-approved interregional plans, which already qualify for backstop siting.
- Use reconciliation where the Byrd rule allows. A carbon fee and dividend, credits, a border adjustment and grants can pass with 51 votes. Anything touching FERC jurisdiction, judicial review or Title IV still needs 60.

## Risks the text creates

The largest risks are fossil irreversibility, weaker biodiversity and Tribal protections, and an open-ended fiscal liability. None is fatal to the climate case, but each should be fought over in November amendments.

| Risk | Where in the text | Rough scale | Possible fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gas pipeline expansion without review | Sec. 2102(b): looping, compression and capacity increases in existing corridors exempt from NEPA and §106 | Many Bcf/d of expansion on existing systems such as Transco, Texas Eastern and Mountain Valley without public review | Cap the exemption by capacity added, or require an EA above a threshold |
| Fossil approvals become permanent | Sec. 1401 covers LNG export authorizations, pipelines and leases; Sec. 1402 lists coal, oil, LNG and refining | A future LNG pause or revocation could cost billions per terminal | Add an express exception for emissions standards and statutory changes |
| Weaker state water leverage | Sec. 1202: "direct point source discharge" only for pipelines and transmission; clear-and-convincing burden for all licenses | Ends Constitution-style 401 vetoes; weakens state fish conditions on FERC dams | Keep the deadline reforms but drop the point-source limit and the evidentiary burden |
| Biodiversity | Secs. 1302–1304: 60-day consultation, D.C. Circuit-only review, state assumption | Depends on which states apply; Interior audits only four years | Require FWS concurrence for jeopardy calls; extend audits |
| Tribal consultation | Sec. 2301 "sole discretion" override; Sec. 1110 bars NEPA review of Tribal trust land decisions except by that Tribe | Concerns NATHPO and many Tribal nations; litigation likely | Require written responses to Tribal objections; allow review on Tribal request |
| Fiscal exposure | Secs. 1401–1402 damages from the Judgment Fund, without appropriation | Uncapped; one bad-faith policy against one technology class could cost tens of billions | Cap damages per project or per year, or require appropriations above a threshold |
| Remedies against bad reviews | Sec. 1110: remand only; no injunction for NEPA or NHPA alone | Projects finish before defects are fixed | Allow vacatur for reviews that omit a significant effect entirely |

The fossil lock-in risk is real but should be kept in proportion. Gas pipelines already have federal eminent domain and a FERC siting process, while transmission had neither. So the bill relieves a far tighter constraint for clean power than for gas. RMI's analysis of the comparable 2024 bill found net emissions reductions even in its worst case (Third Way summary).

## Recommendations

The priority for supporters is speed and a clean presentment date; the priority for skeptics is a few targeted amendments, not a rewrite.

### Senate EPW and ENR staff

- Protect Title IV, §318 consolidated planning, and §228 data-center cost allocation above everything else.
- Move the bill early enough to present it by about Dec. 18–21. That avoids a pocket veto and keeps the offshore wind same-judge window open.
- Spend amendment capital on a Title IV exception for emissions standards and statutory changes, and a cap on Judgment Fund damages. Both also help with CBO and Republican budget hawks.
- Offer progressives the 401 point-source fix, FWS concurrence on state ESA jeopardy calls, and a written-response duty on Tribal objections.
- Ask whether FAA obstruction-evaluation and Defense Siting Clearinghouse determinations count as a "Federal authorization or permit." Onshore wind's real choke point depends on the answer.

### House Democrats

- Take the Senate text as an amendment to the SPEED Act, which needs only concurrence. A Democratic House in 2027 gains no leverage over the Senate filibuster or the White House.
- Trade votes for floor commitments on wind and solar regular order, not for reopening the text.

### Passenger rail drafting gaps

- ESA. Sec. 1303 keys the right-of-way exemption to the MAP-21 §1316 exclusion, which covers highway and transit projects. Add projects under 49 U.S.C. chapters 229 and 243 (FRA programs) within existing railroad rights-of-way.
- NEPA and §106. Grant-only projects escape review where the agency lacks "predominant control." FRA usually exercises such control over state corridor projects, so clarify whether its grant agreements count, or rail may get less relief than transit.
- Litigation limit. Sec. 2201's 150-day limit covers energy and mineral projects only. Rail and transit rely on the general NEPA limit in Sec. 1110, which is fine, but say so in report language.
- Section 1402. Intercity rail is not a listed project type, so a future administration could slow-walk rail approvals without damages exposure. Consider adding it.

### 2029 climate-law planners

- Assume the transmission tools arrive around 2029–30 and sequence funding to plan-selected lines.
- Draft an express override of Sec. 1401 for new emissions limits and compliance obligations.
- Put the carbon fee and dividend, credits, border adjustment and grants in reconciliation; save 60-vote capital for anything touching FERC or judicial review.

### Developers

- File backstop applications for stalled 345 kV+ lines the day FERC's rules allow simultaneous filing.
- Start logging approval and denial data by project type now. Sec. 1402 claims depend on statistics and a five-year baseline.

## Caveats and sources

The bill analysis rests on the full 417-page Senate text (draft KAT26642, released Sept. 30, 2026); the scenario numbers are illustrative judgment, not model output.

- Draft text. Negotiators expect changes in November. Section numbers and cross-references may shift, and a few drafting errors are visible (for example, Sec. 2215 cites a nonexistent "section 205(d)" of this Act for the Abandoned Hardrock Mine Fund).
- Interpretation. How courts read "direct point source discharge," "predominant control," "interfere with … operation at full capacity," and whether FAA or Defense clearances count as federal permits will decide much of the bill's real effect. I have flagged these as open questions, not settled readings.
- Probabilities. These depend on the midterm results, the lame-duck calendar and the White House. A single presidential statement against wind could move the odds by 10 points or more.
- Climate figures. Scenario ranges scale published modeling by my own realization factors. Rhodium's figures come from before this bill; REPEAT's transmission sensitivity dates to 2022; RFF's carbon-fee results predate the Inflation Reduction Act and OBBBA. Read them as orders of magnitude.
- Sourcing. Except for the Argus piece, which I opened for this version, the outside sources below were gathered in the earlier research pass, not re-opened.
