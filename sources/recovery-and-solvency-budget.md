## The direction I recommend

Project 2050 should become a plan for governing the **whole federal budget**, with a recovery phase that improves people's circumstances before the largest new broad contributions begin. It should preserve the basic floor, the full education commitment and Social Security's benefit protections, while being much less deferential to inherited spending allocations and assumptions about how easily revenue can be raised.

The integrated work changes my conclusion in two ways. First, the education expansion is somewhat less expensive than the preceding calculation suggested, once existing spending and old agenda increments are separated by component. Second, combining education, Social Security, leave and general-fund financing makes the middle-income burden more difficult than any isolated fund analysis showed. A $300 adult credit is affordable within a plausible central fiscal scenario, but it does not protect enough people from the accumulated contributions. I would **not adopt either of the two principal tax schedules in this report**. They are useful, specified budgets that expose the choices; neither earns a D3 household pass.

I prefer the architecture of **mixed education financing, explicit public support for social insurance, a simpler income-based replacement for the health-exclusion tax, and a smaller but better justified capacity budget**. I would develop that architecture with a coordinated household contribution credit, rather than keep adding independent payroll charges and then trying to compensate each one separately. The cost of that protection must be part of the settlement from the outset. An automatic fund backstop can prevent interrupted benefits; it cannot manufacture the national resources to pay for them.

## Education: the earlier calculation needed correction

Your concern was right. My earlier calculation subtracted a pooled $0.77T current-law transfer and $1.23T of existing agenda spending from a $2.602T total. That established an accounting identity; it did **not independently verify** the pieces. The source combines gross program targets with new initiatives, and its total interpolation differed from the sum of its component paths.

The corrected working reconciliation is:

| FY2030–39, nominal $T | Amount | Meaning |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Full education-fund spending | **2.572** | Sum of seven independently interpolated source components |
| Existing current-law spending transferred | **0.854** | Meals, recurring IDEA section 611 and Title I-A; independently anchored starting levels, projected forward |
| Increment above current law | **1.718** | The actual additional spending in this interpretation |
| Increment already carried in the old agenda | **1.230** | FAM-2, EDU-1 including IDEA, EDU-3 and EDU-4 |
| Additional spending beyond that agenda | **0.488** | New scope and revised costs, counted once |

Thus the full fund is **$0.854T existing spending + $1.230T already-planned new spending + $0.488T further spending**. Adding all existing education spending to the source's $2.572T would double-count meals, Title I and IDEA. Calling the entire $2.572T new spending would be wrong for the same reason.

The most useful independent checks are that current-law meals are already a substantial part of the proposed universal program; a $30B Title I floor is a total floor, not a $30B addition; and IDEA's percentage-of-per-pupil-spending target describes total federal grants. I anchored those starting amounts to USDA and CRS program data, rather than back-solving a pooled transfer to make the desired total work. The old EDU-1 ledger row combines $0.08T Title I and $0.25T IDEA, so both portions move, without sweeping unrelated competitive ESEA programs into the fund. [Methods, education table and sources 1–5](../index/tools/recovery-budget/README.md#education-what-was-actually-reconciled)

There are still important limits. Current appropriations are not a complete future outlay baseline. The reference grows assigned current-law spending 3% a year; 2–4% gives approximately **$0.408–0.562T** in additional costs beyond the old agenda. This is sensitivity to the baseline, not a confidence interval for the program's cost. The source's 2049 components sum to $441B, versus a printed $442B total; the new calculation retains the actual component sum.

**Child care needs an explicit boundary decision.** The reference retains existing CCDF, Head Start and preschool-development funding outside the fund and treats the source's care line as expansion above them. Its $12B 2030 care line is too small to include those existing programs, already about $25B in FY2026. Moving them into the institution as well would increase the full fund to approximately **$2.895T**, with **$1.177T of existing spending transferred**. The additional cost remains $0.488T: organizational consolidation is not new national spending or a saving. If the new benefits actually replace part of existing care, a finer overlap crosswalk is required. I have not assumed that all existing teacher programs duplicate the new teacher/PARA grants either.

The component check also exposes conflicting ramps. The education source provides less early IDEA expansion than the old fiscal profile, while the old pipeline profile continues spending after the source's temporary surge tapers. Preserving every old component's annual increment would add about **$23B** in the first decade. That sensitivity barely changes the fiscal conclusion, but the choice should be made deliberately. It would be a mistake to interpret every old spending profile as an inviolable benefit promise—or to conceal a slower IDEA ramp as an accounting correction.

## A real federal budget, with explicit limits

The project already had a CBO-based federal baseline. What it lacked was a sufficiently coherent bridge between that baseline and its own programs. The old display held revenue at its baseline while recording many tax increases as negative spending. Social Security receipts began in 2030 in that fiscal table despite the adopted 2031 start. Leave was assumed to net to zero even when annual contributions and benefits differed. HI financing was analyzed separately without consistently entering consolidated fiscal totals.

The new tool separates receipts and outlays; replaces the old Social Security net row with annual receipts and benefit changes; adds leave's actual cash balance; and brings HI's dedicated receipts and health savings into the same budget. It starts with **existing government spending, revenue and debt**, not a blank slate. The general fund's payments to education and HI remain visible, but disappear as transfers when the federal government is consolidated. Trust-fund interest is not counted as a new national resource. Treasury lending is not revenue. Existing estate receipts are not reassigned to education and also left available elsewhere.

This is a material improvement, not a complete Treasury account reconstruction. The original baseline includes preliminary FY2026 data and authored tariff adjustments. Some transportation amounts are budget authority rather than cash outlays, and the inherited ledger nets dedicated receipts against spending. The new report explicitly restores matched receipts and uses for presentation, using approximate annual profiles. That changes the displayed size of government without creating a deficit saving. Account-specific outlay lags, transportation baseline overlaps and state/local budgets remain incomplete. The [audit CSV](recovery-budget-audit.csv) makes those conventions inspectable; the [methods](../index/tools/recovery-budget/README.md#where-the-total-budget-view-is-still-approximate) explain their limits.

I recommend making this total-federal-budget perspective the default analytical view, with the incremental agenda and each fund available underneath it. “Our new programs are paid for” is a weaker claim than “government can continue providing its existing and new commitments.” The adjusted current-law baseline reaches approximately **180% public debt/GDP in 2056**. Even the old agenda's improvement on that path was insufficient evidence of stability.

## Two specified budgets, and what they reveal

Both principal alternatives retain existing core benefits and most existing revenues, repair the social-insurance financing described below, include a $300 refundable adult credit, and use the same spending counterbudget. Amounts below are combined payroll rates; an employer share remains an economic cost to workers over time.

| Choice | Mixed education and income financing | Larger-payroll comparator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Education contribution | 1.4 points uncapped, phased 2031–35 | 2.0 points, same phase |
| Education public share | 30% of full spending, plus a transition grant through 2034 | Transition grant through 2034; no permanent share |
| C19b health-exclusion cap and C20 surtax | Replaced | Retained, phased 2033–35 |
| New higher AGI tier | 4 points above indexed $100k single/$200k joint, phased 2033–35 | None beyond retained C20 |
| New broad AGI tier | 2 points above indexed $20k/$40k, phased 2035–44 | Same |
| Credit | $300 per adult annually in 2030 dollars, CPI indexed; half-year in FY2029 | Same |

Both AGI thresholds are expressed in the source's **2025 dollars**, indexed thereafter; the two tiers are additive. The reference therefore has six additional AGI points above its upper threshold at maturity, replacing the two named old instruments. These are substantial taxes. The yield uses CBO/JCT's December 2024 options as an anchor, scales with GDP and discounts the new yield by 20%; the downside discounts it by 40%. That is a transparent estimate, **not a joint score on today's tax law**, and the upper threshold includes some professional and other middle/upper-middle-income households. It should not be described as a billionaires-only tax. [Source and method](../index/tools/recovery-budget/README.md#tax-credit-stress-and-household-assumptions)

| Result | Mixed | Larger payroll |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Credit cost, 2030–39 | $0.916T | $0.916T |
| New AGI contribution receipts, after new-yield haircut | $1.907T | $0.496T, in addition to retained taxes |
| Education GF grants | $1.078T | $0.437T |
| Public debt/GDP, 2039 | 119.4% | 119.0% |
| Public debt/GDP, 2056 | 113.9% | 112.2% |
| Debt ratio nonincreasing in every year 2045–56? | Yes, centrally | Yes, centrally |
| Fourth-quintile average household net effect in 2039 | **−$2,697** | **−$4,307** |

The last row includes modeled benefits as well as taxes, using the project's distribution assumptions. It is not a prediction for an individual household. It is enough to reject a claim that these schedules satisfy the existing household standard. Lower quintiles show large average gains, while the fourth quintile turns negative as education, leave, Social Security and the later income contribution accumulate. The larger-payroll version is worse on this measure despite its slightly better debt result.

The alternative with stronger protection is informative. A **$1,500 adult credit**, financed partly by raising the higher AGI tier from four to **ten points**, costs **$4.579T** over the decade and raises modeled new AGI receipts to **$4.023T**. Debt/GDP still declines to about **118.4% in 2056** centrally. Yet the fourth-quintile diagnostic remains slightly negative in 2039, about **−$392**, and the higher-rate extrapolation is much less credible. It also requires roughly **$207B** of additional FY2029 primary borrowing including the delivery advance. I would not recommend that universal cash expansion and tax stack as the answer. It demonstrates why precisely targeted contribution relief is worth developing, and why “a rebate will protect the middle class” is not a financing plan.

The next protection design should coordinate the education, leave and later Social Security contributions. A refundable credit against a defined initial band of earnings, with gradual household-income recovery at the upper end, could protect workers more efficiently than the large universal-credit case. Retain a nonworker component so disabled people, caregivers and retirees do not disappear from recovery. Automatic payment and a simple annual reconciliation matter; assistance must not depend on employment. I have **not booked unpriced savings from targeting**. IRS tables were identified but the workbook was not successfully ingested, and the available model cannot establish the joint wage, income, family and benefit distribution needed to price that credit responsibly.

## What I would spend less on—and what I would protect

The clearest candidate for substantial reduction is the **capacity allocation**, not because administration is expendable but because its current scale has little evidentiary foundation. The domain explicitly says its agency allocations were judgmental and were not derived from existing agency budgets. The same allocation shares are applied to a $600B surge and a $490B permanent-increase window. NOAA's $40B surge share plus the proportional sustained share would total roughly $73B over the window; CRS reports FY2026 NOAA appropriations of $6.1B, or $6.6B on the different direct-obligations measure. An addition on that scale requires a program explanation, particularly where satellites, grants and field operations are being labeled together as capacity. [Canonical rationale](../domains/domain-capacity.md); [CRS enacted funding](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13024.html).

I test **$300B surge plus $300B sustained** instead, reducing authorizations by $490B. These are counterbudget ceilings to challenge the inherited scale, not a bottom-up finding that $600B is exactly sufficient. Protect benefit adjudication, revenue administration, grants/payment systems, labor enforcement and hiring bottlenecks first. Require separate justification for large mission-program investments. The counterbudget also reserves a **$200B reduction in projected IRS enforcement yield**: it would be dishonest to reduce the capacity budget while assuming every revenue and delivery promise remains unaffected. The net fiscal improvement from this capacity choice is therefore $290B before interest, with additional revenue downside tested separately.

For displacement support, reduce AI-4's $200B placeholder to **$120B**, while adding a separate **$100B participation offer** and retaining apprenticeships and national service. The redesigned offer should cover practical routes into employment, education, training and community participation—transport, child care during training, accommodations, navigators and paid placements—without requiring a person to prove AI caused their displacement. This is a modest net increase in these two lines, with a different purpose and institution. The budget is an envelope, not proof of a particular number of successful placements. A smaller wage-insurance allocation means less or differently targeted insurance; it is not free efficiency.

Add **$135B for a bounded rental-assistance expansion** beyond the adopted million awards, and **$100B of explicit delivery/cost contingency** for health restoration, claims failures and other still-unpriced obligations. The rental allowance could support roughly another million awards when mature at the earlier illustrative per-award cost, but local rents and lease-up determine actual reach. It must be paired with supply, accessible units and landlord participation. The contingency is a real budget cost, not a permission to claim the health implementation gap has been fully priced. Together with participation, these additions total $335B. The $570B gross CAP/AI reduction, less those additions and the $200B IRS allowance, leaves only **$35B net primary improvement**. The point is better spending composition, not pretending small trims solve government-wide solvency.

I retain the clean-energy credit budget in these fiscal cases because the project's climate experiments do not establish a defensible amount to remove. They do support shifting the next policy-development effort toward grid delivery, adaptation and workforce constraints: the public-grid option materially improves modeled clean power, whereas eliminating the credit bundle increases cumulative emissions. The next climate budget should compare marginal cost and additional emissions reductions with the same cost, transmission and demand assumptions, including household bill effects. It should distinguish transferable credits, refundable credits, loans and direct construction. Neither modeled abatement nor an unpriced grid institution is a Treasury pay-for. [Existing climate comparison](fiscal-redesign-and-participation-2026-10-26.md)

## Solvency belongs to institutions and to the country

**Social Security.** Preserve the benefit floor and CPI-E choice, complete the high-income base changes including SS-5, and test a combined additional two payroll points on the existing capped base, phased 2035–44. The integrated case includes a conservative interaction loss between the investment/pass-through proposals and an income-tax-offset allowance for new wage contributions. It passes the existing combined-fund model's conservative reserve test through 2100, with an approximately **+0.50%-of-payroll actuarial balance**. That is not a joint SSA score, does not certify OASI and DI separately, and leaves the later contribution burden visible in the household test. The legal separation of the accounts cannot be solved by pointing to a combined balance.

**Medicare HI.** Count H1a and the Part A share of HLT-6 only once, and add an explicit GF contribution reaching **0.35% of HI payroll in 2039**, beginning in 2035. That costs the GF approximately **$235B in 2030–39** in the translated fiscal economy. It keeps the summary fund model positive through 2100 even with half the assumed Part A savings. It does not reduce the consolidated deficit: government is moving money between accounts. The case for public support is that hospital insurance should not depend entirely on repeatedly raising payroll taxes or hoping that payment savings exceed their credible range. The amount remains provisional until the HI annual data are better calibrated.

**Paid leave.** The existing 0.85-point ceiling cannot finance the high-take-up cost. A **1.10-point ceiling**, with the existing gradual adjustment mechanism, repays the modeled Treasury loan in **2048**, then remains debt-free through 2100 in the extended run. I use high take-up for this budget because successful access should not itself make the institution insolvent. Contribution changes still cost workers; the general fund also bears the income-tax offset. This is preferable to writing a permanent loan and describing it as a temporary bridge.

**Education.** Fixed 1.4-point/30%-public-share financing survives the central path but fails the combined education downside in **2035**. Meanwhile, favorable central assumptions accumulate an implausibly large reserve over 75 years. A better institutional alternative is a reserve corridor: build toward one year of outlays over 2035–39; provide an explicit public backstop when necessary; reduce grants and eventually contributions if reserves exceed two years of spending. In the modeled downside, the required decade GF grants rise from approximately **$1.309T to $1.703T**. The fund stays solvent because the public obligation is explicit. Consolidated debt does not improve merely because the account now passes its reserve test. This is the education financing rule I would develop rather than adopt the fixed-rate central result as a permanent settlement.

State financing cannot be treated as a saving without consequences. If the care line's $143B in 2049 is federal spending at a 60% share, the corresponding state share is **$95B**, not the source's approximately $57B. The latter multiplies $143B by 40% without first identifying whether it is federal or total spending. The share schedule may apply only to pre-K rather than every care dollar, so $95B is a boundary illustration, not a scored state bill. An 80% federal share on the entire same service base would add roughly **$48B federal spending in 2049** and reduce the state burden by that amount. Poorer states need an equalization rule and protection against substituting this new match for existing education spending. This remains outside the central federal score; the report does not claim all levels of government are solvent.

## Recovery must have a visible first year and an honest long run

The smaller-credit budget provides approximately **$39B of FY2029 payments**, plus a **$10B delivery advance** taken from the following year's capacity surge. That is about **$49B of additional FY2029 primary borrowing**, not a fully financed immediate expansion. Six months of the $300 annual credit is tangible but modest. It should accompany rapid restoration of health coverage and payment rights, meals through existing institutions, rental awards as units become available, and practical access to training and work. The tool does not assume every benefit is deliverable on inauguration day.

Someone unable to work receives the adult credit and retains the basic floor; the participation offer is an opportunity, not a condition for assistance. A worker without children receives the credit and insurance protections but may obtain little immediate value from education benefits while paying the contribution. A self-employed person sees both payroll shares directly. A retiree may face the broad income tier on taxable retirement income. These cases require explicit treatment in the next distributional score. An average gain for families with children cannot stand in for all of them.

The public explanation should be: **recovery will take time, but it must begin with things people can see and use**. Lower inflation does not restore the previous price level. Government can reduce the cost of essentials, improve earnings and bargaining power, expand reliable services, and make contributions fairer. Tie that account to concrete recovery measures—coverage restored, rent paid, a training place actually available—and to enforceable remedies when delivery fails. A notice, appeal, accessible assistance and continuity of essential benefits are part of what government owes, not administrative extras to be cut first.

The long-run result remains conditional. The mixed budget's central debt ratio falls to **113.9% in 2056**. The combined receipt/cost downside reaches **136.5% and is still rising**; a separate one-point interest increase on all public debt reaches **150.1%**. Holding the upper income tier fixed, the model's broad-tier rate needed to stabilize debt rises from about **1.35 points centrally to 5.10 points in the downside and 5.14 under the interest shock**. Those are diagnostic static equivalents, not recommendations to levy a five-point broad surtax. They show that the central two-point schedule has some fiscal margin but not enough protection against major adverse conditions.

Do not impose automatic recession benefit cuts to meet a debt ratio. Require an independent periodic whole-budget assessment, explicit legislative correction of persistent structural gaps, and preauthorized temporary fund support during downturns. Separate the recession escape from the eventual restoration of a sustainable path. A fund reserve rule and a public-debt rule serve different purposes; neither should disguise an unfunded promise.

## The next decision and substantial assignment

I recommend keeping **mixed education finance with a reserve corridor**, replacing the health-exclusion cap in the preferred design, and proceeding with a **smaller, justified capacity counterbudget and a participation institution**. I would retain the basic benefit floor and full education scope while resolving the care boundary and IDEA ramp. These choices preserve the project’s purposes and expose their financing honestly.

The next substantial assignment should be a **joint contribution-and-protection settlement**, using this integrated budget rather than rebuilding separate fund analyses. Price one coordinated refundable contribution credit on a credible current-law tax/benefit distribution; compare it with a narrower late income contribution and with explicit reductions in low-value spending. Test workers without children, disabled/nonworking adults, retirees, single adults and self-employed people alongside income groups. Jointly re-estimate the overlapping high-income taxes and health savings, and replace the largest capacity placeholders with current spending/workload baselines. The central question is whether the project can protect ordinary households while financing a durable expansion—not which isolated levy can close the next row.

The current result is sufficiently developed to guide that choice: there is a credible path to a more capable government with a stable central debt trajectory, but it requires large, visible resources, better-founded spending choices and a stronger household design than the first fiscal pass supplied. I recommend the architecture; I do not recommend adopting the modeled rates before that joint work.

**Validation and limits.** Twelve new accounting/regression tests cover education overlap, current-law consolidation neutrality, the 2031 SS start, actual 2029 cash, fund-transfer cancellation, leave-model extension, whole-debt interest stress, the debt-rate solver and visible household failure.
