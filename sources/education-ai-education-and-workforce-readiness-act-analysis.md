# Bonamici AI and Education Bills: Session Analysis
2026-10-19 · @espen

## Bottom line
The AI Education and Workforce Readiness Act authorizes $1.48B a year ($7.4B over FY2027–31), but its lasting value is the definitions, data and standards it builds, not the money. Its largest fiscal item is actually the Sec. 208 tax credit, which as drafted could cost roughly $50–130B over ten years because nothing limits it to AI training.
  - **Scope:** five titles plus general provisions, covering K-12 and higher ed, workforce reskilling, equity, data center environmental disclosure, and student and worker data privacy.
  - **Design:** a governance-first framework that deliberately fences AI industry influence out of curricula, councils and research centers.
  - **Fit:** it is effectively the AI chapter of Rep. Bonamici's Transforming Education for the Future Act, which never mentions AI but protects the Education Department capacity the AI bill depends on.
  - **Politics:** a 2027–28 deal with Trump would likely pass a skinny workforce-and-credit version. A 2029–30 trifecta could pass most of it, split between reconciliation and an ESEA reauthorization.
  - **Best near-term path:** Title IV's data center disclosure and NIST measurement standards, which fit the bipartisan data center backlash and could move on their own in broader data center legislation.

## Scope and structure
The bill is 137 pages across general provisions and five titles, coordinated mainly by the Departments of Education (ED) and Labor (DOL), with NSF, NIST, IES, EEOC, Commerce, OSTP and the FTC in supporting roles. It is introduced in the House; the committee referral was blank in the text reviewed.
| Part | What it does | Key sections |
| General provisions | DOL list of AI-augmented occupations (with public comment and demographic and geographic analysis); ED/DOL-led Interagency Council and national strategy; a compliance regime binding every funded entity | Secs. 4–6 |
| Title I: Education and training | ED career pathway consortia pairing humanities and AI literacy with paid work-based learning; stackable micro-credential pilot; optional ESEA state plan descriptions; bar on AI firms controlling Title IV curricula or requiring exclusive platforms; school leader training; humanities-based AI ethics research; NSF Regional Centers barred from commercializing products; IES practice guides and expanded RELs | Secs. 101–108 |
| Title II: Workforce | DOL National AI Workforce Strategy; advisory council with AI industry seats non-voting; state implementation plans; apprenticeship, reskilling and public service fellowship grants; Rehabilitation Act accessibility amendments; employer reskilling tax credit; grantee reporting on AI displacement | Secs. 201–209 |
| Title III: Equity | Multilingual AI literacy grants; MSI capacity building and HEA amendments; career equity scholarships; DOL/EEOC/Commerce benchmarks for underrepresented groups in AI-augmented jobs; mentorship networks; DOL/EEOC employer guidance on AI in hiring and evaluation | Secs. 301–307 |
| Title IV: Environmental impacts | DOL grants building regulator and community oversight capacity (AI firms excluded); NSF research on siting effects (not usable to optimize data centers); audited pre-construction and five-year post-operation energy, water and land disclosure by operators with a federal nexus; NIST inference-level measurement standards and an OSTP federal inventory | Secs. 401–405 |
| Title V: Privacy | ED student data breach portal; AI risk assessment framework for edtech; generative AI guidance with harmful-content safeguards and parental opt-outs; human review required for automated decisions in funded workforce programs; MOUs with FTC, NIST and DOJ; funding bans and IG/FTC referrals | Secs. 501–506 |

The compliance regime in Sec. 6 is the bill's spine. Every funded entity must publish under open licenses, undergo independent evaluation, adopt the designated risk management framework, offer student opt-outs from AI tools, and keep a data security program that bars commercial use of student data.

## Appropriations
Every authorization in the bill is "for each of fiscal years 2027 through 2031," so the figures are annual, not five-year totals. The section-by-section summary does not make this clear. Discretionary authorizations total $1.48B a year and $7.4B over five years; the tax credit sits outside these totals.

Funding observations:
  - **Lump sums spread thin.** Each title is one authorization with no per-section allocation. Titles II and III each fund five or six grant programs from $450M a year, leaving wide discretion to appropriators and agencies.
  - **Split across appropriations subcommittees.** Title I mixes ED programs (Labor-HHS) with NSF Regional Centers (Commerce-Justice-Science). Title IV spans DOL, NSF, NIST and OSTP. The Interagency Council reports only to the Labor-HHS subcommittees, so the CJS-funded pieces lack a natural champion.
  - **Compliance outruns its funding.** Sec. 6 obligations fall on every grantee, but only $10M a year supports the list, the council and the compliance machinery. Small grantees such as libraries and tribal colleges will feel the burden most.
  - **Pilot scale.** At roughly $5–10K per participant, Title II reaches tens of thousands of workers a year, not millions (estimate).

## Sec. 208 tax credit
As drafted, the credit would likely cost about $50–130B over FY2027–36, with a central guess of $60–90B. That is roughly ten times the bill's entire discretionary authorization. All figures here are back-of-envelope estimates, not a JCT score.
**The drafting issue.** The provision is titled an "AI reskilling credit," but nothing in the text requires AI content. A qualified training program includes any program sponsored by an employer, trade association, sector partnership or labor organization (§45BB(c)(2)(D)), plus anything on a state's WIOA eligible training provider list. Read literally, it is a permanent 40% credit on essentially all employer-paid training, a market of roughly $100B a year by industry survey estimates.
**Key terms.** 40% of qualified expenses, capped at $250K a year for smaller employers and $1M for others. Effective for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2026, with no sunset. Small businesses can elect to apply it against payroll tax, borrowing the R&D credit's mechanism. Grant-funded costs are excluded, and the deduction is reduced by the credit amount.
| Scenario | Annual cost at maturity | 10-year cost (FY27–36) |
| Text as written, cautious take-up | ~$6–8B | ~$45–65B |
| Text as written, aggressive take-up | ~$12–16B | ~$90–130B |
| Narrowed to AI-specific training | ~$1–3B | ~$8–25B |

How the estimate was built:
  - **Large employers drive most of the cost.** Firms spending $2.5M or more on training hit the $1M cap. Several thousand firms plausibly clear that bar and have tax staff to claim it, adding about $7–11B a year.
  - **Small and mid-sized firms add less.** Claims run roughly $10–50K each, and small-business credits historically see 20–50% take-up at best, adding about $2–5B a year.
  - **Tax-exempt organizations are an upside risk.** The payroll election borrows the Employee Retention Credit's definition of a qualifying tax-exempt organization, which appears to let hospitals and universities claim up to $250K each.
  - **Offsets.** The deduction haircut offsets about a fifth of gross cost. Firms without tax liability can only carry the credit forward, and claims ramp up as Treasury writes verification rules.
  - **Comparisons for scale.** The R&D credit costs a few tens of billions a year; the Work Opportunity Tax Credit about $1–2B; the §127 educational assistance exclusion about $1–1.5B (approximate, from memory).
Adding a requirement that training cover AI skills or target AI-augmented occupations, and possibly a sunset, would cut the score by roughly 80% and make the credit match its name. Note also that the one-pager calls it a "small business" credit, but the text covers every employer.

## Drafting issues and summary mismatches
The two biggest drafting issues are the unbounded tax credit and the breadth of Title IV's "federal nexus."
| Issue | Where | Why it matters |
| Credit not limited to AI training | Sec. 208, §45BB(c)(2)(D) | Drives a ~$50–130B score; first question from Ways and Means or JCT |
| Very broad "federal nexus" | Sec. 401 definitions, Sec. 404 | Operating under any federal permit, license or authorization triggers disclosure, which could reach most large data centers through air, water or grid permits. Its biggest strength and first target for opponents |
| Weak enforcement for Title IV | Sec. 404 | The penalty is ineligibility for federal financial assistance plus IG referral. That works for grantees but has little leverage over operators whose nexus is a permit, not a grant |
| Authorizations split across subcommittees | Titles I and IV | Labor-HHS and CJS would each fund pieces of the same title |
| One-pager: "small business" credit | One-pager vs. Sec. 208 | The text covers all employers; small firms only get a lower cap and the payroll election |
| One-pager: Council publishes the jobs list | One-pager vs. Sec. 4 | DOL publishes the list, not the Interagency Council |
| One-pager: Title V "enforced jointly" with FTC | One-pager vs. Sec. 506 | The FTC's role is referral for commercial misuse of student data, not joint enforcement |
| Section-by-section: funding periods | Secs. 7, 109, 210, 308, 406, 507 | Reads like five-year totals; the text authorizes each amount for each fiscal year |

Likely committee referrals are multiple: Education and Workforce as primary, plus Energy and Commerce and Science, Space and Technology for Title IV, and Ways and Means for the credit.

## What the bill seeks to do
The bill's core claim is that AI policy for schools and workers should be about governance, not just building an industry talent pipeline. It funds literacy and reskilling, but its signature is the fencing of industry influence:
  - AI firms cannot control Title IV curricula or require exclusive use of their platforms (Sec. 104).
  - Industry representatives get only non-voting seats on the DOL advisory council (Sec. 202).
  - Regional Centers cannot develop or commercialize AI products (Sec. 107).
  - AI companies are excluded from the environmental oversight grants (Sec. 402), and the environmental research cannot be used to make data centers more efficient (Sec. 403).
A humanities thread runs through the whole bill, from career pathways that pair AI literacy with the social sciences to AI ethics research housed in humanities departments. That fits Rep. Bonamici's long-standing STEAM priorities.
The deeper strategy is to lay foundations for future legislating and regulation:
  - **Definitions.** The occupations list gives Congress a working definition of an "AI-affected worker," the eligibility hook for any later Trade Adjustment Assistance-style program for AI.
  - **Data.** Displacement reporting (Sec. 209) and equity benchmarks (Sec. 305) create the first federal data series on AI's labor effects, though only from grantees.
  - **Standards.** Title V's risk assessments and Sec. 104 would become de facto national edtech procurement standards, since nearly every district takes ESEA money. NIST's inference-level measurement standards build the data basis for later data center regulation, much as emissions inventories preceded carbon rules.
The result is a deliberate counter-model to an industry-partnership approach to AI education. Its $1.48B a year is small relative to the disruption it anticipates; its architecture is what a future Congress could scale.

## Fit with the Transforming Education for the Future Act
The 466-page omnibus Rep. Bonamici introduced the week before is the foundation; the AI bill is the AI chapter bolted onto it. The omnibus one-pager names generative AI as a force reshaping schools, but "artificial intelligence" appears nowhere in its text. Its National Education Technology Clearinghouse and edtech guidance (Secs. 24201–02) don't mention AI either.
Where the two bills connect:
  - **Protecting ED.** Sec. 30113 bars moving ESEA, IDEA, Perkins or ESRA functions or funds out of ED, including by interagency agreement, the mechanism used to shift programs to DOL. The AI bill makes ED chair of the Council and runs its practice guides through IES, so it needs that capacity.
  - **Wider reach for Sec. 104.** The omnibus raises Title IV-A to $2B a year. The AI bill's ban on industry control applies to Title IV grantees, so more Title IV-A money widens its reach.
  - **One R&D stack.** The omnibus creates the National Center for Advanced Development in Education with a $500M-a-year floor, alongside the AI bill's Regional Centers and REL expansion.
  - **Shared themes.** Both stress humanities and project-based learning.
| Omnibus item | Omnibus level | Rough current level |
| Title I-A | $55B/yr | ~$18B (tripled) |
| Perkins CTE | $4.2B/yr | ~$1.4B (tripled) |
| IDEA Part B | Ramps to the full 40% federal share by FY2033 | ~13% of cost |
| Title IV-A / 21st CCLC | $2B / $1.5B per yr | ~$1.4B / ~$1.3B |
| Universal preschool | Mandatory "such sums," FY27–33 | — |

## Political scenarios
In both scenarios, the bill's effect comes less from its money than from the rules it attaches to federal dollars and the data and definitions it creates. A deal with Trump keeps the money and guts the rules; a trifecta can keep the rules if it finds a way around the Byrd rule. Both assume a focused, AI-only tax credit and timelines updated to the date of passage.
|  | Grand bargain, 120th Congress (2027–28) | Democratic trifecta, 121st Congress (2029–30) |
| Likely enacted | Focused credit, apprenticeship and reskilling grants, micro-credentials (alongside Workforce Pell), national strategy, breach portal and parental opt-outs | Most of the bill, split across two vehicles |
| Likely cut or weakened | Most of Title III's equity framing; Title IV disclosure; humanities requirements and industry exclusions softened | Depends on filibuster rules; regulatory titles need 60 votes or a must-pass host |
| Implementation | Executive discretion defines the risk framework and occupations list; appropriations well below authorized levels | Sympathetic, but ED capacity must be rebuilt first |
| Net effect | A modest employer-friendly training bill | Standards, data and definitions for a future AI adjustment program |

**Grand bargain.** The surviving pieces are the ones with Republican appeal. An administration skeptical of the bill's premises would implement the letter of its discretionary sections, not their purpose; the "designated risk management framework" becomes whatever NIST's revised framework says. The twist is timing. Grant competitions, rulemaking and the first strategy take two to three years, so most implementation would fall to whoever takes office in 2029.
**Trifecta.** Without filibuster reform the bill splits:
  - **Reconciliation** carries the tax credit and the grant funding, converted to mandatory appropriations.
  - **Regular order** carries everything regulatory (Secs. 6, 104 and 504, and Titles IV and V), which the Byrd rule would strike. The natural host is an ESEA reauthorization, possibly combined with the omnibus, or a WIOA reauthorization.
The omnibus splits the same way but is friendlier to reconciliation: preschool is a direct appropriation, meals an entitlement, and the voucher repeal a revenue item. The likely path for both bills is spending in reconciliation and the regulatory pieces packaged as an ESEA reauthorization, with the AI bill as its AI title.
If fully enacted, the main effects would be de facto national edtech standards, audited data center disclosure that reshapes local siting fights, and a working definition of AI-affected workers. The limits: $1.5B a year is pilot scale, the Education Department may be hollowed out by 2029, and federal reskilling programs have a mixed record, though the bill's sector-based, wraparound design matches what the evidence favors.

## Industry positions and leverage
Industry support won't bridge the partisan gap, because the parts industry likes are the parts Republicans already like. Its value is as leverage: when industry needs something from Congress, the bill gives Democrats a ready list of demands.
| Segment | Likely to support | Likely to resist |
| Frontier labs | Workforce titles, occupations list, displacement data, AI literacy, NIST framework adoption, the tax credit, energy measurement standards | Little; possibly the industry exclusions |
| Edtech vendors and deployers | AI literacy grants | Sec. 104 exclusivity and control limits, Sec. 504 testing and human review, Title V risk assessments, open licensing of funded materials |
| Data center operators | Voluntary measurement standards | Title IV mandatory audited disclosure, especially with a broad federal nexus |

What industry wants from Congress, and what it could buy:
  - **Permitting and grid interconnection for data centers.** The natural trade is Title IV transparency for faster buildout: operators get speed, communities get audited disclosure and NIST-standard measurement. This climate-infrastructure bargain could plausibly clear 60 votes.
  - **Federal preemption of state AI laws.** Riskier. Democrats would trade away state protections and should demand a strong federal floor in return, such as this bill's Titles V and IV.
The current AI risk discourse also helps the bill's press. Most coverage centers on frontier and catastrophic risk, which the bill doesn't address and shouldn't claim to. It fits a different pitch: the AI risks already showing up in classrooms, paychecks and local water bills. Children's safety, entry-level hiring and data centers are the strongest hooks, and the AI bill can draw attention to the larger omnibus. Because nothing in the bill slows AI development, Republicans worried about kids and AI can engage with it without endorsing a slowdown.

## Title IV and the data center backlash
Title IV is the part of the bill most likely to move first, most likely outside this bill. Its core is oddly placed in an education and workforce bill, and it fits a bipartisan backlash that is reshaping the 2026 midterms.
**The backlash.** As of September 2026:
  - Gallup found 71% of Americans oppose data center construction in their area, including 63% of Republicans, 74% of independents and 75% of Democrats.
  - A Washington Post analysis found Republican politicians' comments on data centers turned sharply negative over the prior six months.
  - An August NRSC memo said no issue was doing more damage to Sen. Jon Husted's reelection in Ohio, and warned politicians would avoid the next project if he loses. Husted has since introduced legislation requiring data centers to cover their own electricity costs.
  - Texas Gov. Abbott paused roughly 1,800 data center projects; Pennsylvania Gov. Shapiro imposed some of the strictest rules in the country.
  - Trump remains supportive of rapid buildout and publicly called Abbott's freeze a mistake.
**What shapes a bipartisan deal.**
  - **Trump is the binding constraint.** Any 2027–28 deal needs his signature. Midterm losses and his approaching lame-duck status widen the gap between members and the White House, but the realistic path is framing data center provisions as consumer protection he can claim.
  - **Republicans engage on their own terms:** ratepayers, local control, China and children. Audited community disclosure fits those framings; the environmental justice and equity language doesn't. A bipartisan version would likely keep disclosure and NIST standards and add Husted-style ratepayer protections.
  - **The backlash makes Title IV look moderate.** As opposition moves toward moratoria, transparency and measurement become the middle ground both industry and anxious members can accept.
**Why it's oddly placed, and where it could go.** Secs. 402 and 403 fit an education and workforce bill: DOL grants train regulators and auditors, and NSF research runs in consultation with IES. Secs. 404 and 405, a disclosure mandate on private operators and NIST measurement standards, belong to Energy and Commerce and Science. They're here because this was the vehicle available.
Its best use is as ready-made language for other bills. Permitting reform, large-load interconnection rules and ratepayer protection packages next Congress will all need a transparency title, and this one is drafted, defined and paired with measurement standards. It could also become a standalone Science Committee bill built on Secs. 404 and 405. A broader bill would make disclosure a condition of the permit or interconnection approval itself, enforced by FERC, DOE or EPA, which gives it real force.

## Recommendations and open questions
The two highest-value fixes are narrowing the tax credit and pinning down the federal nexus definition before Title IV travels to other bills.
Recommendations:
  - **Narrow Sec. 208.** Require AI-skills content or a link to AI-augmented occupations, and consider a sunset. This cuts the score by roughly 80%.
  - **Define the federal nexus.** Decide which permits trigger Sec. 404 now, rather than leaving it to a conference committee.
  - **Spin off Secs. 404–405.** Offer them as a standalone Science Committee bill or a transparency title for permitting, interconnection and ratepayer packages, with permit-based enforcement.
  - **Reframe Title IV for Republicans.** Lead with ratepayer protection and local control; trade vocabulary for substance.
  - **Position the AI bill as ESEA's AI title.** Pair it with the omnibus in messaging as foundation plus AI chapter.
  - **Fix the summary documents.** Correct the annual funding periods, the "small business" credit framing, the jobs list attribution and the FTC enforcement language.
Open questions:
  - Which committees will the bills be referred to?
  - Will the office accept a narrower credit, or is economy-wide training support intended?
  - Should the Sec. 6 compliance regime get its own funding line for small grantees?
  - Would the office trade Title IV transparency for permitting reform, and on what terms?
