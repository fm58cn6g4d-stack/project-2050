# Project 2050: three consequential alternatives

Supporting design brief · October 25, 2026 · Exploratory, not adopted

Read with the [strategic assessment](project-2050-strategic-assessment-2026-10-25.md). Source: `main` at `beeba090020b7916f8ed08bcff36161fb70d1e69`. These are reviewer proposals developed under Espen's authorization to explore alternatives. They change no canonical design, eligibility, launch, model input or fiscal rule. “H”, “C” and “K” below are labels for this comparison, not new canonical policy IDs. Existing HOU, HLT, CLI and other IDs retain their meanings. The accompanying [arithmetic](strategic-review-arithmetic.md) is deliberately partial.

## H. A housing-security guarantee with a public supply capability

### The choice and the reason for it

The adopted approach combines a million new voucher awards with emergency aid, credits, repair, grants and loans. It offers a credible expansion without promising service to an entire eligible population. Its principal advantage is incremental scale through familiar institutions. Its limitation is that otherwise similar households can remain on opposite sides of a waiting list indefinitely, while many production instruments still depend on the same state and local intermediaries. HOU-3C's distinction between grants for deep affordability and repayable loans is valuable and should survive this redesign. [H1]

The alternative would establish a national entitlement to rental assistance for a defined extremely-low-income population, phased over time, and create a public development route that does not require every state to cooperate. It would replace HOU-4's fixed-award endpoint for that population and expand HOU-8/9 from optional pilots into a durable production capability. It would complement, not duplicate, the existing million awards and construction capital. Retain the AFA, meals and care policies.

An initial eligibility design for evaluation is household income at or below 30% of area median income, subject to a nationally defined poverty-based floor so low-area medians do not exclude very poor households. Assistance would cover approved rent above a household contribution around 30% of adjusted income, with a gradual exit as earnings rise. These are proposed parameters, not an assertion that the model already implements HUD eligibility. Congress would have to decide immigration-status rules, treatment of mixed-status households, disability-related expenses and income volatility. I favor protecting eligible children and residents' assistance without requiring unnecessary disclosure by other household members, with a separately priced comparison of broader coverage.

A phased statutory commitment should specify which cohorts become entitled and when. Until a cohort enters, it is not promised an immediately available lease. Within the initial tranche, prioritize severe rent burden, homelessness or imminent loss, and disability-related need, while retaining an accessible route for adults without children. Families would not lose existing assistance because their locality misses a production target. The development program's scale can respond to local constraints; an individual's right should not become a sanction against an uncooperative mayor.

### What makes the benefit usable

Use portable tenant assistance where markets can supply suitable homes. Add landlord recruitment, deposits, prompt payment, mobility counseling and accessibility support. Offer project-based assistance with public or nonprofit housing where tenant vouchers repeatedly fail, while protecting choice and a route to move. Rent reasonableness, inspection and anti-discrimination enforcement remain essential; a voucher should not become permission to subsidize unsafe housing or uncontrolled rent increases.

Create a federal housing-development sponsor able to finance or commission acquisition, rehabilitation and construction with regional public agencies, Tribes and nonprofit developers. Give it a direct application path when a state declines to participate. Use voluntary land acquisition and available public sites with credible access to jobs and services. Do not assume federal status defeats all zoning, siting or environmental constraints; federal preemption and land powers need a current, project-specific legal design. The Talarico scenario's imagined court holdings establish none of these powers.

Preserve mixed-income developments where they are useful, but make their cross-subsidy explicit. Very low rents cannot generally service full construction debt. Grants or equity, operating assistance, maintenance reserves and debt therefore need separate accounts. Do not describe the whole institution as costing only a small loan-subsidy percentage. A public sponsor's own construction and equity are not automatically federal credit loans. The existing HOU-3 loan approach can remain one instrument without dictating the accounting of every new project.

Tie the supply strategy to frequent transport, utility capacity and climate risk. Acquisition can preserve existing affordable homes sooner than new construction but does not add a dwelling; report those outcomes separately. Reward completed, habitable homes and lasting affordability rather than permits alone. Residents should have representation and grievance rights, and local participation should shape design without creating an unlimited veto for incumbent homeowners over who may live nearby.

### Scale, costs and financing

For an intermediate comparison, the new analysis adds **two million awards beyond the adopted million**, evenly over 2034–39, with half-year cost in the award year and full use thereafter. Using the repository's $15,200 FY2026 voucher-year anchor, 3% nominal annual growth and an explicit 5% administration allowance yields about **$135 billion during 2030–39 and $48 billion in FY2040**. A 25% higher unit-cost case yields about **$168 billion and $60 billion**, respectively. These are planning arithmetic, not scored costs or a forecast of lease-up. They exclude additional construction, mobility, accessible-unit and development-agency costs. The later ramp makes the ten-year total look small relative to its permanent obligation; that is precisely why maturity matters. [H2]

This tranche would bring total new awards to three million. It does not establish how many households qualify for the eventual entitlement or what that full entitlement costs. It also has no new estimated poverty effect: the existing all-proxy case covers a different population and scale and cannot be interpolated into a causal forecast for this design.

Fund continuing assistance and operations from recurring general revenue. Within D4, qualifying time-limited repair and construction investment can use a separately justified capital allocation, but the envelope is not available merely because a project is called housing. It is conditional on maturity financing, and already committed capital cannot be counted again. Compare a supported high-income/corporate package with a broader contribution and refundable protection; keep the carbon dividend and social-insurance funds intact.

Reconsider the incremental middle-income rental tax credit within HOU-2 before cutting deep rental assistance or public-housing repair. In high-cost areas middle-income households also need relief, but subsidizing their demand through another credit is less clearly distinctive than increasing well-located supply. Preserve a serious comparison of the middle-income credit against direct development; book no savings before its component cost is separated from the bundled $150 billion HOU-2 estimate.

### Fair comparison and recommendation

| Design | Main gains | Who may lose or remain excluded | Main uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adopted million-award package | Lower incremental commitment; uses familiar machinery; meaningful help to selected recipients | Eligible households outside the award allocation; unsuccessful voucher holders | Lease-up, production and future real costs |
| Housing-security guarantee plus public/nonprofit supply | Less arbitrary rationing; persistent affordability; assistance for nonparents as well as families | Taxpayers financing expansion; landlords facing stronger conditions; residents harmed by poorly sited or managed projects | National caseload, rent response, development competence and lawful direct delivery |
| Larger unrestricted cash benefit at comparable recurring cost | Recipient flexibility; simpler payment; help with needs beyond rent | Those whose rents absorb the transfer or who cannot find a home; nonrecipient households facing higher rents | Local price effects and how the transfer is targeted |

I prefer the second design, with cash as a genuine comparator rather than a straw alternative. HUD's Family Options experiment gives sustained housing subsidies a strong evidentiary basis for shelter-entering families. It does not prove vouchers dominate cash for all low-income households. A production institution can also fail through political allocation, cost escalation or poor maintenance. That is why the decision paper needs at least one tight-market and one weak-market case, transparent lifecycle costs and a credible fallback to expanded assistance through existing sponsors. [H3]

The proposal's benefits would likely be concentrated among low-income renters, including many women, disabled people and families facing racial disparities in access. Homeowners outside the eligible population would not receive the same direct benefit. Broad tax financing therefore needs a whole-household comparison, not a statement that every housing row is a benefit. National distribution and race/disability effects remain to be measured.

**What could change my recommendation:** evidence that the public development route adds little supply at materially greater lifecycle cost; an entitlement caseload that cannot be financed without displacing more valuable protections; or evidence that a cash design produces comparable housing stability with fewer restrictions. A difficult first-year lease-up rate would instead favor stronger supply and service design, not necessarily abandoning the entitlement principle.

## C. Basic home support and protection against intensive-care costs

### Why the current approach may be too small

The current design increases Medicaid HCBS resources, supports providers and workers, and now explicitly includes targeted grants alongside enhanced matching. Those choices respond to real bottlenecks and remain the best immediate base. But a Medicaid expansion leaves substantial dependence on means tests, state administration and unpaid relatives. The chosen HLT-4 bundle is a down payment, not universal long-term-care insurance. [C1]

A more ambitious alternative would establish an **all-age basic home-support benefit**, funded collectively, without requiring asset depletion. For comparison, use a needs threshold of assistance with at least two activities of daily living expected to last at least 90 days, or substantial supervision because of severe cognitive impairment. This resembles a threshold examined in ASPE's research; it is a starting proposal, not a complete disability assessment. It would exclude some people with substantial instrumental-support needs and must be compared with a broader functional standard. [C2]

The initial benefit would cover assessed assistance **up to twenty hours a week**, with actual support based on need, not a uniform cash award. Recipients could choose qualified agency support, an independent worker or a paid family caregiver under equivalent pay and safeguarding rules. A fiscal intermediary would handle employment administration where needed. Protect recipient control, accessible communication and independent appeal; do not assume a relative can safely or willingly provide care. No employment-history requirement should exclude disabled people who never entered paid work.

Twenty hours is not complete care. Preserve stronger Medicaid entitlements and existing services. Develop a second, separately scored tier covering approved additional home or residential support after twelve months of continuous substantial need, with earlier protection for people unable to meet costs. The waiting period, cost sharing and whether the intensive tier has a spending ceiling are consequential choices to compare, not details to conceal in drafting. Avoid favoring an institution merely because home support is capped. Do not call the basic tier “catastrophic protection” if it cannot cover prolonged high needs.

### Financing and supply are part of the policy

The basic tier could use mandatory general revenue, while a broader insured intensive tier could have a dedicated contribution if its revenue, reserve and benefit obligations are genuinely linked. A dedicated label does not make it solvent. An uncapped earnings contribution is administratively intelligible but burdens labor and would stack with leave, Medicare and any Social Security increase. A combined earnings and capital-income design would share the burden more broadly but must respect revenues already assigned to HI, SS and the general fund. No rate is defensible before a caseload and utilization estimate.

The scale illustration is intentionally simple: two million users receiving twenty hours weekly at a loaded cost of $30 an hour require **$62.4 billion a year**; five million at $35 require **$182 billion**. Both are in constant 2026 dollars, with assumed users and prices. They are not a national low/high forecast, and they exclude intensive care, assessment, administration and capital. They cannot be subtracted directly from the nominal FY2040 financing space. [C3]

A federal score would separate additional care from payment for previously unpaid care and substitution for existing public or private spending. Some Medicaid spending might move to the new benefit, but its federal and state shares differ. Freed state funds are not automatically federal savings, and any maintenance-of-effort arrangement has costs and enforcement requirements. Do not book hospital savings or increased caregiver earnings as financing before the evidence and scoring rules support them.

Pair the benefit with predictable provider rates sufficient for the chosen wage standard, portable training and credentials, collective worker voice, immigration pathways, accessible housing and respite. Use existing administration where possible, with a federal fallback if a state refuses. A national entitlement should not require every state to build a new insurance system. Workforce entry takes time; financing should begin capacity development before large service cohorts enter, without converting inability to find a worker into a permanent forfeiture of the promised support.

### Three defensible choices

| Design | What it does best | What it leaves unresolved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strengthened Medicaid HCBS plus grants | Targets public resources and builds on an existing payer; can move sooner | Asset and income eligibility, state variation, unmet needs outside Medicaid |
| Basic home support plus intensive-care protection | Supports daily independence and covers a wider range of family circumstances; makes the charter's promise more concrete | Large recurring financing, assessment and workforce expansion; transition from existing benefits |
| Catastrophic insurance only | Pools long-duration financial risk with less public involvement in short episodes | Families still bear early care costs and work disruption; waiting periods can be harsh for those without savings |

I favor developing the second design, while retaining Medicaid improvement as the immediate route. A catastrophic-only program is a serious alternative if the broader benefit cannot be sustainably staffed and funded. A small lifetime cash account would be easier to bound financially but should be described as assistance, not insurance against the longest care episodes.

The strongest distributional case is independence for disabled and older people and relief for unpaid caregivers, who often sacrifice earnings. Some benefits would also go to households with substantial assets. That is a deliberate departure from spend-down-based protection, which needs progressive financing and an explicit public justification. Paying relatives can recognize undervalued work; poorly designed, it can also entrench expectations that women remain out of paid employment. Offer a real outside-care alternative and track recipient choice, unmet hours, caregiver strain and worker retention rather than counting authorized budgets alone.

**What could change my recommendation:** evidence that a universal basic tier crowds out higher-need support at plausible revenue levels; an assessment process that produces unmanageable exclusion or gaming; or a superior way to provide first-dollar help while pooling catastrophic risk. Those questions justify a bounded care-financing inquiry, not promising a national launch date now.

## K. A climate delivery and adaptation compact

### What changes and what remains

Retain the adopted carbon fee/dividend and the principle that households with little tax liability can obtain clean-energy support. Retain statutory clean-power and vehicle standards. Change the priority of marginal spending and institutional development: make a transmission delivery backstop and adaptation central, with selective procurement for difficult industrial transitions. This elevates parts of CLI-9/10 and strengthens CLI-7/8; it does not adopt the full durability-session option bundle. [K1]

For transmission, give an existing or new public sponsor a duty to deliver independently established regional needs when a competitively selected private or regional developer fails to advance an approved project. Start with planning and development rights, voluntary agreements and a small set of consequential corridors; select public build, co-ownership or competitively procured capacity according to lifecycle cost and execution ability. Do not assume TVA's or a power marketing administration's authority, territory or staff can simply be extended by administrative choice. New authority and siting powers require legislation and legal review.

Use transparent regional planning, beneficiary cost allocation, reliability requirements and public oversight. Indigenous rights and Tribal sovereignty require substantive treatment, not a consultation box. Community compensation and route changes should address harms; neither unlimited delay nor unlimited federal discretion is an adequate rule. Training and procurement must recognize competition with housing for skilled trades and equipment. National service cannot instantly supply experienced lineworkers, electricians or project managers.

For difficult industrial projects, compare contracts for verified low-carbon output or advance purchase commitments with tax credits. Use competitive awards, emissions measurement, delivery milestones and repayment or loss-sharing terms. Separate a useful industrial-demand guarantee from a preselected reactor-fleet order. I would not commit now to the source scenario's twenty-to-thirty-reactor purchase: technology, schedule, cost and alternatives need an actual procurement comparison. Long contracts can create capacity and lock in mistakes. Their full expected cost and termination exposure must remain visible.

### Adaptation as a continuing public responsibility

Establish predictable formula funding based on hazard, vulnerability and fiscal capacity for cooling, smoke protection, water reliability, resilient public facilities and relocation assistance. Require plans to distinguish protection in place from voluntary movement where risks cannot reasonably be reduced. Reserve a direct federal or Tribal route where a state refuses to act. Integrate housing acquisition and affordable receiving-area development; otherwise relocation payments can simply move a family from one unaffordable market to another.

Insurance support should protect people while allowing risk information to affect land use. Compare means-tested premium assistance and mitigation with broad public reinsurance. The latter can socialize losses for high-value properties and encourage repeated rebuilding. If used, cap exposure, price and disclose the contingent liability, condition rebuilding support on risk reduction, and give tenants and displaced people assistance independent of a property's insured value. A person should not need to own a home to receive help surviving a disaster.

The standing program needs both capital and operations. Cooling centers without staff, restored wetlands without maintenance and relocation without support are incomplete investments. Where permanent obligations arise, D4 requires recurring funding; a “resilience investment” label cannot turn all of them into borrowable capital.

### Evidence, finance and the alternative

The existing model's grid option raises 2035 clean electricity from 76% to 82%, while road-fuel carbon convergence adds comparatively little emissions reduction within its assumptions. This supports testing delivery before further increasing prices. It does not show that a public corporation has a particular benefit-cost ratio. The model represents grid expansion through parameters, not individual projects, litigation or labor markets. DOE's 2026 draft-study announcement offers independent support for the importance of transmission under rising load, without resolving those delivery choices. [K2]

Compare two coherent portfolios on a common baseline: the adopted credit-restoration portfolio and a version that redirects part of marginal extensions toward transmission, low-income building upgrades and clean-industry procurement, with adaptation funded explicitly. Preserve already necessary support for near-term deployment and avoid retroactive contract disruption. The $0.90 trillion CLI-1 midpoint is not an available pot to raid: it includes restoration and expansions with different effects. The model's credit accounting also covers a different set of provisions. No dollar-for-dollar swap is priced in this brief.

Capital financing may combine user charges, federal equity, grants and loans. Federal lending incurs credit risk and administrative cost; owning an asset incurs construction and operating exposure. No unsupported generic five-percent subsidy rate establishes the price of a public builder. Recurring adaptation assistance needs general revenue or another durable source; falling emissions should not destabilize it, and the existing transition share is already committed. Do not finance a new dividend and permanent services with the same receipts.

The adopted approach is simpler to launch and lets many decentralized actors decide what to build. Its weakness is that credits cannot by themselves resolve coordination failures or provide a fair response to unavoidable loss. The alternative could build scarce common infrastructure and make climate policy useful to people who cannot buy a new car or rooftop solar. It adds public execution risk, contentious siting and fiscal liabilities. Retain both models of provision and choose where public responsibility is indispensable.

**What could change my recommendation:** regional evidence that private delivery can meet the required schedule at lower public exposure; weak additionality of procurement; or an adaptation allocation that mainly preserves property wealth rather than reducing vulnerability. A serious comparison should also examine a fee-repeal or hostile-administration case. Statutory authority and a signed contract do not make execution immune to political change.

## What these alternatives imply together

These proposals are complements in purpose and competitors for resources. Housing construction, transmission and care expansion need different skills but share public administrative capacity and some construction labor. Cheap energy can reduce housing and care-provider costs; badly allocated grid charges can increase them. Better care can let people work or move, but those gains are neither immediate nor guaranteed revenue. A housing guarantee can protect a family during a climate relocation, while poorly located subsidized development can create new climate exposure.

Do not sum the broad poverty proxy, the grid option and the fiscal stress test into a package result. They have different populations, baselines, assumptions and coverage. The financing-space exercise includes only the explicit downside and the illustrative housing tranche. It establishes no room for a complete care guarantee or climate compact.

The recommended decision is to pursue this direction through a housing-security comparison first, preserve the adopted social floor, and make the larger care commitment an explicit subsequent choice. If Espen prefers to hold the present revenue architecture and overall scale much closer to the current agenda, the honest alternative is to strengthen delivery and resilience selectively and state that universal housing security and dependable all-age care remain aspirations. Both are coherent positions. Promising the larger settlement while funding only the smaller one is not.
