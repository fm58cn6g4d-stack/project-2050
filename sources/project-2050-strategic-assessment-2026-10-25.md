# Project 2050: make freedom dependable

Independent strategic assessment · October 25, 2026 · Prepared for Espen's review

**October 26 follow-up.** Espen affirmed a secure life for all and emphasized that the state must also provide practical routes into work, education, training and social participation, with accommodations and without punitive work requirements. The [development response](fiscal-redesign-and-participation-2026-10-26.md) develops that complementary purpose and begins the requested wholesale fiscal review, with a new climate-portfolio screen. Financing redesign is now the lead assignment; the housing-first sequence recommended below records the original assessment. The response adopts no numerical or policy alternative.

**Recommendation.** Keep the project's ambition and most of its redistributive and democratic core. Reorganize its next development around the institutions that make a secure life possible: homes people can obtain, care they can rely on, a clean and resilient physical environment, and power they can exercise at work and in public. Develop a larger housing commitment first. Make dependable long-term care the next major expansion to evaluate. Elevate public grid delivery and adaptation within climate policy. Finance this direction as a durable settlement, rather than successively finding the last tax needed to make a midpoint balance.

## What the project could become

Project 2050's strongest idea is that freedom requires both material security and the ability to influence the institutions on which we depend. A parent needs income, a home and care arrangements that survive an ordinary disruption. A disabled person needs control over assistance, not merely an insurance card. A worker needs somewhere else to go and a voice where they are. Citizens need a government that can act and ways to challenge it when it acts badly. The charter understands these relationships unusually well. [1]

The strongest public project would make that argument concrete and contestable: **an account of how a democratic society can make a good life dependable through profound change**. That gives Project 2050 a more distinctive purpose than assembling a comprehensive progressive platform or demonstrating that a hypothetical administration could pass many bills. It also sets a harder standard. Passing a law, protecting a payment and reducing a modeled poverty rate are important achievements, but none by itself establishes dependable freedom.

The current agenda is ambitious in fiscal redistribution and institutional repair. It is less developed on who supplies essential services, who bears risks that households cannot reasonably manage, and who owns or governs the gains from technological change. Its public vision sometimes reaches beyond what its selected instruments would reliably provide. This is a reason to strengthen the program, not retreat to less demanding prose.

There is also a tension within the project's human purposes worth addressing directly. A “fair shot” can sound like a fairer competition for jobs, credentials and scarce homes. Many of the charter's strongest examples demand more: a decent life for people who cannot compete, cannot work, do not form a conventional family or choose forms of contribution that markets poorly reward. I would make **security without a test of market usefulness** explicit. Children, disabled people, caregivers and older people are not exceptions to the project's account of freedom. They help define it.

That does not require equalizing every outcome or making government responsible for every disappointment. It requires distinguishing a guaranteed foundation from opportunities people pursue beyond it. The project should retain pluralism about the good life, including the right to reject its preferred community scene, family arrangement or form of participation.

Security should also make participation possible. Cash without a usable route into learning, good work or community life can leave people isolated. The state has a positive role in supplying those routes, including accommodations, practical support and real opportunities with employers. Neither an assumption that everyone can work nor an assumption that disability prevents participation is adequate. The October 26 follow-up makes this responsibility explicit while preserving the unconditional human claim behind the basic floor.

## What deserves to survive a serious reconsideration

The AFA credit, universal school meals, affordable child care and paid leave are a persuasive foundation. Monthly refundable support reaches people whom deductions cannot help. Universal meals reduce the administrative and social burden of proving need. Child care and leave address time as well as money. The recent decision to preserve the AFA's purchasing power and expand vouchers is a substantive improvement, not something to reverse simply because it made financing harder. The case for this foundation does not depend on claiming that it eradicates child poverty. [2–4]

Retain health coverage restoration, a public option, Medicare benefit repair and an attack on excessive prices. The go-big design's decision to exclude an age-based Medicare expansion from the base has a defensible rationale: the source estimated substantial enrollment but comparatively few newly insured people at age 60. I would not displace restoration or care investment with that expansion now. Keep dental on its adopted path; postponing it beyond a budget window does not fund a permanent program. The newer current-coverage-restoration clarification is also sensible: facilitating coverage today is different from reopening years of medical claims. [2,5]

Keep labor and competition reform near the center. Collective bargaining, first-contract enforcement, non-compete restrictions and constraints on dominant platforms change bargaining relationships. They should not be judged merely by their direct federal cost or whether they enter the child-poverty model. A program that redistributes income while leaving workers and tenants dependent on unanswerable private power would be less transformative than the current agenda. [6]

Preserve majority-capable legislating with minority rights, voting access, civil-rights protection, executive constraints and the capacity of Congress to oversee government. The rationale for graduated cloture is more persuasive than the old precise floor-day estimates: it allows decision while guaranteeing a bounded opportunity to contest and amend. Keep D2-F as the working route, but treat the guarantees as commitments owed to opponents, not tactical concessions. The fiscal channel discipline, no-double-counting rules and refusal to spend hypothetical growth revenues are similarly worth preserving. [2,7]

Finally, the public prototype is a real advance. It starts with recognizable purposes, connects policies, and gives readers a route into evidence. Its principles-led direction should continue. The weakness to address is the narrowness of the experience it currently depicts, rather than its choice to put detailed funding analysis in a companion. [8]

## Three changes large enough to matter

### Make housing security a defining commitment

The adopted housing package has useful pieces: emergency assistance, a million additional vouchers, repair, zoning incentives, revolving funds and federal loans. HOU-3C was chosen for good reasons. Grants support very low rents that debt cannot carry, while loans recycle capital and use existing state underwriting capacity. Keep that distinction. But a million awards is a supply allocation, not a principle describing who should be secure. And the same state and local channels are asked to deliver much of the assistance, lending and construction. More money through those channels does not resolve refusal, weak administration or the absence of viable sites. [3]

I recommend developing a **housing-security guarantee for extremely-low-income renters**, phased alongside a national public and nonprofit development capability. The guarantee should eventually replace waiting-list luck for its defined eligible population. The development capability should acquire and repair existing housing as well as build, retain affordable ownership, and have a lawful direct route where state participation fails. It need not be a giant new federal construction workforce. Regional public and nonprofit sponsors can deliver projects under federal financing and accountability, with a federal sponsor available as a backstop.

The proposal builds on HOU-4C, HOU-8 and HOU-9 but changes their relationship: permanent affordability and production become part of the same institutional commitment. A first expansion tranche of two million additional awards beyond the adopted million is an illustrative scale for comparison, not the estimated size of an entitlement. The supporting brief explains eligibility, tenant protections, capital, state refusal and the limits of direct federal action.

The existing poverty research makes this worth prioritizing. With the same enhanced-delivery assumptions, the annual AFA analogue and one million awards yield 8.34% child poverty; the broad 6.76-million-award proxy case yields 6.32%, and deep poverty falls from 1.85% to 1.16%. Those are conditional, partial-agenda comparisons on a fixed reference population. The larger proxy pool is not statutory eligibility, proven lease capacity or a scored program. It also does not estimate the effect of my two-million increment. But it is a consequential signal about an unmet need, not a marginal optimization. HUD's randomized Family Options Study separately supports the value of sustained rental assistance for families entering shelters; its findings do not establish the effects of a national entitlement on rents or construction. [4,15]

Why choose this before simply raising the child credit again? Housing security benefits children and adults without children, addresses a major source of instability, and can connect anti-poverty policy with land, transport, climate preparation and public ownership. Cash remains essential. The question is whether part of the next dollar should make a home obtainable and secure, rather than compensate households for an increasingly expensive housing system. My answer is yes, subject to a credible supply and financing design.

### Give dependable care an institutional home

The largest gap between the public vision and the chosen agenda is long-term care. HLT-4 contains an HCBS down payment, workforce support and safety-net expansion; the newer specifications add enhanced matching and targeted grants. These are valuable. They do not constitute a dependable, nationwide care entitlement for everyone who needs help living independently. Improving Medicare's acute-care benefits does not fill that gap. [5]

Develop an **all-age home-support benefit with protection against prolonged intensive-care costs**. A concrete comparison design would offer assessed basic assistance without an asset-spend-down requirement, allow a paid family caregiver as a genuine option, and preserve stronger existing Medicaid services. More intensive and residential support would require a separate entitlement and financing specification; a modest home-support budget must not be advertised as complete long-term-care insurance. The alternatives brief compares this approach with enhanced Medicaid and with catastrophic insurance alone.

This would shift some costs now borne through unpaid time and family depletion onto a visible collective budget. That is partly the purpose, not an accounting defect. It could improve independence, caregiver freedom and job quality. It could also drive demand faster than a workforce can be built, pay for some care families would otherwise provide, and create a large permanent fiscal commitment. Those costs should be acknowledged before claiming that prevention, labor supply or fewer hospitalizations make the benefit self-financing. ASPE's 2022 microsimulation illustrates both concentrated long-duration risk and substantial unpaid care; it is not a score for this proposal. [16]

I would put this ahead of an additional Medicare age expansion and ahead of a new general AI cash dividend. Neither is intrinsically mistaken. Care is already a defining charter purpose with a clear institutional gap. The country should not have to wait for a particular AI productivity scenario before deciding who will support people with substantial needs.

Two existing social-insurance commitments also need substantive reconsideration. The dedicated-funds model shows that paid leave's high-take-up cost exceeds the 0.85% contribution cap; its Treasury loan rises to $865 billion by 2050 rather than being repaid. A permanently growing loan is not a temporary bridge. Keep twelve weeks of leave, but compare an adequate contribution rule and an explicitly financed subsidy instead of retaining a cap that fails under sustained demand. Likewise, D8 plus SS-5 still leaves a modeled 75-year Social Security deficit, before resolving provision interactions. Develop a full solvency package, including a phased broad rate increase as a serious comparator, while retaining low-income benefit protection. “Solvency first” should mean completed solvency, not a later depletion date. [9]

### Move climate policy from restoration toward delivery and adaptation

The climate package contains more than tax credits: a fee and dividend, standards, grid rules and disaster reforms. Yet its most developed spending commitment restores the credit architecture, while public construction remains optional and major adaptation costs are unpriced. The balance deserves to change. [10]

The project's own model supplies a useful reason. Its selected package reaches 76% clean electricity in 2035 against the 80% policy target; the modeled public-grid option reaches 82%. Adding the road-fuel carbon price produces comparatively little additional emissions reduction within this model. Even the selected package reaches only about 64% below 2005 net greenhouse gases in 2050. None of those numbers is a national forecast or a definitive estimate of the value of a legal institution. They indicate that a stronger price alone is unlikely to be the most useful next design question. [11]

I recommend elevating a **public grid-delivery backstop, targeted clean-industry procurement and a standing adaptation commitment** into the main alternative to blanket restoration-and-extension. Retain the fee, dividend, clean-power standard and support that makes household electrification accessible. Compare whether marginal credit dollars would do more in transmission, affordable building retrofits and industrial projects whose main obstacle is coordination or first-project risk. Do not cancel credits indiscriminately: predictable support has value, and grid construction also needs projects ready to connect.

Adaptation should cover more than disaster recovery: affordable cooling, water and smoke protection, resilient public facilities, and the option to relocate without losing one's community or being displaced without remedy. Avoid turning an insurance backstop into an open-ended subsidy for rebuilding expensive assets in escalating danger. Renters, people with disabilities and Tribal governments need distinct routes and rights. DOE's July 2026 draft-study announcement independently identifies load growth and transmission constraints; it strengthens the case for examining delivery but does not validate this project's buildout assumptions or select a federal corporation. [17]

This is a major redesign of priorities within the climate portfolio. It is not a claim that all three new commitments can be financed by redirecting a fixed amount from existing credits. Their common-baseline costs, overlaps and recurring obligations remain to be established.

## Finance the ambition, and expose its real tradeoffs

The current $53.7 billion FY2040 general-fund primary gap is manageable in relation to the national economy. It is also a misleading measure of how much financing work remains. Important savings and receipts are uncertain, some costs are missing, and the agenda's new aspirations could be much larger than the residual. Repeatedly adding a tax until the central path barely passes encourages a fragile program. [12]

I tested a limited joint downside using existing ranges: HLT-6's general-fund savings fall by $350 billion over 2030–39, G8 enforcement receipts by $200 billion, and C39 receipts by $150 billion. Following their existing annual profiles raises the FY2040 gap to **$153.8 billion, or 0.284% of modeled GDP**. The earlier corporate-31% illustration then leaves **$84.7 billion** uncovered. A mixed corporate-29.5% and additional 0.35-point payroll illustration leaves **$51.0 billion**. These are deliberate simultaneous low cases, without assigned probabilities; they exclude the related HI risk and unpriced additions. They are not an official score or a forecast of tax failure. [13]

The lesson is to compare coherent financing architectures, including their distribution and political meaning:

| Architecture | Strongest case | Main cost or weakness | My judgment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extend the present top-end/corporate strategy | Makes concentrated gains contribute; protects most workers from a new broad tax | Interacting tax bases, legal exposure and uncertain health offsets; an extra rate point is not an independent revenue stream | Retain as the core, but stop treating a central estimate as sufficient security |
| Top-end core plus a broad contribution with explicit household protection | Supports larger recurring services and makes their collective financing visible | Workers pay, including those without children; payroll alone is exposed to weak wage growth and AI-driven changes in income | Develop alongside the first route; do not select a rate from the present proxy model |
| A broader consumption base with a refundable household credit | Provides a substantially different base if a much larger social settlement is chosen | Price-level transition, difficult administration, retiree and low-income burdens, and a large gross-to-net rebate wedge | Worth a genuine comparator if care scale warrants it, not a convenient new line in the current ledger |

The third route is not a recommendation to adopt a VAT now. It means broad license should produce an actual architectural comparison, rather than restricting the discussion to the three earlier family-financing examples. Likewise, public equity may share future upside but is not a dependable pay-for for near-term entitlements. Carbon dividends, HI reserves and already committed pass-through revenues cannot be spent twice.

As a scale test, a new **net** recurring revenue stream reaching 0.5% of GDP in 2034 would leave about $117 billion of FY2040 space after the modeled joint downside. That is a required yield, not an identified tax, and compensation must come out before calling it net. My illustrative two-million housing tranche uses about $48–60 billion of that FY2040 space. Care and climate are not included. This shows that a larger ambition is economically discussable, while also showing why a new care guarantee cannot be promised from a small residual pay-for. [13]

Retain D4's discipline: finance permanent commitments, count capital and contingent obligations honestly, and keep social-insurance channels whole. Its original rationale for relative debt guardrails remains persuasive; the project need not solve every inherited fiscal imbalance before improving lives. Add an explicit comparison of central and joint-downside financing and the consequences of failure. Do not convert that into an automatic austerity trigger during a recession or a rule that suspends benefits while Congress searches for revenue. [7]

Retain D3's progressive purpose, but strengthen its interpretation. A positive quintile mean cannot support the claim that each middle-income household gains. Insurance has value even in a year without a claim, but that value should not be disguised as spendable cash. Examine annual cash flow, access to services and protection against risk separately, especially for childless workers, retirees, disabled people and people facing several taxes before benefits arrive. The choice is whether to protect every household mechanically, which can be very expensive and entrench inequalities, or adopt a distributional standard with explicit protection for vulnerable losers. That judgment remains Espen's. [2,12]

## Power, uncertainty and the parts the models do not settle

The project should be more demanding about democratic legitimacy than about the survival of its own coalition. D1 explicitly stipulates favorable political control. That is useful for an enactment case, but not a sufficient organizing assumption for a 2050 project. Develop a companion strategy in which power changes hands in 2031 or 2033, state cooperation is uneven, and no second trifecta arrives on schedule. Judge reforms by whether they remain worthwhile under those conditions, not whether they reliably preserve Democratic control. This does not itself move a bill out of the adopted first-trifecta window. [1,2]

I would **remove CRT-16's automatic expansion response to judicial invalidation of term limits**. I read the original deterrence rationale and its acknowledged coercion problem. Making the trigger a court ruling does not cure the reciprocity problem: it makes a change in the Court's size contingent on the Court reaching the government's preferred legal conclusion. It can also bind Congress to escalation when a different response would be wiser. Retain prospective term-limit development, the amendment route, ethics, neutral assignment, adequate judgeships and a fresh democratic decision about any later structural response. The cost is losing a threatened deterrent; the benefit is a more credible commitment to independent judgment and reciprocal rules. This is a normative recommendation, not a claim that the proposal's legality has been resolved. [14]

I would also **replace AI-attributable displacement as the gateway to worker support**. AI-4 currently uses a BLS occupation-region trigger, not an individual requirement to prove AI caused a dismissal. That is a sensible way to reduce individual burden, but help still depends on an agency distinguishing AI displacement from outsourcing, demand or managerial reorganization. Workers with similar losses outside a triggered cell can remain excluded. Build general unemployment and earnings-loss protection, short-time compensation and access to retraining around observable loss and job quality. Keep AI-specific measurement for oversight, along with pre-deployment evaluation, workplace notice, collective consultation and appeal rights. Wage insurance should not become a subsidy to low-paying successor employers, and training should not substitute for a shortage of good jobs. This redirects AI-4's existing development budget for comparison; it does not establish that a broader entitlement fits its $0.2 trillion working estimate. [6]

The wider agenda needs emphasis rather than a new policy for every problem. The health, care, housing and energy programs compete for workers; immigration and apprenticeships are part of their capacity strategy, not unrelated agenda items. Education needs attention to learning, disability inclusion and transitions into adult life, not only federal grant levels. Public safety's concern for victims and prevention is worth keeping, but clearance targets need protection against coercion, wrongful accusation and data manipulation. Transportation should be assessed by access to jobs and services and the continuing ability to operate them, not only capital authorized or user-fee coverage. International climate cooperation, pandemics and nuclear risk deserve more weight in a long-horizon account than a mainly domestic fiscal ledger gives them. I would not claim a defense dividend before specifying the commitments, force posture and transition that produce it. [6,10,18]

Across these areas, the largest missing perspective is **what people can do when delivery fails**. Automatic enrollment and uninterrupted payments are excellent defaults. They must coexist with correction, privacy, accessible human help, collective representation and remedies that do not require a successful federal lawsuit. A single data infrastructure can reduce burden and create a single point of exclusion or surveillance. Participation should let people change priorities, not merely explain why a preselected project is good for them.

The analytical foundation is valuable precisely because some outputs complicate the story. Poverty remains substantial; the grid binds; Social Security remains short of sustainable solvency; high-take-up leave loans grow; financing fails after the family adoption. The next use of models should be to challenge choices, not accumulate a more impressive dashboard.

Three cautions matter particularly. First, the Talarico scenario is an authored future narrative with internal numbers, not empirical evidence that a legislative sequence causes a particular seat loss, that a federal builder necessarily succeeds, or that a bipartisan vote ensures durability. It can generate hypotheses. It cannot validate them. Second, smoothly calibrated fiscal profiles inherit the weaknesses of ten-year estimates: the G11 profile explicitly flattens transition receipts, for example. A precise maturity balance is not necessarily a precise estimate of permanent revenue. Third, the poverty and carbon models cover mechanisms unevenly; an unmodeled benefit is not worthless, and a modeled gain is not the whole outcome. [4,9,11–14]

## A public project that can earn trust

Keep the current front door. The public project should begin with what people could gain, not its remaining research tasks. But its next major editorial piece should test its promise in a place where parts of the agenda conflict: a renter in a flood-prone town whose landlord benefits from rebuilding, a disabled adult who wants control over support, or a worker whose community depends on a facility that must close. Let readers see what the current package does, what a proposed alternative adds, and which difficult choice remains. This gives uncertainty a human meaning rather than relegating it to a disclaimer. [8]

I would publish a small number of substantial, illustrated essays linked to public evidence casebooks before building a larger personalized explorer. An essay can make an argument, acknowledge objections and offer a usable contribution to an existing policy debate. A calculator risks implying knowledge of eligibility and household outcomes that the current models do not have. The prototype can organize those essays without becoming a simulation of the whole country.

The most credible posture is that of a public project willing to revise its own proposals. Show a serious alternative alongside the preferred one; identify who loses as well as who gains; distinguish a fiscal transfer from a social cost; and explain what evidence would change the recommendation. Invite eventual challenge from people who use and deliver the services, as well as specialists. This review authorizes no contact. Accessible evidence, human comprehension testing and release approval remain necessary before publication, but a complete government operating manual does not.

## The judgments to make, and the next assignment

The strategic choice is between a better-financed version of the existing restorative agenda and a broader settlement in which housing, care and resilience are defining commitments. I recommend the latter direction, developed in stages rather than adopted as an unpriced bundle. It preserves the existing floor of benefits and rights while being willing to replace instruments that cannot plausibly fulfill the public promise.

The principal decisions are whether housing assistance should become a right for a defined low-income population rather than a fixed number of awards; whether care should remain primarily a Medicaid expansion or become a broader social guarantee; and whether the larger settlement justifies broad contributions alongside substantial taxation of wealth and corporate income. These decisions concern the kind of society the project proposes, not just its financing gap. The governance recommendations—reciprocity without an automatic court-size threat, support without AI-causation tests, and a real adverse-control case—can be assessed on their own merits.

**The next substantial assignment I would choose is a housing-security compact decision paper.** Compare the adopted million-award package, a phased extremely-low-income assistance guarantee with public/nonprofit supply, and a larger cash-first alternative at comparable recurring cost. Specify who becomes eligible, how many additional households could actually be served, how assistance and rents interact, how state refusal is handled, what is built or acquired, and which recurrent revenue architecture can support it under a joint downside. Use the existing poverty work for its valid comparisons and add evidence on lease-up and production; do not build a whole-economy forecast. Produce one preferred design, a credible fallback, a financing range and a public explanation that exposes the choice. Stop before legislative operating manuals or adoption.

Housing comes first because it combines a defining outcome, a strong existing analytical foothold, and an instrument question that reaches well beyond raising a benefit. Care is the next major design inquiry, not an extra task to launch simultaneously. A bounded climate-delivery decision can follow using the existing carbon model and a regional infrastructure case. This ordering follows the potential to change the agenda, rather than the order of the current queue.
