## The direction I recommend

Project 2050 should offer a recovery that people can experience, followed by a durable settlement about what government guarantees and how the country pays for it. That requires a different fiscal argument from “we found enough offsets for the legislative package.” The present rules and models are useful disciplines, but paying for the agenda at maturity does not stabilize the inherited federal budget. Nor does a government-wide surplus in one part of the plan make Social Security, Medicare or education solvent.

My preferred direction has three parts. Establish the basic floor and restore delivery first. Finance education through a visible payroll contribution **and an explicit general-fund share**, rather than force the whole expansion onto wages. Then enact a long-term revenue and social-insurance settlement, with later broad contributions conditioned on funded household protection and reliable services. The decisions should be enacted together wherever feasible, even when their effective dates differ. A promise to find the rest of the money in a future Congress is not a financing plan.

The central finding is encouraging but limited. Full EDU-9B can move $2.0 trillion of existing agenda and current-law costs out of their former general-fund accounts during 2030–39. That does **not** free $2.0 trillion for other uses. Under the mixed-financing illustration developed here, transition and continuing grants, an allowance for tax interactions and a household-protection reserve reduce net general-fund relief to about **$352 billion**. Government-wide improvement is about **$526 billion**, from the new contribution less additional benefits and allowances. Both are meaningful. Neither is permission to spend the gross transfer again.

The review also changes my confidence in an exclusively payroll-financed education design. It can produce strong fund balances, but it uses wage capacity that Social Security may also need and exposes households without children to visible charges. A public education institution has an intergenerational contribution rationale; its broader civic and economic purposes also justify progressive general revenue. That is a substantive reason for mixed financing, not merely a device to make a lower contribution look adequate.

## Recovery must include receipts, not just announcements

“Recovery” should describe the first phase of governing, not a euphemism for delayed relief or an assertion that a recession will exist in 2029. Its purpose is to repair lost coverage, damaged administrative capacity and household insecurity while making the longer reforms possible. The public explanation can be straightforward:

> Slower inflation does not undo the increases families have already absorbed. Recovery means making a decent life affordable again: reliable income, lower essential expenses, and services people can actually get. Some improvements can arrive quickly. Others require homes, trained workers and infrastructure, and we will explain the stages and show the results.

Lower inflation means prices rise more slowly; it does not automatically restore the old overall price level. Some prices can fall, especially when a specific shortage, restriction or excessive charge is addressed. The objective should therefore be greater purchasing power and lower essential burdens, not a promise of economy-wide deflation. Avoid telling a household that it is mistaken about its grocery bill because aggregate inflation has improved. [S1]

For 2029–30, prioritize instruments that can use existing delivery systems: the AFA's monthly income support once authorized and operational, current-coverage restoration and facilitated reinstatement, meals and Summer EBT, actual voucher leases, and administrative repair that gets claims and applications resolved. The existing health work identifies component-level opportunities in 2029; it does not establish a nationwide early launch. Keep its distinctions between authority, enrollment, usable coverage and first receipt. The fiscal model still begins most program cash flows in FY2030. **FY2029 acceleration and administration are not priced by this review**, so an early-recovery budget must fund them explicitly before a new effective date is promised. [S2]

For 2031–34, the visible transformation should increasingly come from additional care places, lower child-care bills, functioning health benefits, more rental assistance that can actually be used, and supported routes into work or education. The labor strategy belongs inside this phase: employers with real vacancies, paid training, accommodations, transport and care support, and a named public institution responsible when referrals fail. The decent-life floor remains unconditional; participation services are an additional obligation of government.

For the longer term, the institutional promise is that these gains can survive a recession, a change of administration and demographic change. Education, care, housing and grid policy all require workers, and competing public expansions can bid against each other for scarce staff. Finance pay and training together; expand physical capacity alongside subsidies; and use procurement and reimbursement to improve productivity without assuming that labor-intensive care can shed staff indefinitely. Redistribution can improve affordability for recipients before it increases supply, but a transfer alone does not create another apartment or qualified worker.

The public recovery account should report a few tangible measures: income after essential expenses, care fees and available places, coverage actually usable, voucher utilization and housing instability, and time to resolve a failed benefit or service. Publish differences by income, disability, family arrangement and place. A person whose case fails needs an accountable office, an accessible appeal, a correction deadline and a lawful interim remedy. Those are budget obligations as well as public-facing promises. Do not equate the amount appropriated with the improvement received.

## Integrating the full education package

The adoption includes meals, CACFP and Summer EBT; IDEA at 40%; pre-K and child care; the $30 billion mandatory Title I floor; the teacher salary floor through declining federal grants and state sustainability plans; paraprofessional pay and school mental-health staff; the pipeline and temporary teacher bridge credit. Higher education, competitive ESEA and Title I above the floor remain outside. A parent's contribution record is not a condition of a child's benefit. [S3]

The source's spending anchors imply the following nominal 2030–39 bridge, using geometric interpolation rather than inventing a fresh detailed score:

| Gross fund spending | $T | Treatment |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Previously adopted FAM-2, EDU-1, EDU-3 and EDU-4 | 1.230 | Remove from their former general-fund location once |
| Existing meals, Title I and IDEA spending absorbed | 0.770 | Current-law account transfer; pooled source estimate |
| Additional benefits in full EDU-9B | 0.602 | New federal cost, not a transfer |
| **Full fund** | **2.602** | Includes the three lines above |

The source's approximately $0.63 trillion new-benefit estimate does not exactly equal the annual aggregate interpolation; this bridge uses the residual $0.602 trillion. It preserves the aggregate anchors, including their rounding, rather than pretending the component estimates have been reconciled at appropriation level. Existing education benefit incidence is retained, and only the new increment is added. The absorbed current-law spending is not counted as a new household gain. [S3, S4]

The old contribution estimate also needs correction. It used estate receipts, including revenue already represented by gains-at-death taxation and estate changes in G6, G9 and C35. Moving those receipts to education would reduce general-fund revenue by exactly the same amount. This review keeps them where they are and assigns **zero estate receipts** to the fund. At 1.4 points from 2030, without a grant or the estate money, the model's reserve becomes negative in **2034**. The old rate is therefore not a self-financing solution on the project's present accounts.

### Two financing structures worth choosing between

Both alternatives below contain the full benefit package. Both begin education collections in 2031, with no education payroll charge in 2029 or 2030. The contribution rises in five equal steps through 2035. Both fund the early shortfall explicitly; neither calls an indefinitely growing loan a temporary bridge.

| 2030–39, nominal $T unless noted | Larger payroll contribution | Mixed contribution and public share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Mature combined employer/employee rate | 2.0 points | 1.4 points |
| Dedicated payroll receipts | 2.387 | 1.671 |
| General-fund grants, including transition | 0.463 | 1.105 |
| Modeled income/payroll-tax interaction allowance | 0.298 | 0.209 |
| Household-protection funding reserve | 0.477 | 0.334 |
| Net general-fund relief | 0.761 | 0.352 |
| Government-wide improvement relative to the pre-education agenda | 1.010 | 0.526 |

In the mixed structure, the general fund pays 30% of fund outlays permanently, plus enough during 2030–34 to cover the additional transition shortfall. In the larger-payroll structure, general-fund grants end after 2034. The fund receives the full contribution in both cases. Household rebates are paid from the general fund, so they do not silently drain fund receipts.

The 12.5%-of-receipts tax-interaction allowance and 20% protection reserve are **authored planning allowances**, not scores. Employer contributions can reduce wages or taxable business income and thus other federal receipts; the exact combination needs joint scoring. A protection reserve says what we have set aside, not who has already been protected. Each additional ten percentage points of gross contribution receipts used for these allowances costs about $167 billion in the mixed design over the window. No dynamic growth revenue pays either bill.

I prefer the **mixed structure as the next design reference**. It lowers the long-run wage burden, preserves a contributory institution and makes the public obligation explicit. Its cost is smaller general-fund relief and a permanent claim on progressive revenue. The larger-payroll alternative has a simpler dedicated financing story and greater fiscal room. A 1.8-point case also avoids depletion under the central assumptions, but provides less buffer. The reason to retain 2.0 as a comparator is to examine protection and stress capacity, not to claim that 2.0 is an actuarially necessary rate.

An illustrative protection rule would refund the full combined education contribution on the first **$20,000 of each worker's earnings**, with the household refund phasing out above an indexed $100,000 single/$200,000 joint income threshold. Treat this as a design to score, not as a claim that the 20% reserve buys exactly that benefit. Include self-employed workers; avoid joint-filing cliffs; deliver through withholding or regular payments rather than requiring a year's wait. A permanent household credit is a permanent general-fund obligation.

At a $65,000 wage, the full economic burden of a 1.4-point contribution is $910 a year before protection; the statutory employee half would be $455. A full refund on the first $20,000 would reduce the economic illustration to $630, if eligible. These are arithmetic examples, not predicted wage changes. The employer half is not free, and a family with no young children may receive no immediate education service.

That matters in the aggregate test too. With all existing taxes retained and the protection reserve still unallocated, the mixed case leaves Q4 slightly negative in 2039, about **−$93 per household** on average. The 2.0-point case is roughly **−$1,163**. Average Q1–Q3 gains do not establish that every lower-income person wins. Neither case can yet be certified under D3; the mixed case especially needs a scored credit and replacement of the least defensible middle-reaching taxes. Service benefits valued at cost are not cash in a household's checking account. [S4]

The institution also needs a clear home. I recommend Treasury as collector and custodian, Education as the lead steward of the funding settlement, and continued program responsibility in USDA for nutrition and HHS for child care. Publish one consolidated financial statement and name the agency responsible for each benefit and appeal. A shared fund must not become a place where agencies can send people away because another department owns the payment or the service.

### Solvency is a rule over time, not a single rate

The fund tests run through 2104, a 75-year window from 2030. They preserve source outlay anchors through 2049 and then extrapolate: central payroll growth 4%, outlay growth 3.7%, and a 4% Treasury interest rate after the existing fiscal horizon. Those are transparent assumptions, not a population or actuarial forecast.

The two principal designs avoid depletion in the central case, a temporary payroll recession and a 10% cost-overrun case. However, the slower-payroll/faster-cost case ends with a **falling** reserve ratio. It therefore fails a sustainable-financing test even though reserves remain positive within the displayed horizon. Conversely, central assumptions generate excessively large distant reserves. A fixed rate extrapolated forever is an inadequate design in both directions.

Recommend a one-to-two-year reserve target, regular actuarial valuation, and a statutory response when a sustained imbalance appears. Temporary recessions should use reserves and bounded repayable lending, with a repayment path disclosed. Structural changes in the wage base, staffing costs or participation require adjustment of contributions, the permanent public share or other identified revenue. Preserve benefit continuity while Congress responds; do not let a reserve trigger quietly cut meals or IDEA obligations. Any automatic rate step needs a limit and household-protection funding. Such a mechanism still needs joint modeling before it is selected.

AI makes this especially important. If productivity gains accrue mainly as capital income while employment and wages weaken, payroll may grow too slowly even when the economy is productive. The existing education projection assumes the opposite alignment. A general-fund share supported by a broader income base provides some diversification; it does not eliminate the risk. Do not book speculative AI-levy receipts or public-investment returns as the solution.

### The federal-state cost shift deserves a separate decision

The source reduces the federal pre-K share from 100% through 2034 to 60% in 2041. Teacher grants also taper while states are expected to sustain pay. Those changes can improve the federal books by moving costs to governments with balanced-budget constraints and unequal tax bases. That is a potential source of unequal service access and procyclical cuts.

There is also an unresolved accounting inconsistency. The source reports combined federal pre-K/child-care spending of $143 billion in 2049 and state cost of $57 billion. If the entire $143 billion is a 60% federal share of the same services, the corresponding 40% state share would be about **$95 billion**, not $57 billion. The source may be applying different coverage or denominator conventions, but it does not reconcile them. Do not describe the lower number as established.

I recommend testing a permanent **80% federal floor with higher matching for lower-capacity states**, coupled with maintenance of effort, rather than mechanically taking every state to 60%. Applying the same share to the whole combined care line is an explicit sensitivity: an 80% floor adds about **$47.7 billion federal spending in 2049**, and retaining 100% adds about **$95.3 billion**. The generated reserve cases include both; they are not in the reference fiscal totals. Resolve the component boundary and state distribution before adoption. This would protect households and workers in poorer states, but it needs genuine federal financing and cannot be added to the package on the strength of the gross $2.0 trillion transfer.

### Social Security: complete the commitment, with a delayed broad-rate fallback

The existing D8 model closes roughly 64% of the long-run gap. Adding SS-5 alone remains insufficient. Its interaction with SS-2 matters: the repository records SSA's joint investment/pass-through estimate below the sum of the separate provisions. I test a conservative 0.34-point-of-payroll interaction loss from 2031 rather than silently stacking both in full. This is an authored annual approximation based on that comparison, not an SSA package score. H1a/H1b interactions remain additional uncertainties. [S5]

With that adjustment, D8 plus SS-5 depletes in about **2073** in this model. Adding one payroll point phased over 2035–44 still depletes in **2096**. Roughly **two additional points**, one per statutory side, phased over those years, pass the model's conservative reserve test. Waiting until 2040 to begin the same two-point increase leaves a positive 75-year actuarial balance but fails the stable-terminal-reserve condition. A positive summarized balance is not enough.

My recommendation is to complete the high-income base reforms first and retain a **slow, later broad-rate increase as a specified solvency fallback**, alongside a serious comparison with further high-income-base or upper-end benefit-accrual changes. Protect the benefit floor; do not use retirement-age increases as a supposedly neutral shortcut for people with shorter lives or physically demanding careers. The bipartisan SS-10 path remains worth assessing, but removing the investment-income provision requires replacing its lost resources explicitly; political durability is not actuarial financing.

Do not adopt “two points” from this exercise as a final rate. It is evidence that the present one-point option may be too small once timing and interactions are included. Education, leave and Social Security together could add several points to payroll. Any final package needs common household scoring, early-worker protection and a legal plan for OASI and DI separately. Combined OASDI arithmetic does not itself authorize transfers between the two funds, and Social Security cannot be carried through ordinary reconciliation. [S5, S6]

### Other funds need honest liabilities

**Medicare HI:** retain H1a and the HI share of HLT-6 savings. The summary engine's baseline differs from the Trustees; the improvement is more informative than its absolute balance. Halving assumed HLT-6 HI savings moves modeled depletion to 2042, compared with 2064 under its central program case. That does not prove either date, but it demonstrates why the claimed HI reserve is not spare money. Develop a defined high-income financing reserve against the joint health score. Medicare SMI remains an automatic general-revenue/premium obligation; an HI fix does not solve all Medicare financing or justify pushing costs into beneficiary premiums.

**Paid leave:** the high-take-up case costs about 1.01% of payroll at maturity, above the adopted 0.85% rate cap. Its Treasury loan grows to about **$865B by 2050**. Raising the tested cap to **1.10%**, with the existing gradual step rule, pays the loan off by 2050 without cutting benefits in this model. I recommend developing that funded high-take-up response, with household protection, rather than describing the current loan as self-liquidating. A 1.20% cap offers no additional improvement in the tested case because the rate never needs to reach it. These are alternatives, not adopted rates.

**Transportation:** renewal coverage in the 2040s is encouraging, but the engine ends in 2046. It cannot prove 75-year solvency. Preserve explicit top-ups and build a later tax-base transition without simultaneously charging households the same fuel/carbon/mileage externality twice. **Carbon, merger and immigration fees:** keep their own revenue and spending paired. Neither carbon dividends nor fee-funded enforcement are unused financing for education or Social Security.

## Redesign the general-fund settlement, not just the closer

The existing employer health-exclusion cap, C19b, is my first candidate for replacement. It is poorly aligned with a recovery narrative centered on restoring health security; people with expensive coverage can face new liabilities without feeling that they chose unusually generous benefits. Geographic costs and worker health needs complicate the case for a uniform premium percentile. C20's explicit income surtax is more legible and progressive, but its timing still matters. The earlier reasons for choosing both—broadening financing and meeting the average household test—deserve weight; they do not settle the combined burden after education adoption. [S7]

Compare three structures:

| Structure | What it buys | Principal cost or weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep the existing taxes, with later C19b/C20 phase-in | Most dependable preservation of recorded fiscal room; education and fund repairs can proceed with fewer replacement assumptions | Retains a hard-to-defend health-tax design and strains Q4 after new payroll charges |
| Replace C19b/C20 with a progressive contribution on broad income | Clearer liability; capital and labor both support common obligations; explicit exemptions/protection can serve households without children | Gives up $1.33T of existing receipts first; the new base needs joint scoring against already-taxed gains, business income and high-income surtaxes |
| Later consumption tax with an advance universal credit | Diversifies revenue away from wages and can raise substantial permanent resources | An initial consumer-price effect conflicts with recovery; substantial credit cost, retiree/transition issues and administrative preparation; not an early-2029 instrument |

I prefer **progressive broad-income replacement for development**, with the mixed education structure, while retaining the existing tax package as the funded comparator until a replacement is scored. Build the contribution on wages, self-employment and capital income, above a meaningful indexed household allowance; coordinate with existing Medicare/SS investment-income rules and repeal the replaced taxes dollar for dollar. Avoid a second general-fund wage-only W1 levy on top of education. Examine a 31% corporate-rate alternative and additional high-end base repair as components, not as a complete solution or sums of independent headline scores. The earlier corporate illustration showed that this alone did not close the stress gap.

The new model separates two requirements. For the **agenda general fund**, retaining existing taxes with mixed education financing needs approximately **0.063% of GDP** in additional permanent net resources to maintain an illustrative 0.1%-GDP margin in the central case. With additional housing, an unallocated 0.1%-GDP service allowance and the deeper receipt downside, that rises to **0.639%**. Removing C19b/C20 as well raises it to **0.981%**. These are net financing requirements after modeled costs, not recommended tax rates. The service allowance is not a costed participation, care or adaptation entitlement.

For the **whole federal government**, the requirement is larger. The reference path still reaches roughly **150% debt/GDP in 2056**, despite improving on the inherited baseline. Solving for additional net resources phased in over 2035–44 sufficient to make debt/GDP nonincreasing in every modeled year 2045–56 gives roughly **1.7% of GDP** in the reference/recovery case and **2.7%** in the replacement-plus-expansion downside. That adjustment lowers modeled 2056 debt/GDP to about 118–124%, rather than claiming an arbitrary low debt target. These are conditional resource requirements, not a scored tax package or a forecast. [S4]

The whole-government requirement **includes** the agenda gap; do not add the two. Nor should Social Security or HI closers be added to these simulations without reconciling the old ledger's fund cash flows, their joint tax interactions and the credit for their additional receipts. The scenarios deliberately leave those fund comparisons separate. The present tool is strong enough to show that “better than baseline” is inadequate; it is not yet a unified actuarial score of the recommended settlement.

Keep D4's permanent-financing rule and investment discipline, and add a proposed whole-government objective: a sustainable debt trajectory under central assumptions, with transparent recession escape and recovery rules, while every dedicated fund can honor its obligations. Do not use a debt target to force automatic benefit cuts during recession. If the country chooses a broader basic floor, it must choose the dependable revenue to sustain it. If broader taxation remains politically unacceptable, specify the resulting limits on expansion instead of relying on optimistic savings or administrative “efficiency.”

## The judgments to make next

I would retain the full education commitment and develop the **1.4-point contribution plus explicit public share** as the preferred reference, with the larger-payroll alternative retained for comparison. Choose whether the general fund should have this permanent role, and whether the pre-K share should be redesigned to protect poorer states. Those decisions determine the size of the progressive revenue requirement.

I would replace the employer health-exclusion cap in the preferred redesign, compare whether C20 should remain as part of a broader income contribution, and keep new broad household charges out of the opening recovery phase. The model's deferral of C19b/C20 to 2033–35 forgoes about **$400B**; it does not make that money reappear later. Education protection is already charged as an allowance, but rebates for fuel, alcohol, leave or other existing charges would cost more. Protecting only the education deduction does not protect a household's total budget.

I would keep the Social Security floor and targeted benefits, complete its financing with a joint high-income/slow-rate package, and repair paid leave's high-take-up cap. The remaining choice is how much later payroll capacity to use versus other income sources or upper-end benefit adjustments. No one should be told that an employer share or a distant effective date makes the cost disappear.

The next substantial assignment should be **one integrated Recovery and Solvency budget**, not separate financing exercises for each attractive policy. It should put two fully specified alternatives on the same tax and benefit baseline: mixed education plus broad-income replacement, and larger-payroll education plus retained taxes. Price the household credit and realistic FY2029 delivery; reconcile appropriation-level education transfers and state matching; jointly score the high-income provisions, health savings and SS/HI flows; then test debt, every fund and household outcomes together. Use households without children, disabled/nonworking people, retirees, single adults, self-employed people and low-wage families as explicit cases, not just quintile averages.

Keep a bounded housing/participation expansion and a disaggregated climate allocation within that comparison. The prior climate results favor investigating grid delivery before adding more undifferentiated subsidies, but they do not justify repealing the entire credit bundle: that experiment increased cumulative emissions. Financing the recovery should support the workforce and infrastructure needed to deliver it. The standard for the next budget is that a person can understand both what improves and what they contribute—and that the institutions responsible can keep the promise.
