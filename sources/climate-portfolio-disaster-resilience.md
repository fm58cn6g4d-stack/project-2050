# Disaster preparedness and response: design and pricing

**Context.** Claude Code, October 26, 2026; updated after Espen funded the fuller tier. Policy IDs: CLI-7, CLI-7A (new), CLI-7B, CLI-7C, CLI-8, CLI-5C, with the FEMA, NOAA and flood-map rows in CAP-7/CAP-7S. Source base: `main` at `77f8a5e`; branch `claude/climate-portfolio`, PR #26. Status: completed for review, not merged.

**Why this file exists.** CLI-7 has carried "unscored, in no total" in every ledger since the domain was written, so disasters keep showing up as a gap in Project 2050's accounting. This file prices CLI-7 component by component and separates the agenda's cost from a current-law exposure that the baseline does not budget. It also sets out how the pieces layer with CLI-8 and the climate portfolio.

**Files.** The numbers are generated by `index/tools/climate-portfolio/pricing.py` from `pricing.json`: see [climate-portfolio-pricing.md](climate-portfolio-pricing.md) and the component table [climate-portfolio-disaster-components.csv](climate-portfolio-disaster-components.csv). This file is the readable design.

## Bottom line

1. **Price CLI-7 as a core package of about $100B over 2030-39** ($61-151B, 2025 dollars; about $0.12T nominal), plus about $2B one-time to resolve NFIP's debt:
   - a standing mitigation fund;
   - a larger post-disaster mitigation share;
   - NFIP premium help for low- and moderate-income households;
   - wildfire risk reduction;
   - extreme heat;
   - faster survivor aid.

   This replaces the old "unscored, $0.05-0.15T" placeholder. FEMA staffing and flood maps are already in CAP-7, so they are listed but not counted again.
2. **Fund the fuller tier too, about $54B** ($31-90B). Espen decided on October 26 to fund it as soon as possible. It covers insurance help for low- and moderate-income owners and renters facing wind, wildfire and hail risk, and a standing voluntary buyout program. It starts in FY2030 with the core tier. Insurance help does not wait for a reinsurance backstop; states keep it by adopting CLI-8 codes within three years.
3. **Disclose the bigger gap honestly.** About $15-25B a year of disaster appropriations recurs but sits outside the extrapolated baseline. GAO counts at least $448B over FY2015-24, plus the $110B December 2024 act. That is current-law exposure, not agenda cost. It should appear in D4 tests next to the Judgment Fund line, so a bad hurricane season doesn't look like the agenda failing its fiscal rule.
4. **Finance by type.**
   - The mitigation fund and wildfire work are ten-year capital programs (about $65B mid), borrowing-eligible under D4-B.
   - The permanent pieces (D2, D3, D6, D7 and D8; about $78B nominal over 2030-39) are general-fund costs that continue after 2039. Their financing is part of Espen's open decision on the carbon-fee increment.
   - The climate portfolio's combined budget is in the [pricing file](climate-portfolio-pricing.md).
5. **Keep the federal role.** The FEMA Review Council (May 2026) would shift response to states through parametric block grants and move flood insurance toward the private market. Most of that needs Congress. The project's design adopts the useful parts: faster, estimate-based Public Assistance and a single survivor application. It rejects shrinking federal capacity, because the 2025 cuts (FEMA staff down 17%, BRIC cancelled) are what produced the current gaps.

## Where things stand (checked October 26, 2026)

- **Total disaster spending.** At least $448B was appropriated for disaster assistance across 30-plus federal entities in FY2015-24, about $45B a year. The Disaster Relief Supplemental Appropriations Act of December 2024 added $110B (GAO-25-108125). CBO puts FEMA's own spending at about $12B a year over 30 years (2022 dollars), ranging from $2B (1992) to $47B (2020).
- **FEMA.** The Disaster Relief Fund is about $26.4B for FY2026 (reported; not confirmed against enacted text). Staff fell from 25,134 in FY2025 to 20,968 by April 2026, cuts GAO found were made without a workforce plan.
- **BRIC**, the pre-disaster mitigation program:
  - April 2025: cancelled.
  - December 2025: a federal court ruled the cancellation unlawful.
  - March 2026: FEMA said it would release the remaining funds and open up to $1B of applications.

  I could not confirm what was actually disbursed after March.
- **NFIP:**
  - It owes Treasury $22.525B and has $7.9B of borrowing authority left.
  - It has run on 35 short-term reauthorizations since FY2017, and the current one expires September 30, 2026 (CRS IN10784, March 2026). I did not find a later status.
  - Risk Rating 2.0 is associated with an 11-39% drop in new policies (a December 2025 study cited by senators).
  - Congress cancelled $16B of its debt in 2017.
- **Wildfire.** The Forest Service and Interior received $7.3B for wildfire in FY2025 (CRS IF13102). The Forest Service's 2022 Wildfire Crisis Strategy projected $50B over ten years to treat up to 50 million more acres, of which $20B is on its own land.
- **The FEMA Review Council** approved its final report on May 7, 2026: ten recommendations and more than 150 actions. They include a parametric block grant ("RAPID") replacing Public Assistance, a single survivor payment, higher declaration thresholds and shifting flood insurance toward states and the private market. Most need Congress (CRS IN12693).

## Design: what the package does

### Ready: reduce losses before disasters

- **D1. Standing mitigation fund (new CLI-7A), $3-6B a year.** This is mandatory money with a formula floor for every state and tribe, so small and rural places don't lose to grant-writing capacity, plus competitive large projects. It carries EXE-3's anti-impoundment terms, so no administration can cancel it the way BRIC was cancelled. It is a ten-year authorization, which makes it time-limited capital under D4-B, with reauthorization tied to an evaluation of results.
  - NIBS (2019) finds federal mitigation grants save about $6 per $1. I use 3-6 for future projects, because the cheapest projects tend to get done first.
  - Mid-case avoided losses are about $250B over project lives. That figure is not banked.
- **D2. Post-disaster mitigation share up from 15% to 20%, $0.8-1.8B a year.** This replaces the domain's "$1 of mitigation per $1 of supplemental" set-aside, which would be pro-cyclical (largest right after the worst year) and unbounded.
- **D5. Wildfire risk reduction, $1.5-3.5B a year above today's level.**
  - What it covers: completing the Wildfire Crisis Strategy's priority firesheds, community defensible space, and a year-round prescribed-fire and fuels workforce.
  - Returns: benefit-cost evidence is mixed (I use 1-4).
  - Overlap: CLI-13's forestry money is limited to reforestation, so the two are not double-counted.
- **D6. Extreme heat, $0.3-1.0B a year.** Heat becomes an eligible hazard, with cooling-center and tree-canopy grants and CDC's Climate-Ready States and Cities extended to all 50 states. Cooling help through LIHEAP stays in CLI-5.
- **CLI-8, unchanged, at about $0 federal cost.** It is the cheapest piece:
  - a forward-looking floodplain standard and hazard-resistant codes as conditions of new federal aid (NIBS: model codes save about $11 per $1);
  - risk disclosure at sale or lease;
  - forward-looking underwriting.

  It is also the condition that makes D8 and D10 safe to offer.

### Respond: faster, fairer help

- **FEMA capacity (already in CAP-7, not added again).** About $0.9B a year restores 5,000 staff, gives FEMA cabinet-level independence and builds the survivor application. Future-conditions flood maps are a $9B surge item. This package depends on them: a mitigation fund with no planners and no current maps just sends money to the places with the best consultants.
- **D7. Universal Survivor Application and estimate-based Public Assistance, $0-1.5B a year.** This is the program effect of aid reaching people faster and more completely; the administration is priced in CAP-7. It takes the Review Council's best idea, fast estimate-based payments, without its block-grant retreat.

### Recover: keep people insured and housed

- **D3. NFIP premium help for low- and moderate-income households, $1.0-2.5B a year.**
  - Who qualifies: primary residences at or below 120% of area median income, capped at 400% of poverty, as in FEMA's own legislative proposal.
  - How it works: the help is tied to the household, so it can't be sold with the house. Risk-based prices stay visible, so the signal not to build in harm's way isn't lost.
  - Scale: about 1.1-1.5 million current policies plus 0.3-0.8 million new low-income policies, at $600-1,000 average help.
  - It is permanent, so it needs a pay-for (below).
- **D4. Resolve NFIP's debt, about $2B scored ($0-6B; face value $22.5B).** Cancel the debt together with a multi-year reauthorization (to 2035) and a reserve rule. The scored cost is low because repayment was never realistic: CBO scored a similar $17.5B forgiveness in 2007 at about $1.2B net. This ends the 35-extension cycle that keeps the program on the edge of lapsing.
- **D8 (fuller tier, funded). Insurance help for wind, wildfire and hail, $1.5-5B a year.** Premium help for low- and moderate-income owners and renters in high-risk counties. It is available from FY2030 without waiting for a reinsurance backstop. States keep it by adopting CLI-8's codes within three years, so federal money doesn't keep subsidizing rebuilding to old standards.
- **D9 (fuller tier, funded). Voluntary buyouts and relocation, $1.9-4.7B a year.**
  - Scale: 10,000-25,000 properties a year at a 75% federal share.
  - Also covers: receiving-community investment and renter relocation aid, so tenants aren't left behind when owners sell.
  - Cost: about $250,000 per property including about 10% activity costs (Curran-Groome et al. 2022).
  - Returns: Iowa's buyouts returned a benefit-cost ratio of 2.19.
- **D10 (option CLI-7C; designed Oct 26, recommended, pending Espen). Federal residential catastrophe reinsurance facility.**
  - *What it covers.* Treasury reinsures insurers' residential wind, wildfire, hail and (where a state opts in) earthquake losses in a layer between a state-level 1-in-100-year and 1-in-250-year industry loss. Insurers keep 10% of the layer so they still price and underwrite. The annual cap is $150B. Flood stays in NFIP (D4).
  - *Who gets it.* Only states that adopt CLI-8's hazard-resistant codes and risk disclosure, and only insurers that offer all-peril residential policies and mitigation discounts (FORTIFIED-type). Code adoption is the price of entry, as with D8.
  - *How it is priced.* Premiums are risk-based by state and peril, at 1.3-1.5 times modeled expected loss, with no cross-subsidy built into rates. The expected loss in the layer is roughly 0.6-1.0% of limit a year, about $1-1.5B (estimate).
  - *How a bad year is paid.* The fund pays first. Treasury borrowing authority up to the $150B cap pays what the fund cannot. A TRIA-style recoupment surcharge on all residential property premiums (capped at 3% a year, up to 15 years, about $75B) repays the borrowing. This is the FAM-3 pattern with TRIA's recoupment added.
  - *D4 treatment.* Scored at expected net cost: −$0.3B / +$0.3B / +$1.0B a year (low / mid / high), covering pricing error, a draw before the fund has built up, and draws beyond the recoupment cap. It is a permanent line. Any draw not recouped is a disclosed contingent liability in every D4 test: $75-150B in a severe year. That is the same size as the Talarico stress draw ($150-200B in one season, scenario figures).
  - *Why do it.* Reinsurance is the cost that has been pushing insurers out of Gulf and Western markets. The Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund shows both how this works and where it stops: it has $17.0B of claims-paying capacity on a $1.29B premium for 2025-26, but only about $9B is liquid, so after a large storm it relies on post-event bonds. U.S. insured catastrophe losses were about $100B in 2025, 12% above the 10-year average (Gallagher Re). Only a federal balance sheet can hold the tail without post-event bonds. Sen. Schiff's INSURE Act (July 2025) takes the same approach, a Treasury program above a catastrophe threshold. This design adds the code condition, risk-based pricing and recoupment, which answer the industry's objection that a national program raises costs for low-risk policyholders.
  - *What stays open.* Whether the recoupment surcharge should be state-weighted by risk. A flat surcharge spreads Gulf and Western tail costs nationally. Weighting it by state risk is fairer but makes it harder to pass. Recommended: flat up to 1%, risk-weighted above.

**Financing.**
- **Core capital (mitigation fund and wildfire).** Borrowing-eligible inside the D4-B investment envelope, using part of the room freed when the rebuild surge was cut to about $105B.
- **Permanent pieces** (D2, D3, D6, D7 and D8: about $78B nominal over 2030-39 in the central case, and about $12B a year by FY2050). They are general-fund costs. The climate credit redesign's savings help in this decade but end by about 2040, so the corrected [whole-package budget](climate-portfolio-pricing.md) shows a continuing general-fund cost after 2040 that needs a permanent financing source (Espen's decision on the carbon-fee increment, in the allocation file).

With both tiers, D4-B borrowing is about $152B central ($108-212B) against about $195B of freed room, overshooting by about $17B only in the unfavorable case. NFIP debt resolution (about $2B, FY2030) is now in the budget.

## Interactions and layering

- **CLI-8 codes and disclosure** reduce what D1, D3, D8 and D10 have to pay for. They are the condition for offering D8 and D10.
- **D1 and D9** compete for the same high-risk properties. The fund pays for elevation and floodproofing where people stay; buyouts handle repeated-loss places where staying isn't safe.
- **D3 and risk-based pricing work together.** Help is tied to need, not the property, so prices still discourage new building in flood zones.
- **D5 wildfire work and CLI-13 forestry** together protect the land sink that the climate targets depend on. CLI-13 forestry covers reforestation only.
- **Avoided losses are not counted as savings.** The mid case is about $420B over project lifetimes with the fuller tier, mostly from D1. Some would reduce future disaster supplementals, but those sit outside the baseline anyway, so counting them would be counting savings against spending that was never budgeted.

## Durability under a hostile administration

- **What survives:**
  - Mandatory money with anti-impoundment terms (D1, D5, D9) is the strongest protection.
  - A statute-defined formula floor for D1 resists the cancellation that ended BRIC.
  - Tying premium help to the household gives it a constituency.
- **What stays exposed:** must-pass riders and FEMA's leadership. Cabinet status with Senate-confirmed leadership and its own inspector general, already in CLI-7, narrows that exposure.

## Limits

- The component amounts are planning estimates built from sourced unit costs and program sizes; they are not CBO scores.
- The NFIP figures use approximate policy counts. I could not retrieve FEMA's current policies-in-force or average premium.
- The FY2026 Disaster Relief Fund level is as reported, not checked against enacted text.
- Benefit-cost ratios come from national studies and vary widely by place.
- I could not confirm BRIC disbursements or NFIP's status after March 2026.
