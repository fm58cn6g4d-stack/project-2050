# Climate Durability Pathway: Session Findings
2026-10-19 · @espen

## Summary
A durable U.S. climate transition is achievable from one 18-month legislative window, if the law is written to run itself, insulated from a hostile Congress, and built to lock in most of the shift before January 2037. In this session's model the expanded plan cuts net emissions about 64% below 2005 by 2035 and about 95% by 2045, against 30% and 39% under current policy. Even if a Republican trifecta repeals the fee, dividend and vehicle standard in 2037, 2045 emissions stay about 85% below 2005.
**Main findings**
  - **Republican votes.** Broad bipartisan authority for EPA over power-plant carbon is unlikely before the mid-2030s. Near-term openings are carrots (nuclear, geothermal, transmission), industry-backed narrow rules on the AIM Act model, and carbon-intensity trade tools aimed at China.
  - **Long run.** Republican support most plausibly arrives as codification of a mostly clean grid in the late 2030s, after partisan passage, trade policy and red-state investment have changed the interests at stake.
  - **Design.** Congress acts as the implementer: prices, standards, schedules and payments in statute, delivered through the tax system, utilities, grid operators, states, public power and contracts, because rebuilt agencies will be thin in 2029-31.
  - **Insulation.** Nearly all money can be placed beyond a Republican Congress's reach in 2031-37 through tax law, mandatory spending, fully funded contracts backed by the Judgment Fund, borrowing authority and fee funding. The residual exposure is must-pass leverage and Senate confirmations.
  - **Lock-in.** The 2032-36 window matters most: replace long-lived fossil equipment at natural turnover, leave 400 GW or more under construction in January 2037, and multiply the households, firms, states and trading partners with a stake.
  - **Cost.** About $2.5T gross in the first decade and about $1.8-1.9T net of fee and border revenue (estimate). That breaks Dec. D4 as modeled; see the conflicts section.
**Status.** This is session analysis, not a canonical file. It was built under a harsher scenario than Dec. D1 and departs from Dec. D7 and Dec. D4 in places. Each proposal is mapped to existing domain-climate.md IDs (CLI-1 to CLI-8) or REV-C1/REV-C2 and offered as an option, not a change.
**Number tags.** Sourced = a cited figure; scaled = a cited figure moved to another window; estimate = this session's judgment. Unless marked, costs are 2029-38 totals as modeled in session, roughly equal to 2030-39, in trillions. Emissions are net GHG in Gt CO₂e.

## Republican votes: why they stopped and the near-term openings
Republicans wrote and passed the 1970 Clean Air Act and the 1990 amendments, but since the 2009 Waxman-Markey fight carbon regulation has become a party-identity issue. Persuasion on climate will not change that soon; aligned interests can.
**What has worked**
  - **The AIM Act model (2020).** HFC phase-down passed with broad Republican support because U.S. chemical makers held the substitutes and asked for it. The template: regulation passes when a major home-state industry wants it.
  - **The 1990 acid-rain model.** A Republican president wanted it, and cap-and-trade made it acceptable. Presidential leadership remains the strongest single lever over the conference.
  - **Carrots.** Nuclear (the 2024 ADVANCE Act), geothermal, carbon capture and transmission draw cross-party votes; sticks rarely do.
**Near-term openings**
  - **Trade and China.** Carbon-intensity border tools (for example Sen. Cassidy's foreign pollution fee idea) appeal to Republicans because U.S. steel, aluminum and other goods are generally less carbon-intensive than Chinese equivalents. Border tools need domestic measurement and standards, a door to performance rules.
  - **Permitting bargains.** Republicans want faster permitting and limits on litigation. The 2024 Manchin-Barrasso bill cleared Senate ENR 15-4. In this project the bipartisan permitting deal (AAJA) is stipulated as enacted in 2026-28 (Dec. D1), so the bargaining chip is partly spent.
  - **Design concessions.** Explicit statutory limits answering West Virginia v. EPA and Loper Bright, reliability safety valves, cost caps and market compliance address the stated objections.
**What does not work by itself**
  - **District benefits.** IRA investment concentrated in Republican districts produced letters defending the credits in 2025, then mostly votes for repeal. Benefits create soft resistance, not affirmative votes, unless paired with credit-claiming and ownership.
  - **Disasters.** Direct experience moves opinion modestly and briefly, filtered through partisanship. It drives adaptation politics more than mitigation votes.
**Binding constraint.** Primaries. With few competitive seats, most Republicans face more risk from the right. Reconciliation cannot carry regulatory authority (Byrd rule), so absent Republican votes the alternative is a Democratic trifecta with a Dec. D2 rules path.

## Twenty-year projection, 2026-2046
Durable federal power-sector carbon authority most likely arrives first through trade policy or a Democratic trifecta, with Republican acceptance following once the transition is largely done. These are judgment calls, not forecasts.
**2026-2030: peak polarization, quiet groundwork.** Executive whiplash continues: EPA finalized repeal of the 2009 endangerment finding and federal vehicle GHG standards on Feb 12, 2026, with challenges pending in the D.C. Circuit (sourced, domain-climate.md). AI data-center load drives a gas buildout and Republican enthusiasm for nuclear and geothermal. The EU's border adjustment enters its charging phase and China's clean-tech export dominance sharpens. Mid-decade redistricting moves toward less competition.
**2030-2037: the contested middle.** The post-Trump Republican Party defines itself, the largest uncertainty in the projection.
  - **Oil demand plateaus,** driven mostly by Chinese and developing-world EVs. U.S. LNG keeps gas interests strong and geopolitically framed.
  - **Trade becomes the main bipartisan venue.** An industrial carbon-intensity standard with a border fee is the most plausible bipartisan emissions law of the period. It covers heavy industry first but builds the measurement machinery that later reaches power.
  - **Insurance crises mature** in Florida, California and Louisiana. Impacts drive adaptation politics (resilience, insurance, water), which normalizes federal climate action in Republican vocabulary.
  - **The 2030 census** shifts seats toward climate-exposed but Republican-leaning Sun Belt states. The 2031 redistricting is the real test of reform.
**2037-2046: the codification window.** Millennials and Gen Z are most of the electorate and a growing share of members. Large parts of red-state grids (Texas, Oklahoma, Iowa, Kansas) are clean on economics alone, so a federal standard mainly protects incumbents' investments. That is when Republicans have historically come along: HFCs, and the Respect for Marriage Act dynamic of codifying a settled status quo. Wild card: solar geoengineering, which could appeal as a technological alternative to regulation.
**Forces and counterforces**
| Force | Direction | Caveat |
| Generational turnover | Toward support; younger Republicans more concerned | Young men shifted right in 2024; climate is a low priority even when concern is high |
| Shrinking fossil constituencies | Toward support as coal fades | Oil and gas concentrated in fewer, safer states |
| Physical climate costs | Toward adaptation first | Weak and short-lived opinion effects |
| Trade with China and CBAMs | Toward carbon accounting as a trade weapon | Depends on post-Trump trade doctrine |
| Partisan sorting | Against; identity can outlast economics | Deepening urban-rural and education divides |
| Timing | Codification may come after the decisive decade | The main reason not to wait for bipartisanship |

**Rough odds on the route to durable power-sector authority by 2046** (estimate): partisan passage after filibuster change, about a quarter to a third; trade-first bipartisan law extended to power, about a fifth; late bipartisan codification, about a fifth to a quarter; no durable statute, about a quarter.

## Structural levers: statehood, districts and primaries
The reforms most likely to produce bipartisan climate law probably have to pass on party lines first; reform is downstream of the partisan path, not an alternative to it. The designs themselves belong in domain-democracy.md; this section adds only the climate lens.
**DC and Puerto Rico statehood.** DC almost certainly elects two Democrats. Puerto Rico's politics run on status, and its pro-statehood party includes Republicans (Gov. Jenniffer González), so a Democratic lean with a possible split delegation is realistic: about three to four more Democratic-leaning seats in all (estimate). A Puerto Rican Republican senator would likely be among the chamber's most climate-friendly Republicans, given Hurricane Maria and grid failures. Admission is a majority statute but needs the same rules path as climate law; its value is durability of later majorities. Risks: backlash and retaliation, and DC's 23rd Amendment problem.
**Redistricting reform** is a modest lever. Most polarization comes from geographic sorting and primaries, nationwide commissions would add only a few dozen competitive seats, and districts do not touch the Senate, the binding constraint.
**Primary reform** matters more for Senate votes. Top-four or top-five primaries with ranked-choice generals (Alaska) cut the primary penalty for crossover votes. 2024 was rough: measures failed in Nevada, Colorado, Oregon, Idaho, Montana, South Dakota and Arizona, Alaska's system narrowly survived repeal, and more than a dozen states have banned ranked choice. Status may have changed since mid-2026.
| Rank | State | Why it is plausible | Main obstacle |
| 1 | Alaska | Proof of concept; Murkowski model | Defending against repeal |
| 2 | Montana | Came close in 2024; independent streak; accessible initiative | Hostile legislature |
| 3 | Nevada | Passed in 2022 before failing in 2024 | Swing state, so moderates both parties |
| 4 | Nebraska | Initiatives, nonpartisan unicameral, Osborn's 2024 run | Low reform infrastructure |
| 5 | Arizona | Initiatives, large independent bloc; 2024 loss tied to litigation | Competing measures |
| 6 | Ohio or Missouri | Initiatives exist | Missouri banned RCV in 2024; lighter open-primary reform more viable |

Target: three or four new states over two decades, yielding perhaps four to eight senators with general-election incentives (estimate). With a Puerto Rico Republican and existing moderates, that approaches the ten Republicans a 60-vote bill needs.
**Other levers.** Repealing the federal single-member-district mandate to allow proportional multi-member districts is the largest lever, since it would elect urban and suburban Republicans with climate constituencies, but it needs a trifecta (Dec. D1 moves STV and House size to a 2033 second-term package).

## Scenario and design logic
The session's stress scenario: a Democratic president January 2029 to January 2037, a trifecta with filibuster change in 2029-30, loss of one or both chambers in the 2030 midterm and no trifecta again before 2037, and a Supreme Court that drifts slightly toward the center as one or two conservative seats open. That is harsher than Dec. D1, which stipulates a Democratic Senate through 2036 at 52+ seats and a House possibly regained for 2033-36. A plan that survives this case survives D1's.
**Four constraints follow**
  - **One window.** About 18 months of lawmaking (January 2029 to summer 2030). Anything left for a phase-two bill does not happen. This matches D1's "no 2033 queue" and D5's R1-R3 vehicles.
  - **A veto shield, 2031-37.** Repeal is off the table, but a hostile chamber can still use appropriations riders, shutdowns and the debt limit, and a Republican Senate can block confirmations.
  - **Exposure after 2037.** A majority-rule Senate cuts both ways (D2's reciprocity rule). Durability must come from contracts, built infrastructure, state redundancy, household benefits and industry incumbents, not from the statute being hard to amend.
  - **The Court.** First major challenges reach the Supreme Court around 2032-34, likely before the median shifts. Draft for a skeptical Roberts or Kavanaugh median: numbers in the statute, authority stated expressly (design rule "Write for this Court"; CLI-4; CRT-8 templates).
**The organizing idea.** Congress is the implementer. The statute sets prices, standards, schedules and payments directly and runs them through machinery that already works at scale, because agencies cut after 2025 will still be rebuilding in 2029-31 (design rule "Capacity first"; D9 and CAP-7).
**Confirmation risk.** If Republicans take the Senate in 2031 they can block agency heads, FERC and NRC commissioners and judges, and could hold a Supreme Court seat open as in 2016. That weakens the scenario's Court assumption. Mitigations: confirm the full slate and as many judges as possible in 2029-30, create judgeships in 2029, and let commissions act with reduced quorums.

**Why statutory caps matter.** AAJA's Sec. 1401 protects permitted plants' "operation at full capacity," so CLI-3's override is a precondition for any power-sector limit. Caps written in statute also survive a future administration that declines to regulate, which the 1990 acid-rain caps showed. Allowances should keep Title IV's rule that they are not property, avoiding takings claims when caps tighten.
**Double coverage to resolve.** REV-C1 already prices coal and gas upstream. Statutory power caps on top of the fee would either act as a backstop that binds only if the fee falls short (the session's intent) or need a fee credit for capped emissions.

## Insulation from a hostile Congress, 2031-37
Nearly all of the plan's money can be placed beyond a Republican Congress's reach until 2037, because changing enacted law then needs the president's signature. What stays exposed is must-pass leverage, Senate confirmations and anything left on annual appropriations.
| Tool | Best used for | Residual risk |
| Tax credits (permanent law) | Clean power, EVs, heat pumps, manufacturing | Essentially none until 2037 |
| Mandatory spending, available until expended | Grants, climate bank, state formula funds, energy-communities fund, resilience program | Riders in must-pass bills |
| Trust fund with a permanent appropriation | Climate Dividend from fee revenue (passes the trust-fund rule: a permanent benefit with its own revenue) | Very low |
| Contract authority plus the Judgment Fund | Contracts for difference, advance purchase commitments, long-term transmission capacity | Low: unpaid contractors sue and are paid from the Judgment Fund, a permanent appropriation |
| Borrowing authority | Climate bank, DOE loans, TVA and the power marketing administrations | Low |
| Fee-funded operations | EPA climate office, compliance registries, enforcement, FERC and NRC permitting staff | Low; structurally like the CFPB funding the Supreme Court upheld in 2024 |

**Closing the gap**
  - **"Shall obligate" deadlines** with state and grantee standing to sue, plus EXE-3's anti-impoundment terms (termination only for cause, deemed apportionment).
  - **Fund contracts in full at enactment,** so no liquidating appropriation is ever needed.
  - **Remove hostage points:** an automatic continuing resolution at prior-year levels, and repeal or long suspension of the debt limit.
  - **Do not rely on agency independence.** FERC commissioners are now removable at will after Slaughter (domain-climate.md CLI-6; EXE-9); the self-executing statute and contracts are the safer design.
The AAJA parallel is worth noting: the stipulated permitting law already uses Judgment Fund damages to protect permitted fossil projects. The same tool protects clean contracts.
**Rule timing (corrected in session).** An earlier draft let major rules be finalized by mid-2036; that was too late. The Congressional Review Act lookback for a Congress starting January 2037 reaches back to about summer 2036, but the bigger risk is a 2037 administration declining to defend rules still in litigation, as in 2025. The goal is final judgments, not just final rules, before 2037:
  - **Core in statute.** Caps, allowance or credit formulas, fleet standards, fee rates and dividend mechanics are self-executing; no rule is needed to start.
  - **Implementing rules by 2031,** under 12-24 month statutory deadlines with default standards if missed, pre-drafted by transition teams in 2027-28.
  - **Litigation resolved by 2034-35,** with challenges channeled to the D.C. Circuit on short filing windows and expedited review. Tradeoff: expedited review reaches the Supreme Court before its median shifts, but statutory numbers are far less exposed to the major-questions doctrine than rules.
  - **Nothing major finalized after 2033** except updates the statute requires.

## Gasoline: phase-in and what it shows about Dec. D7
Slowing the fee on gasoline costs about 1% of the plan's avoided emissions, gives up about $0.27T of revenue and buys a large political gain; the same logic largely supports D7's choice to exempt gasoline from the fee altogether (estimate).
**Session schedule.** No gasoline fee in 2030-31; $10/t in 2032, rising to reach the general rate by 2040, with an accelerator if transport misses its statutory targets. Collected at the fuel terminal like the existing excise.
| Year | Added cost per gallon, full fee | Slower phase-in |
| 2030 | 22¢ | 0¢ |
| 2033 | 52¢ | 18¢ |
| 2035 | 70¢ | 36¢ |
| 2037 | 89¢ | 62¢ |
| 2040 | ~95¢ | ~95¢ |

All figures are estimates on the session's $25-to-$100 path, at about 8.9 kg CO₂ per gallon.
**Effects**
  - **Emissions:** about 20-40 Mt/yr more in the mid-2030s, 0.3-0.5 Gt through 2046; 2035 moves from about −62% to −61%. Transport cuts come from the sales standard and trade-in rebates, not the pump price.
  - **Revenue:** gasoline fee revenue falls from about $0.51T to $0.25T over 2030-38; dividends fall about $50-70 per person a year at a 70/30 split.
  - **Inflation:** about 0.2 points lower in 2030, when the pump price would otherwise rise.
  - **Distribution:** shifts toward rural and exurban drivers; slightly less progressive by income.
  - **Politics:** the 2030 midterm happens with checks arriving and pump prices unchanged.
  - **Recovery:** about $15-20B more for trade-in rebates over the decade offsets the lost tons.
**Reconciling with D7.** D7 exempts gasoline and diesel entirely and taxes them through Moving America's fuel-tax steps. On the session's analysis that costs little in emissions, because the vehicle standard does the work; the open question is only whether Moving America's steps reach a comparable price by the late 2030s. Options:
  - **D7 as decided (baseline):** no fee on road fuels.
  - **D7 plus a convergence date:** road fuels join REV-C1 in about 2040, once most vehicles are electric. A departure from D7 that needs a decision.
  - **Session phase-in:** gasoline from 2032. A larger departure; not recommended over D7 given the small emissions gain.

## Accelerating and locking in, 2032-2036
The aim for 2032-36 is irreversibility by January 2037: long-lived fossil equipment replaced, a large pipeline already under construction, clean technology cheaper without subsidy, and enough stakeholders that repeal is expensive. These measures raise the 2045 floor under a 2037 repeal from about 75% to about 85% below 2005 (estimate).
**1. Fix the slow start (2029-31)**
  - **Reuse the IRA's text and Treasury guidance** for restored credits so projects move in weeks (CLI-1).
  - **Build a shovel-ready pipeline in 2027-28** with states, utilities and developers; pre-draft rules.
  - **Spend through existing channels first:** DOE loans, federal procurement, TVA and the power marketing administrations, state energy offices.
  - **Surge bonus:** richer credits for construction starts in 2030-33, stepping down after.
**2. Build the 2032-36 wave**
  - **Start-of-construction grandfathering:** a project begun before any repeal keeps its full credit stream. Target: 400 GW or more under construction in January 2037.
  - **A nuclear fleet order** on France's Messmer model: a federal contract (TVA, public power, contracts for difference) for 20-30 reactors of one or two proven designs, signed by 2034.
  - **Geothermal and long-duration storage** advance purchase commitments with volume targets.
  - **Deployment-indexed incentives:** bonus credits rise automatically if a sector misses its 2032, 2034 or 2036 milestone.
  - **Workforce:** large-scale apprenticeship, interstate electrician license reciprocity, and possibly trade visas (contested).
**3. Replace fossil equipment at natural turnover**
  - **New homes:** all-electric or electric-ready construction as a condition of Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and FHA mortgages from about 2032, reaching roughly 6-7 million homes by 2036 (estimate).
  - **Furnaces and water heaters:** point-of-sale rebates plus statutory standards; about a third of homes change heating systems in 2032-36 (estimate).
  - **Steel:** tie contracts for difference and the product standard to the rebuild dates of the roughly dozen active blast furnaces.
  - **Industrial boilers and kilns:** contracts and fee escalation timed to replacement cycles.
  - **New gas plants after ~2031:** capture-ready with a statutory capture date, or a retirement date.
  - **Retired coal plants:** demolition or conversion (often to storage on the existing interconnection) so they cannot be mothballed and restarted.
  - **Neighborhood gas retirement:** fund utilities to electrify whole blocks instead of replacing old pipe.
**4. Reach cost parity without subsidy by 2036:** EVs near $25,000, cheaper heat-pump installs, clean steel and cement near parity, standardized reactors. Contested option: letting foreign automakers, including Chinese firms, build EVs in U.S. plants under strict content, data and ownership rules.
**5. Multiply stakeholders**
  - **Households:** at least half with a direct stake by 2036 beyond the dividend (EV, heat-pump, rooftop, plug-in or community solar owners). Rooftop solar owners have beaten net-metering rollbacks across party lines in Arizona and Florida.
  - **Ownership:** co-op and community solar incentives, employee ownership in new factories, retail climate savings bonds.
  - **Red-state concentration:** siting bonuses so governors and members in Republican states defend the projects.
**6. Utilities and finance:** performance-based regulation (CLI-6), FERC clean-capacity markets, a Fannie/Freddie secondary market for clean home loans, and sovereign green bonds.
**7. Trade and states**
  - **Make the U.S. fee count abroad:** agreements under which REV-C1 counts against the EU's border charge, so repeal would immediately cost U.S. exporters.
  - **The 2032 USMCA joint review:** a natural opening to write carbon-intensity terms into North American trade.
  - **Mirror laws:** grants rewarding states that write matching targets into state law by 2034.
**8. Reliance in the legal record.** Under the 2020 DACA decision, an agency rescinding a policy must weigh the reliance interests built on it. Every contract, factory, loan and household investment by 2036 strengthens that record; the statutory core still needs Congress to change.
**Main risk.** Execution capacity in 2030-33. Nearly every lock-in measure depends on hitting the 2032 milestones, which is why the day-one channels, the pre-built pipeline and the workforce program matter as much as the headline policies.

**Economic effects (estimate)**
  - **Growth:** studies of fee-and-dividend designs find long-run GDP effects of a few tenths of a percent either way; investment adds near-term demand. Best guess: roughly neutral to slightly positive over the decade.
  - **Investment:** clean investment runs about $70B a quarter today; it could reach $500-600B a year by the mid-2030s.
  - **Labor:** gains in construction, electrical work, manufacturing and grid services; losses in coal and later oil and gas. Skilled trades are the binding constraint.
  - **Inflation and rates:** about 0.2 points a year during the fee phase-in, plus demand pressure from deficit-financed investment; possibly somewhat higher interest rates.
  - **Household energy:** electricity bills may rise modestly in the early 2030s, then fall as fuel costs leave the system. With the dividend, most lower- and middle-income households come out ahead; the D3 test still needs the project's incidence model run on the final design.
  - **Regions:** Sun Belt, Plains and industrial Midwest gain. New Mexico, Wyoming, North Dakota and West Virginia face state budget losses from severance revenue and need a state fiscal-transition fund.
  - **Trade:** energy exports, including LNG, need a fee rebate at export to avoid disputes.
  - **Markets:** coal written down but cushioned by securitization; oil and gas reprice gradually on a known path; utilities gain rate base.
**Benefits.** At the Biden-era EPA's roughly $190/t social cost of carbon, 40 Gt avoided is worth about $7-8T in avoided global damages, before air-quality health benefits. The current administration has abandoned that valuation, and the figure is sensitive to the discount rate.
