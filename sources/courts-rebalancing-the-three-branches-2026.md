# Rebalancing the Three Branches: A Constitutional and Administrative-Law Review of U.S. Court Reform Proposals (2026)

The strongest reform agenda is not one that starts by adding seats to the Court. It starts with reforms that rest on Congress's clearest Article I and Article III powers and on long-used tools: judgeships, circuit and venue design, ethics and disclosure statutes, jurisdiction channeling, remedies law, and statutory repairs of Chevron, the VRA and reproductive rights. Congress should rebuild its own capacity to draft and defend those repairs, and hold the most escalatory and constitutionally contested reforms (Court expansion, statutory term limits for sitting justices, supermajority voting rules) as a second tier to be used deliberately, not first.

## TL;DR

- **Congress has more power over the federal courts than current political culture assumes.** It has set the Court's size seven times, restructured appellate review (Evarts Act 1891, Judges' Bill 1925), created and abolished specialized courts (Emergency Court of Appeals 1942, TECA 1971, Federal Circuit 1982), and channeled review of whole regulatory programs to single courts (Yakus v. United States, 1944). The binding limits are narrower than the rhetoric: Congress cannot dictate outcomes in pending cases (Klein, Plaut), cannot reduce judges' pay or remove them except by impeachment, and, after Jarkesy and Trump v. Slaughter, it faces new limits on moving adjudication out of Article III courts and on insulating officials from presidential removal.
- **The record of 2013–2026 shows all three failure modes the user identified.** (1) Judicial overreach: Shelby County, West Virginia v. EPA, Sackett, Ohio v. EPA, Loper Bright, Corner Post, Jarkesy, Dobbs, and Louisiana v. Callais (April 29, 2026), which Justice Kagan said "renders Section 2 all but a dead letter." (2) Congressional abdication: no statute codifying Roe in 49 years, and the 1995 cut of House committee staff by one-third plus the abolition of OTA. (3) Failures to check the executive: Trump v. United States (2024) and an emergency docket that had granted 83% of the second Trump administration's decided applications by October 2025. The Court also did check the President in Learning Resources v. Trump (tariffs, February 2026) and Trump v. Barbara (birthright citizenship, June 2026).
- **Recommended sequencing:** first, a bipartisan "institutional integrity" package (enforceable ethics and recusal, a JUDGES-Act-style judgeship expansion phased across administrations, statutory random assignment and venue reform, emergency-docket transparency, an Article I immigration court, restoration of OTA and congressional staff). Second, targeted statutory repairs (a VRA Section 2 rewrite, deference and statute-of-limitations fixes to the APA, codified reproductive rights, remedies and class-action reform after CASA). Third, structural reforms (18-year terms, with a constitutional amendment as the durable backstop; Court expansion only as a response to further escalation), each designed to survive review by the very Court it would reform.

## Key Findings

1. **Congress's structural power over the judiciary is historically routine, not radical.** The Court's size changed in 1789 (6), 1801 (5, repealed in 1802), 1807 (7), 1837 (9), 1863 (10), 1866 (7) and 1869 (9). Ex parte McCardle (1869) upheld Congress's repeal of the Court's appellate jurisdiction over a pending habeas appeal. The Evarts Act created the circuit courts of appeals, and the 1925 Judges' Bill made most of the docket discretionary. The Emergency Court of Appeals, TECA, the Tax Court, the Court of Federal Claims and the Federal Circuit show that Congress routinely creates specialized forums with exclusive jurisdiction.
2. **The Court's administrative-law turn systematically shifts power from Congress-plus-agencies to courts.** The major questions doctrine (West Virginia v. EPA, 2022), the end of Chevron (Loper Bright/Relentless, 2024), the limitations reset (Corner Post, 2024), the Seventh Amendment limit on agency penalties (Jarkesy, 2024) and more aggressive arbitrary-and-capricious review (Ohio v. EPA, 2024) together raise the cost of every future regulatory statute. The doctrine is not one-directional: in 2026 the Court used major-questions reasoning to strike down President Trump's IEEPA tariffs. But the net effect is to demand far more specificity from Congress, which reinforces the case for rebuilding congressional drafting capacity.
3. **The Court has signaled resistance to reforms aimed at itself.** In a July 2023 Wall Street Journal opinion-page interview with David B. Rivkin Jr. and James Taranto, Justice Alito said, "No provision in the Constitution gives them the authority to regulate the Supreme Court—period." The Court's November 2023 code has no enforcement mechanism. Callais was sent down to the lower courts on May 4, 2026, less than a week after it was decided, even though Rule 45.3 normally allows 32 days. Any statute touching tenure, voting rules or internal operations should be drafted assuming skeptical review.
4. **Trump v. Slaughter (June 29, 2026) changes the design space for new institutions.** The Court overruled Humphrey's Executor and held that "the Constitution's separation of powers prohibits Congress from requiring the President to have cause before removing a subordinate" who exercises executive power. New adjudicators (immigration judges, specialized regulatory judges) belong inside the judicial branch, or clearly outside the executive, not in "independent" executive bodies.
5. **Congress's capacity deficit is measurable.** A 1996 CRS report found that House committee staff "went from 2,001 in 1994 to 1,317 in 1995," a one-third cut. OTA, with a peak budget of $21.3 million in FY1995 and a statutory staff cap of 143, was "not funded beyond February 1996." Brookings data show GAO falling from 5,062 staff (1992) to 2,989 (2015) and CRS from 838 to 609. Capacity restoration is the cheapest, least legally risky and most bipartisan reform on this list, and a precondition for the statute-specific repairs a post-Chevron world demands.

## Details

### Part I: Diagnosis — Three Failure Modes

**(1) Where the Court has overstepped**

- *Environmental and regulatory statutes.* *West Virginia v. EPA* (2022) formally named the major questions doctrine and held that Clean Air Act §111(d) did not authorize generation shifting. *Sackett v. EPA* (2023) narrowed "waters of the United States" to waters with a "continuous surface connection," a reading Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence said departed from the statutory text covering "adjacent" wetlands. *Ohio v. EPA* (2024) stayed the "good neighbor" ozone rule on the emergency docket, relying on arbitrary-and-capricious reasoning about an issue the dissent said commenters had not properly raised. Together these cases read ambiguity, and sometimes clarity, against regulatory authority.
- *Deference and timing.* *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* and *Relentless v. Department of Commerce* (June 28, 2024) overruled Chevron and required "independent judgment" under APA §706. *Corner Post v. Board of Governors* (2024) held that the six-year period in 28 U.S.C. §2401(a) runs from a plaintiff's injury, so newly formed entities can challenge decades-old rules. *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) required a jury for securities-fraud civil penalties, calling into question agency adjudication of "legal" claims.
- *Arbitrary-and-capricious review.* A&C review has become a flexible tool used against administrations of both parties: *Department of Commerce v. New York* (2019, census citizenship question), *DHS v. Regents* (2020, DACA rescission), *Ohio v. EPA* (2024); by contrast, in *FDA v. Wages and White Lion* (2025) the Court sided with the agency on a change-in-position claim. Bagley's "procedure fetish" critique applies: open-ended procedural review lets judges decide outcomes without owning the substance.
- *Voting rights.* *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) struck down the Section 4(b) coverage formula, leaving Section 5 preclearance inoperative. *Brnovich v. DNC* (2021) narrowed Section 2 vote-denial claims with "guideposts" found nowhere in the text. *Louisiana v. Callais* (argued March 24, 2025; reargued October 15, 2025; decided April 29, 2026, 6–3, Alito, J.) held Louisiana's second majority-Black district an unconstitutional racial gerrymander. CRS reads it as "significantly narrow[ing] the circumstances under which a racial vote dilution challenge" may proceed. Per the syllabus, Section 2 now "imposes liability only when the evidence supports a strong inference that the State intentionally drew its districts to afford minority voters less opportunity because of their race." That in effect replaces the 1982 amendments' results test with the intent standard Congress rejected when it overrode *City of Mobile v. Bolden*.
- *Precedent.* *Dobbs* (2022) overruled Roe and Casey. *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) overruled Humphrey's Executor; the Chief Justice wrote that stare decisis "is at its weakest in constitutional cases." Loper Bright overruled Chevron. This willingness to overturn precedent is itself a reason to put important rights and structures into statutes rather than rely on judicial settlement.

**(2) Where the political branches abdicated**

- *Reproductive rights.* The Freedom of Choice Act was introduced repeatedly from 1989 onward and never received a floor vote. The Women's Health Protection Act passed the House in 2021 and 2022 but failed in the Senate (a 49–51 cloture vote in May 2022). For 49 years Congress treated Roe as a substitute for legislation.
- *Capacity.* The 104th Congress cut House committee staff by one-third, defunded OTA as a "budget trophy" saving about $20 million, and presided over a GAO decline from 4,958 staff (FY1993) to 3,341 (FY1997). The Legislative Branch Appropriations Subcommittee chair justified eliminating OTA because "The CRS has a science division… GAO has a science capability… They can do the same thing as OTA." In practice lawmakers lost in-house technical expertise just as regulatory statutes grew more complex, and lobbyists and agencies filled the gap.
- *Delegation by default.* Broad, aging delegations (Clean Air Act 1970/1990, IEEPA 1977, the Communications Act 1934) left agencies and presidents to improvise. Under Chevron the Court absorbed those gaps; now the Court fills them itself.

**(3) Where the political branches overstepped and the Court failed to check them**

- *Presidential immunity.* *Trump v. United States* (July 1, 2024) recognized absolute immunity for core constitutional powers and presumptive immunity for other official acts, and excluded official-act evidence from prosecutions. It is the clearest example of the Court widening, rather than checking, executive power.
- *Emergency docket.* As of October 1, 2025, the Court had fully granted 19 of the Trump administration's emergency applications, 83% of those decided. The administration filed 34 applications by the end of 2025, compared with 19 across the Biden administration and 8 across the combined Bush and Obama administrations. The Court usually gave little or no reasoning; stays such as *Trump v. Wilcox* (May 2025) effectively previewed the overruling of Humphrey's Executor before merits briefing.
- *Counter-examples.* The Court did check the President in *A.A.R.P. v. Trump* (2025, Alien Enemies Act due process), *Learning Resources v. Trump* (February 20, 2026, 6–3: "IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs") and *Trump v. Barbara* (June 30, 2026: Executive Order 14160 on birthright citizenship held unconstitutional). A fair reading: the Court checks the President on questions it considers legally easy or central to its own authority, and gives far more room on personnel, spending, immigration enforcement and removal, exactly where Congress's statutory design choices are most exposed.

### Part II: The Reform Universe — Reform-by-Reform Analysis

#### 1. Supreme Court expansion

- **Problem:** A 6–3 majority entrenched by vacancy timing, including the 2016 Garland blockade and the 2020 Barrett confirmation.
- **Mechanism:** Amend 28 U.S.C. §1. The Judiciary Act of 2021 (Markey/Johnson/Nadler/Jones) and the Judiciary Act of 2023 would set the Court at 13 justices, one per circuit.
- **Precedent:** Strong. Seven size changes between 1789 and 1869, often partisan: the 1801 Federalist lame-duck reduction, and the 1866 cut to deny Andrew Johnson appointments. FDR's 1937 plan failed politically, not legally.
- **Pathway:** Statute. The Presidential Commission found broad congressional authority over size but took no position because of "profound disagreement."
- **Risks:** Highest escalation risk on the list; tit-for-tat expansion is easy to foresee. In Doerfler and Moyn's terms it is a personnel reform that leaves the Court's power intact. It likely requires ending the filibuster, and public support is thin and polarized: a Marquette Law School Poll (September 2–9, 2026) found expansion "favored by 53% and opposed by 47%," including just 35% of Republicans, compared with 79% support for term limits and 89% for a strict ethics code.
- **Likely Court response:** Unlikely to strike down a size change; there is no plausible doctrinal hook. The realistic danger is political: a legitimacy crisis and future retaliation.

#### 2. Lower-court expansion (judgeships)

- **Problem:** No comprehensive judgeship legislation since 1990 and no new district judgeship since 2003.
- **Mechanism:** The JUDGES Act of 2024 (S. 4199) would have created 63 permanent and 3 temporary district judgeships over 10 years. It passed the Senate by unanimous consent and the House 236–173, and President Biden vetoed it on December 23, 2024. JUDGES Act of 2025 bills (H.R. 1702, H.R. 1929) are pending.
- **Precedent:** Routine, most recently the Omnibus Judgeship Act of 1990.
- **Pathway:** Statute.
- **Risks:** Low, if phased across presidential terms. Biden's veto shows the timing problem: each party supports judgeships only when it expects to fill them.
- **Likely Court response:** None; the Judicial Conference itself requests them.
- **Assessment:** Pass a phased bill with effective dates straddling at least two presidential terms as a bipartisan good-faith gesture.

#### 3. Circuit reform and new circuits

- **Problem:** According to CRS Report R48242, the Ninth Circuit "has a current population of 67.1 million, making it the most populous of the 12 regional appellate circuits," with 29 authorized judgeships against the Fifth Circuit's 17. The Fifth Circuit has become the main destination for forum-shopped challenges to federal policy.
- **Mechanism:** Split circuits, add appellate judgeships, or change en banc rules (the Ninth already uses limited en banc).
- **Precedent:** Strong. The 1980 Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals Reorganization Act created the Eleventh Circuit; the Hruska (1973) and White (1998) Commissions studied splitting the Ninth; the Federal Circuit was created in 1982.
- **Pathway:** Statute.
- **Risks:** A split drawn to change a circuit's ideological balance invites retaliation. Splitting the Fifth (e.g., separating Texas) would concentrate rather than dilute one state's influence unless paired with new judgeships.
- **Likely Court response:** No judicial obstacle.
- **Assessment:** Treat circuit design as a caseload question governed by neutral criteria (population, filings per judgeship).

#### 4. Jurisdiction stripping (general)

- **Problem:** The Court's final say on contested policy. Doerfler and Moyn argue for "disempowering reforms" that "take power away from the Court and redirect it to the political branches."
- **Mechanism:** Use the Exceptions Clause (Art. III, §2) to remove appellate jurisdiction over categories of cases, or define lower-court jurisdiction (Sheldon v. Sill, 1850).
- **Precedent:** *Ex parte McCardle* (1869); *Lauf v. E.G. Shinner* (1938, upholding Norris-LaGuardia's limits on labor injunctions); *Yakus* (1944); the Detainee Treatment Act and Military Commissions Act (limited in *Boumediene*, 2008); *Patchak v. Zinke* (2018, plurality upholding a statute stripping jurisdiction over one pending land-trust case).
- **Pathway:** Statute, within limits. Congress may not (a) direct a rule of decision in a pending case (*United States v. Klein*, 1872), though *Bank Markazi v. Peterson* (2016) lets it change the underlying law; (b) reopen final judgments (*Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm*, 1995); (c) suspend habeas outside the Suspension Clause (*Boumediene*); or, on the Hart-and-Wechsler "essential functions" view, leave constitutional rights with no judicial forum.
- **Risks:** Very high escalation and precedent risk: a future Congress could strip jurisdiction over civil-rights or environmental claims. Stripping federal jurisdiction sends cases to state courts, which may be worse.
- **Likely Court response:** Precise stripping over statutory claims is probably upheld. Stripping that leaves constitutional claims with no forum invites the Court to adopt limits it has so far left open.
- **Assessment:** Use narrowly and pair with an alternative forum.

#### 5. Jurisdiction channeling for administrative review (the more promising version)

- **Problem:** After Loper Bright and Corner Post, any rule can be challenged anywhere, at any time, by a newly formed plaintiff, before a hand-picked judge.
- **Mechanism:**
  - Channel pre-enforcement review of rules into a single court of appeals (typically the D.C. Circuit) with a short filing window, modeled on Clean Air Act §307(b) (60 days) and the Hobbs Act.
  - Bar collateral attacks in enforcement proceedings.
  - Override Corner Post so claims accrue at publication, not at a particular plaintiff's injury.
  - Write standard-of-review provisions into organic statutes: "the Administrator's reasonable interpretation of [term] shall be upheld."
- **Precedent:** Strong. CAA §307(b); Hobbs Act (28 U.S.C. §2342); the Emergency Price Control Act's Emergency Court of Appeals, upheld in *Yakus*; TECA under the 1971 Economic Stabilization Act amendments; *Thunder Basin Coal v. Reich* (1994). *Axon v. FTC* (2023) held structural constitutional challenges are not implicitly channeled, so channeling must be explicit.
- **Pathway:** Statute; the most constitutionally secure way to discipline administrative litigation.
- **Risks:** Moderate. Short windows cut off later-injured parties, raising due-process arguments; concentrating review makes the D.C. Circuit's composition a political prize.
- **Likely Court response:** Probably upheld, with *Axon*-style carve-outs for structural claims and Jarkesy limits on agency penalties.
- **Assessment:** A top-tier reform.

#### 6. Codifying deference (the "Chevron fix")

- **Problem:** Loper Bright.
- **Mechanism:** (a) A general APA amendment directing deference to reasonable agency interpretations, as in the Stop Corporate Capture Act (Jayapal, first introduced 2021; Warren Senate version, July 23, 2024) and the 2025 EXPERTS Act (Warren/Jayapal). (b) Better: statute-specific express delegations. Loper Bright itself recognized that Congress may "expressly delegate" discretionary authority, "subject to constitutional limits."
- **Precedent:** Congress has written standards of review before: substantial evidence, the APA §706 categories, Skidmore-type provisions.
- **Pathway:** Statute, but there is a live argument (Justices Thomas and Gorsuch; critics in the op-ed literature) that mandatory deference violates Article III's "judicial power."
- **Risks:** A general rule invites direct confrontation with a Court that just held interpretation to be the judiciary's core function. Express, subject-specific delegations fit Loper Bright's own terms.
- **Likely Court response:** A broad "courts shall defer" statute has a meaningful chance of being narrowed or struck down. Express delegations ("the Secretary shall define…") will likely be honored, subject to the major questions doctrine and a possible nondelegation revival.
- **Assessment:** Draft delegations explicitly and specifically, which requires the capacity discussed in Reform 20.

#### 7. Specialized administrative or regulatory courts

- **Problem:** Generalist judges applying independent judgment to technical regulatory questions.
- **Mechanism:** An Article III "Court of Regulatory Appeals," modeled on the Federal Circuit, with exclusive jurisdiction over rule challenges, or exclusive jurisdiction for an existing court (the D.C. Circuit).
- **Precedent:** Strong. The Commerce Court (1910–1913, abolished after controversy, a cautionary example), the Emergency Court of Appeals, TECA, the Federal Circuit (1982), the Court of International Trade, the Tax Court (Board of Tax Appeals 1924, Article I court 1969), and the Court of Federal Claims (Court of Claims 1855, Claims Court 1982, renamed 1992).
- **Pathway:** Statute. Article III is safest; Article I courts face the *Northern Pipeline*, *Stern v. Marshall* and now *Jarkesy* limits on "private rights."
- **Risks:** Capture by repeat players (a critique leveled at the Federal Circuit in patent law); appointments become a flashpoint; the Commerce Court shows Congress can later abolish a court it dislikes.
- **Likely Court response:** No constitutional problem for an Article III court; the Supreme Court keeps certiorari review unless Congress also strips it.
- **Assessment:** Pair with channeling (Reform 5).

#### 8. Judicial ethics: binding code, recusal, enforcement, gifts, disclosure

- **Problem:** Undisclosed gifts and travel from wealthy benefactors (ProPublica reporting on Justices Thomas and Alito, 2023). The November 13, 2023 Code of Conduct has no enforcement mechanism, and each justice decides his or her own recusal.
- **Mechanism:**
  - The Supreme Court Ethics, Recusal, and Transparency Act of 2025 (S. 1814 / H.R. 3513, Whitehouse/Johnson, introduced May 20, 2025) would require a binding code, an investigation process, review of recusal motions by other judges, written recusal explanations, tighter gift and travel disclosure, and amicus funding disclosure.
  - The Supreme Court Ethics and Investigations Act (H.R. 7692) would create an Office of Investigative Counsel whose reports go to Congress.
- **Precedent:** Strong. Congress already applies 28 U.S.C. §455 (recusal) and the Ethics in Government Act of 1978 (disclosure) to the justices, sets gift and outside-income limits, and created the Judicial Conduct and Disability Act of 1980 for lower courts. Alito himself says he "voluntarily" complies with disclosure laws.
- **Pathway:** Statute (Necessary and Proper Clause, plus Congress's power over judicial structure and funding).
- **Risks:** Low political risk; this is the most popular reform. The legal risk is at enforcement: an outside body sanctioning a justice, or overriding a recusal decision, raises separation-of-powers objections.
- **Likely Court response:** Disclosure, gift rules and transparency are likely upheld, since the Court has complied for decades. Binding outside recusal decisions or inspector-general investigations are the likely points of resistance, with Alito's "no provision… period" as the stated view. Lower-risk design: have the Court's own members decide recusal motions with published reasons; enforce through disclosure and referral to Congress, not sanctions; and use the appropriations power (e.g., conditions on travel reimbursement funds).

#### 9. Term limits (18-year staggered terms)

- **Problem:** Actuarial randomness decides who controls the Court, and each nomination carries extreme stakes.
- **Mechanism:** Appointments every two years (first and third years of each presidential term), with justices moving to "senior" status after 18 years. The bills:
  - TERM Act (Rep. Hank Johnson; Whitehouse et al., 2022).
  - Supreme Court Term Limits and Regular Appointments Act of 2025 (H.R. 1074, Khanna): "the nine most junior Justices" hear cases, and Senate consent is deemed waived if the Senate does not act.
  - Supreme Court Biennial Appointments and Term Limits (BATL) Act (Whitehouse, reintroduced with 10 cosponsors): justices past 18 years hear only original-jurisdiction cases.
  - Welch–Manchin constitutional amendment (2024) and H.J.Res. 174 (119th Congress).
- **Precedent:** Moderate. Senior status for lower-court judges (28 U.S.C. §371) and historical circuit-riding assignments support Congress defining a judge's duties. No statute has ever limited a justice's service.
- **Pathway:** Contested. The Commission found scholars "divided"; some commissioners favored an amendment because a statute would be judged by its own subjects: "No matter which way the Court came out… the Court's legitimacy… would be undermined." Prospective application to new appointees is much safer than application to sitting justices. Deemed consent is the most vulnerable piece, because the Appointments Clause requires Senate "advice and consent."
- **Risks:** Senate refusal to confirm, the transition, the chief justiceship, counting senior justices. A 2+2 rhythm could still let one president seat a majority over two terms.
- **Likely Court response:** Uncertain for prospective application; likely hostile if applied to sitting justices against their will under "hold their Offices during good Behaviour." Pairing a statute with a proposed amendment signals good faith.
- **Assessment:** A prospective statute plus a constitutional amendment is the most defensible combination.

#### 10. Rotation and panel systems

- **Problem:** Same as term limits, plus a small, celebrity-driven institution.
- **Mechanism:** Epps and Sitaraman's "Supreme Court Lottery" would make every court of appeals judge an associate justice, hearing cases in randomly drawn nine-member panels; their "Balanced Bench" would have ten party-split justices select five more from the lower courts. Other proposals draw circuit judges onto the Court for set terms.
- **Precedent:** Weak. Circuit riding (1789–1891) is a distant analogy; lower courts routinely use visiting and designated judges.
- **Pathway:** Statute, with heavy questions: "one supreme Court," whether circuit appointees are properly appointed justices, and partisan-balance requirements.
- **Risks:** High legal risk. Critics note random panels would yield 6–3 or 7–2 lineups in most draws, destabilizing precedent.
- **Likely Court response:** Probably hostile. Most useful as a design idea, e.g., rotating circuit judges onto a specialized regulatory court.

#### 11. Impeachment and the meaning of "good behaviour"

- **Mechanism:** Article III's "good Behaviour" and Article II, §4's "high Crimes and Misdemeanors." The House has impeached 15 federal judges and the Senate convicted 8, most recently Judge G. Thomas Porteous in 2010. The only justice impeached, Samuel Chase (1804–05), was acquitted, setting the enduring norm against impeachment for rulings.
- **Precedent:** *Nixon v. United States* (1993) held Senate trial procedures nonjusticiable.
- **Pathway:** Existing constitutional mechanism. A minority view holds "good Behaviour" permits removal by other means (e.g., scire facias); most scholars and practice reject it.
- **Risks:** Impeachment over decisions would destroy the Chase norm, and the other side would reciprocate; 2025 calls to impeach district judges who ruled against the Trump administration show the danger.
- **Assessment:** Reserve for corruption and serious misconduct; use ethics statutes for everything short of that.

#### 12. Immigration courts

The immigration courts are the clearest current case of an executive-controlled adjudicator under stress. On February 4, 2025 there were 726 permanent immigration judges (683 IJs and 43 assistant chief IJs); by February 2026 there were 520 IJs and 33 ACIJs, after nearly 100 firings plus resignations. TRAC/EOIR records show 279 IJs fired or resigned during FY2025–2026, about 38% of 735. TRAC reports that "at the end of August 2026, 3,092,988 active cases were pending before the Immigration Court," down from what EOIR described as "more than 4.18 million" earlier in FY2025, and in June 2026, 94% of asylum cases were denied. In 2026 the MSPB held IJs are "inferior officers" removable at will, abrogating statutory protections (*Jackler and Jaroch v. DOJ*, 2026 MSPB 3). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025) funded 10,000 ICE officers but capped immigration judges at 800.

- **(a) Moving cases into Article III courtrooms:** Restore district-court habeas and pattern-and-practice jurisdiction curtailed by the REAL ID Act of 2005 and 8 U.S.C. §1252, and give de novo review on questions of law and, after *Wilkinson v. Garland* (2024), mixed questions. Statute; moderate caseload burden on the circuits; likely upheld.
- **(b) Folding immigration adjudication into Article III entirely:** Hundreds of life-tenured immigration trial judgeships. Constitutionally clean but practically daunting: roughly 700–1,000 Senate confirmations, entrenchment of a single appointing president, lost flexibility. The strongest guarantee of dignity and independence, and the least feasible.
- **(c) An Article I court plus due-process reforms:**
  - *Mechanism:* The Real Courts, Rule of Law Act of 2026 (H.R. 7836, Lofgren/Raskin/Johnson/Goldman, March 5, 2026; S. 5094 in the Senate) would create a court "under article I" that "is not an agency of, and shall be independent of, the executive branch," with trial, appellate and administrative divisions, modeled on the Tax Court.
  - *Support:* The ABA, the Federal Bar Association ("with a current backlog of approximately 3.6 million cases… it is long past time to fix an ineffective system"), NAIJ and AILA. NAIJ says an Article I court "shields decision-making from political pressure."
  - *Due-process additions:* appointed counsel for children and people with serious mental illness; access to government evidence (A-files); limits on in absentia orders; bond hearings.
  - *Risks after Slaughter:* Judges' removal protection must survive Slaughter. The design should (1) place the court clearly outside the executive, as the bill does; (2) give fixed terms with for-cause removal, analogous to the Tax Court; and (3) include a fallback folding the court into the judicial branch if removal protections are invalidated.
  - *Likely Court response:* Public-rights doctrine covers immigration (*Crowell v. Benson*; Jarkesy distinguished public rights), so a non-Article III forum is permissible. Removal protection is the vulnerable point.
- **Assessment:** Enact (c) now with expanded Article III review under (a); treat (b) as a long-term aspiration.

#### 13. Venue, judge shopping and standing

- **Problem:** Single-judge divisions (e.g., Amarillo; Waco for patents) let plaintiffs pick their judge. Standing has become asymmetric: *TransUnion v. Ramirez* (2021) denied standing to individuals whose statutory rights were violated, while states and companies gain access through theories like *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023, MOHELA) and Corner Post's fresh six-year clock for a newly formed company. (*FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, 2024, correctly rejected standing for doctors.)
- **Mechanism:**
  - Codify the Judicial Conference's March 2024 policy requiring district-wide random assignment for civil actions that "seek to bar or mandate state or federal actions, 'whether by declaratory judgment and/or any form of injunctive relief.'" It is only guidance, and some districts, including the Northern District of Texas, declined to adopt it.
  - Amend 28 U.S.C. §1391(e) so challenges seeking relief beyond the parties go to D.D.C. or to three-judge district courts (28 U.S.C. §2284) with direct appeal.
  - Create statutory causes of action conferring individual standing for statutory-rights violations, within Article III limits.
- **Precedent:** Congress sets venue and assignment rules; three-judge courts have been used for redistricting and, historically, for injunctions against statutes (1910–1976).
- **Pathway:** Statute.
- **Risks:** Low to moderate. D.D.C. concentration shifts rather than removes the stakes; expanded standing runs into *Spokeo*/*TransUnion*.
- **Likely Court response:** Venue and assignment rules upheld; expanded standing statutes tested and partially cut back.

#### 14. Nationwide and universal injunctions after Trump v. CASA

- **Problem:** Before 2025, individual district judges issued nationwide injunctions against both parties' policies.
- **CASA (June 27, 2025):** Universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority granted in the Judiciary Act of 1789. The Court did not reach the birthright order's merits and left open class actions and state-plaintiff remedies. Plaintiffs promptly used Rule 23(b)(2) class certification (the New Hampshire class in *Barbara*), and the Court ultimately struck down the order on the merits (June 30, 2026).
- **Mechanism:** Congress can now set remedies directly: authorize vacatur under APA §706 (the Court has left open whether "set aside" means universal vacatur); specify when universal relief is available; or route such cases to three-judge panels. The House passed the No Rogue Rulings Act in 2025 to restrict such relief. A better approach is symmetrical: channel universal relief to three-judge courts with expedited review rather than eliminate it.
- **Pathway:** Statute; CASA rests on statutory interpretation, so Congress holds the pen.
- **Likely Court response:** Deference to Congress's remedial choices is likely, since CASA invited them.

#### 15. Emergency ("shadow") docket reform

- **Problem:** Consequential, unexplained rulings. Federal-government grant rates were 46% in Trump's first term, 31% under Biden, and 67% (SCOTUSblog) to 83% (Ballotpedia) in the second Trump term, depending on how cases are counted.
- **Mechanism:**
  - Require written explanations and recorded votes for any grant of a stay or injunction.
  - Require argument or expedited merits review when a stay stays in place beyond a set period.
  - Deny precedential effect to emergency orders without a reasoned opinion, responding to *Trump v. Boyle* (2025), which said interim orders "inform" how lower courts decide like cases.
  - Codify stay standards (currently *Nken v. Holder*) and require public reporting of docket statistics.
- **Precedent:** Congress wrote the All Writs Act and 28 U.S.C. §2101(f) and sets stay rules.
- **Pathway:** Statute.
- **Risks:** An "opinion" mandate could collide with the Court's control of its deliberations, and responses could be boilerplate.
- **Likely Court response:** Vote disclosure and some written explanation are probably accepted in practice, as the Court has begun doing voluntarily in some cases; rules dictating the substance of reasoning are vulnerable.

#### 16. Docket reform (mandatory jurisdiction, cert)

- **Problem:** By Erwin Chemerinsky's SCOTUSblog count, the Court "decided 56 cases with signed opinions after briefing and oral argument" in October Term 2024, whereas "in the 1980s, the court was deciding over 160 cases a year." Its self-selected agenda favors ideological case selection and certiorari before judgment.
- **Mechanism:** Restore some mandatory jurisdiction (e.g., over invalidations of federal statutes or circuit splits); require a minimum number of merits cases; create a certiorari screening division drawn from circuit judges; limit certiorari before judgment.
- **Precedent:** Strong: the 1925 Judges' Bill and the 1988 Supreme Court Case Selections Act.
- **Pathway:** Statute. **Risks:** Low; mandatory jurisdiction can also expand the Court's policy reach.
- **Likely Court response:** Compliance (as in 1925 and 1988), with possible resistance to outside cert screening.

#### 17. Supermajority requirements and legislative override

- **Problem:** Narrow 5–4 or 6–3 invalidations of federal statutes.
- **Mechanism:** Require a 7–2 (or two-thirds) vote to invalidate an Act of Congress, or a legislative override letting a congressional supermajority reinstate a statute (Canada's "notwithstanding" clause is a model).
- **Precedent:** None federally. North Dakota (four of five) and Nebraska (five of seven) require supermajorities to invalidate statutes.
- **Pathway:** A statutory voting rule is contested; an override requires an amendment.
- **Risks:** High. It could entrench bad statutes and weaken rights protection.
- **Likely Court response:** A statutory rule is likely held unconstitutional as dictating "the judicial Power." Doerfler and Moyn defend it as reallocating power, but it is the reform most likely to provoke direct confrontation.
- **Assessment:** Pursue only within a constitutional amendment package.

#### 18. Campaign finance after Citizens United

- **Problem:** *Citizens United* (2010), *SpeechNow* (D.C. Cir. 2010), *McCutcheon* (2014) and *FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate* (2022) enabled super PACs and dark money, including the confirmation spending the SCERT Act targets. The Court heard *NRSC v. FEC*, a challenge to coordinated party expenditure limits, in the 2025 Term.
- **Mechanism:**
  - *Statute:* the DISCLOSE Act (disclosure was upheld 8–1 in *Citizens United* itself); small-donor matching public financing (secure under *Buckley v. Valeo*, subject to *Arizona Free Enterprise*, 2011, which bars "trigger" matching); tighter anti-coordination rules; foreign-money limits (*Bluman v. FEC*, summarily affirmed 2012); shareholder-consent rules.
  - *Amendment:* the Democracy for All Amendment.
- **Risks:** Disclosure faces *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* (2021) "exacting scrutiny"; the amendment's prospects are remote.
- **Likely Court response:** Disclosure and public financing likely survive; spending limits will not.

#### 19. Judicial transparency and justices' papers

- **Problem:** Justices' papers are private property released on their own terms; the Court resists cameras.
- **Mechanism:** A Presidential Records Act-style regime (44 U.S.C. §2201 et seq.) for judicial working papers, released after a set period (e.g., 12–20 years or after a justice's death); mandated live audio and video of arguments (Cameras in the Courtroom Act; live audio has continued voluntarily since 2020); searchable electronic disclosure reports.
- **Precedent:** The Presidential Records Act of 1978 made presidential papers public property after *Nixon v. Administrator of General Services* (1977) upheld the Presidential Recordings and Materials Preservation Act.
- **Pathway:** Statute. **Risks:** Claimed chilling of deliberation; the Dobbs leak investigation shows internal sensitivities.
- **Likely Court response:** A prospective statute with a long embargo likely survives; the Presidential Commission was generally supportive of cameras.

#### 20. Changing legal culture and restoring Congress's capacity

- **Problem:** Legal education and judicial culture treat Congress as incompetent. The major questions doctrine presupposes that Congress rarely makes big decisions, which becomes self-fulfilling when Congress lacks staff to draft detailed statutes.
- **Mechanism:**
  - *Capacity:* Revive OTA; the OTA Improvement and Enhancement Act (Takano/Foster/Casten/Beyer) would rename it the Congressional Office of Technology, and the House Select Committee on the Modernization of Congress unanimously endorsed reestablishing "an improved" OTA. Restore GAO and CRS at least to 1992 levels (GAO 5,062; CRS 838). Raise pay caps and committee staffing, reversing a 35% decline in committee staff from 1994 to 2014 while leadership staff funding rose 89% (Pascrell, 2019). Create a nonpartisan office that tracks judicial interpretations of statutes and proposes override legislation.
  - *Culture:* Expand legislative drafting clinics (e.g., Georgetown's federal legislation clinic) and first-year "Legislation and Regulation" courses pioneered at Harvard; support legisprudence scholarship (Gluck and Bressman's *Statutory Interpretation from the Inside*, 2013; Victoria Nourse's *Misreading Law, Misreading Democracy*; Jeremy Waldron's *The Dignity of Legislation*); fund congressional clerkships parallel to judicial ones; normalize override statutes after statutory decisions, as Congress did after *Ledbetter* (2009) and in the Civil Rights Act of 1991.
- **Precedent:** The Legislative Reorganization Acts of 1946 and 1970 and the Congressional Budget Act of 1974 (which created CBO).
- **Pathway:** Statute and appropriations, entirely within Congress's control.
- **Risks:** Minimal legal risk; the political risk is "Congress spending on itself" optics.
- **Likely Court response:** None needed. Over time, a Congress that legislates with specificity weakens the premise of the major questions doctrine.

#### 21. Statutory repairs for specific decisions

- **VRA:** The John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act would restore preclearance with a coverage formula based on recent violations (answering *Shelby County*). After *Callais* it must also clarify that Section 2 is a results test and that remedial race-conscious districting is permitted, backed by an extensive Fifteenth Amendment enforcement record. Risk: Fourteenth Amendment strict scrutiny of race-conscious remedies.
- **Reproductive rights:** A Women's Health Protection Act-style statute resting on the Commerce Clause and §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. Risk: *Lopez*, *Morrison* and *City of Boerne* ("congruence and proportionality") challenges; commerce-based provisions (drugs such as mifepristone, interstate travel, provider services) are sturdier.
- **Environment:** Amend the Clean Water Act definition of "waters of the United States" to cover adjacent wetlands and a significant-nexus test; give EPA explicit authority over power-sector greenhouse-gas emissions, including generation shifting (answering *West Virginia*).
- **Agency adjudication:** After Jarkesy, let agencies seek civil penalties in district court, or restructure penalties within public-rights exceptions.
- **Immunity and executive accountability:** Congress cannot overrule *Trump v. United States* by statute, but it can legislate evidentiary procedures, strengthen inspectors general, enact criminal statutes that expressly reach presidential conduct, and propose an amendment (the "No One Is Above the Law" amendment proposed in 2024).
- **Independent agencies after Slaughter:** Rebuild bipartisan commissions with tools that do not depend on removal protection (staggered terms, partisan-balance requirements, quorum rules, appropriations and reporting conditions), or move quasi-judicial functions to Article III or Article I adjudicators.

### Part IV: Sequencing and Packaging

**Package 1: "Institutional Integrity and Capacity Act" (bipartisan, year one).** A judgeship bill phased across 2029–2039; ethics disclosure, gifts and amicus transparency, with recusal decided by the Court but publicly explained; statutory random assignment and a three-judge option for nationwide challenges; emergency-docket vote and reason disclosure; the Real Courts, Rule of Law Act; and OTA revival, GAO/CRS restoration and staff pay reform. Each item has had Republican sponsors or supporters (Tillis on judge shopping; Young and Lankford on judgeships; the Modernization Committee's unanimous OTA vote), and none changes who sits on the Court. Passing them first builds a good-faith record that lowers the escalation risk of later steps.

**Package 2: "Congress Legislates" (years one to two).** An APA modernization act (review channeling, a Corner Post fix, remedies law after CASA, penalties restructured after Jarkesy, express delegation templates); statute-specific repairs (CWA definitions, CAA §111 clarification, a VRA rewrite with an extensive record); and the Women's Health Protection Act. These use Package 1's capacity and test the Court's formalism on its own terms: if Congress speaks clearly and specifically, the major questions doctrine and Loper Bright should, by their own logic, uphold the statutes.

**Package 3: Structural reform (only if Package 1 and 2 reforms are struck down or escalation continues).** Prospective 18-year terms by statute plus a proposed amendment; a specialized Article III regulatory court; narrow channeling or stripping with an alternative forum; and Court expansion held in reserve, explicitly conditioned on further escalation, for example as a contingent provision that takes effect only if term limits are struck down, which gives the Court an incentive to uphold the milder reform.

## Recommendations

1. **Lead with capacity and integrity, not seats.** These reforms are the cheapest, most bipartisan and most constitutionally secure, and they directly answer the "Congress abdicated" diagnosis.
2. **Answer Loper Bright with specific statutes, not a general deference mandate.**
3. **Design every new adjudicator to survive Slaughter**, with fallback clauses.
4. **Treat CASA as an invitation** to legislate a symmetrical, channeled route to universal relief.
5. **Pursue term limits prospectively, paired with an amendment**; avoid deemed Senate consent.
6. **Keep Court expansion and supermajority rules as deterrents, not openers.**
7. **Invest in legal culture**: congressional clerkships, drafting clinics, legislation courses.

## Caveats

- **2026 developments.** The descriptions of *Callais*, *Slaughter*, *Learning Resources* and *Barbara* come from the opinions' syllabi, CRS Legal Sidebars and case trackers; their doctrinal reach is still being tested in lower courts. The outcome of *NRSC v. FEC* was not confirmed for this review.
- **Emergency-docket figures depend on counting.** The 67% (SCOTUSblog, August 2025) and 83% (Ballotpedia, October 2025) grant rates cover different sets of cases and dates.
- **Immigration-judge counts differ by source.** NPR (726 → 553 permanent IJs plus ACIJs), TRAC/EOIR (279 departures of 735) and AIC (735 → 600) use different dates and definitions.
- **Capacity figures differ by counting method.** CRS (House committee staff 2,001 → 1,317) and Brookings (2,147 → 1,266) count differently; the current CRS headcount (about 600) is approximate.
- **Constitutional predictions are judgment, not certainty.** Statutory term limits, supermajority rules and deference mandates raise questions the Court has never decided, and the justices could surprise in either direction.
- **Political feasibility.** Almost all Tier 2 and Tier 3 reforms require unified government with filibuster reform or bipartisan buy-in; as of September 2026, none of the reform bills cited has been enacted.
