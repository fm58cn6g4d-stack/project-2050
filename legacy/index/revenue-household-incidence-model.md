Espen Swanson · Generated by models/tools/incidence-model.py, incidence-years.py and incidence-make-md.py from the ledger (base $5.85T, gap $5.87T, about $0.86T after the accepted options). Role: index. Regenerate it rather than editing by hand.

Use [the integrated education and fiscal recovery bridge](fiscal-recovery-model.md) for current selected-scope costs and conditional financing. Old “held education” and “decided package” labels below describe the earlier vintage; they do not reverse that adoption or establish a current financing/D3/D4 pass. Exact new rates and transition finance await decision.

This is the test D3 sets: every income group below the top comes out ahead on net from the agenda's combined taxes, fees and benefits, and new general-fund revenue comes mostly from the top 1% and corporations. The unit is the bottom four quintiles, with the top quintile split out. Figures are average dollars per household per year over 2030-39 (nominal), from ten-year totals divided across about 140M households (estimate). Every distribution share is an **estimate** anchored to a published pattern; no row is yet a microsimulation.

**The decided package (scenario Q)** is the base, Package B (C18, C35, C19b, C20), the top-end sprint package C38 (REV-TS1 to TS11), and C39-C41 (REV-TS12, a 28% cap on itemized deductions above $450K; REV-HP1, the CEO pay-ratio corporate surtax; REV-HP3, an excise on top-officer pay). No program trim is taken, so universal meals (EDU-3) and Medicare dental stay. Every quintile below the top comes out ahead: Q1 +$11,906, Q2 +$10,094, Q3 +$6,110 and **Q4 +$1,139** a household a year on the ten-year average. 72% of new general-fund revenue comes from the top 1% and corporations. The fourth quintile is −$1,452 in 2030, +$413 in 2031 and +$1,629 by 2034.

**October 24 family adoption:** costs now include FAM-1D/AFA and one million new voucher awards. The existing CTC/housing distribution keys remain provisional proxies; this screen does not implement exact AFA tax losses, new funding or within-quintile effects. A positive aggregate result is not a completed distributional score for the new design.

## Bottom line

- **The retained allocation-key screen is positive on the ten-year average.** The fourth quintile gains about +$1,139 a household a year (0.6% of income); the first three gain +$11,906, +$10,094 and +$6,110.
- **The employer health-exclusion cap is the swing.** At the 75th percentile (C19b, decided) the fourth quintile gains +$1,139; at the median premium (C19, the fallback if the sprint package falls short) +$444. The hospital-price pass-through to wages is the assumption most able to flip the result: without it the fourth quintile lands at +$585.
- **The revenue-mix test passes** in every scenario: the top 1% (by incidence) and corporations (statutory) bear 72% of new general-fund revenue on the decided package and 86% on the base alone; the rule is more than half.
- **The 80th-99th percentiles pay about $16,155 a year** on net, where C18 and C20 land, and the top 1% about $671,324.
- **The education fund (EDU-9B, held) would pass as a closer in place of C19b and C20, but not for free in the fourth quintile.** Its contribution (about 1.4 points of payroll, all counted on workers) and added benefits give Q1-Q4 +$12,284, +$10,220, +$5,891 and +$301. Whether to use a middle-reaching contribution at all is a D3 and D4 decision, deferred to the revenue strategy session on payroll raisers.
- **Averages hide losers inside the fourth quintile.** A household there without children, without marketplace or Medicare coverage, and with employer coverage above the 75th-percentile premium gets few of the benefits and most of the costs. The next version needs within-quintile spread.

- **The fourth quintile is behind in 2030** on the decided package (−$1,452), and positive from 2031 on. The early shortfall is the carbon fee, leave contributions and the exclusion cap arriving before the public option and full child care.
- **Year five (2034) passes for every quintile below the top**, D3's interim test. The 2030 shortfall lands just before the November 2030 midterm.
- **Fixing 2030 is a sequencing choice, not a size choice.** The levers: start C19b and the carbon fee in 2031, front-load the Climate Dividend (pay the 2030 dividend at the 2031 rate from a Treasury advance repaid from later receipts), or start leave contributions with benefits in 2031. Each costs about a year of revenue and needs a D4 check.

## Open choices this model informs

These are options with their effects, not edits to any canonical file.

1. **The 2030 dip.** The sequencing levers above; none is decided.
2. **EDU-9B, the education fund (held).** As a closer in place of C19b and C20 it moves the fourth quintile from +$1,139 to +$301. It needs Espen's call under D3 (a middle-reaching contribution) and D4 (a new dedicated fund).
3. **SAF-R2.** Dropping the alcohol excise moves the fourth quintile from +$1,139 to +$1,196 and puts about $0.07T on the top-end base.
4. **The Climate Dividend.** Shifting part of the 20% transition share into the dividend (CLI-2C) helps every quintile equally.
5. **The hospital-price pass-through (H5p)** needs a sourced estimate before the result is final.

## Dynamic effects and the D3 test

The ledger carries a dynamic sensitivity table (not in any total): under CBO/JCT-style conventions macroeconomic feedback is about −$0.56T over 2030-39 (−$1.52T to $0.33T), and about $0.70T under Talarico-style assumptions (estimates). It does not change the quintile results above, for three reasons. **Most of it is revenue, not household money:** the largest row is lower investment and more avoidance by corporations and the top 1%, which lands on the top quintile and the gap, not on the bottom four. **The household-facing pieces roughly offset:** the care labor-supply gain raises earnings mostly in the second to fourth quintiles, while the small labor-supply drag from coverage and the Child Tax Credit falls on the same groups through slightly lower hours, a choice the household makes rather than a cost imposed on it. **Wage effects of corporate taxes are already in:** the model puts 25% of corporate taxes on workers (the JCT/CBO convention), which is the channel a dynamic score would otherwise add. If a dynamic gap had to be closed with middle-reaching revenue (C19 or C20) rather than top-end options, that choice would move the quintiles and should be run as its own scenario.

## Limits

- **Shares are estimates.** Each row uses a pattern key; replacing them with published TPC and CBO tables is the next version.
- **Year profiles are shapes, not scored annual paths.**
- **No behavior.** Coverage shifts, avoidance beyond the ledger's 15% haircuts, and macro effects are left out (the education session's maternal-employment effect, plausibly $25-45B a year of federal revenue at maturity, is not counted).
- **Public goods are left out** on both sides: the agency rebuild, transit service, climate credits to firms, defense, education research (EDU-8) and the civic and information rows.
- **Full pass-through** of carbon, border, transport and alcohol charges to households, which is conservative for the middle. EDU-9B's contribution is counted entirely on workers.
- **Corporate taxes** use the JCT/CBO convention: 25% labor, 75% capital.
- **Not modeled:** the minimum wage (LAB-4), the H-1B fee, COM-5's bank-fee limits (a household benefit), veterans health (NSC-10), and immigration's legalization effects.
