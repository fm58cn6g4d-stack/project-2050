# Instruments and Consequences

### A critical review of the Unified Reform Inventory, and an assessment of what the program would actually do

---

# Part I — Where the instrument is wrong

Most of the inventory is correctly specified. These are the items where the goal is right and the chosen mechanism is not, or where a better one exists.

### 1. House enlargement: cube root, not the Wyoming rule

The Wyoming rule pegs district size to the smallest state's population, which makes the size of the House **hostage to Wyoming's growth rate** — if Wyoming grows faster than the nation, the House *shrinks*. It also requires re-legislating the premise every time the smallest state changes.

**Tweak.** Adopt the cube-root rule — roughly 692 members — with automatic decennial recalculation written into the statute so it never needs relegislating. The cube root has international precedent, is self-adjusting, and delivers roughly 480,000 constituents per member against today's 760,000.

**Unaddressed constraint.** The House chamber seats about 448. Six hundred ninety-two members requires either chamber reconstruction or a distributed-voting architecture, and that is a multi-year capital project that has to start in the first Congress or the reform arrives with nowhere to sit.

### 2. Proportional representation: specify district magnitude

"Multi-member districts of three to five" is doing a lot of quiet work. A three-member STV district has an election threshold near 25%, which changes almost nothing — you get the same two parties with slightly better internal competition. A five-member district has a threshold near 17%, which is where viable third parties actually appear.

**Tweak.** Specify **five-member districts wherever population permits**, three only where geography forces it. If the goal is multi-party democracy rather than better primaries, magnitude is the variable that decides it.

**Also worth stating:** STV was chosen over mixed-member proportional deliberately. MMP requires party lists, which sits badly with candidate-centric American political culture and strengthens formal party organizations at a moment when they are distrusted. STV preserves individual candidate choice. That tradeoff should be argued, not assumed.

### 3. The filibuster: declining threshold beats talking filibuster

The talking filibuster has a known failure mode, which is why it collapsed historically: **it burdens the majority, not the minority.** Holding the floor requires one senator speaking; defeating it requires the majority to maintain a quorum around the clock. The romance of *Mr. Smith* obscures an asymmetry that runs the wrong direction.

**Tweak.** Adopt the **declining-threshold model** — cloture at 60, then 57, then 54, then a simple majority across successive votes separated by mandatory debate periods. It preserves genuine extended debate and real minority leverage over timing while guaranteeing eventual resolution. It is also far easier to defend as *restoration* rather than abolition, which is the frame that carries institutionalists.

Keep the talking filibuster as rhetoric. Legislate the declining threshold.

### 4. Judicial term limits: prospective-only, with severability

The senior-status mechanism faces a real objection — that moving a justice off active service constitutes removal from the office of Associate Justice, which "good behaviour" tenure forbids. I stated this too confidently before.

**Tweak.** Draft the statute so it applies **prospectively to newly appointed justices only**, with retroactive application as a *severable* provision. A justice appointed under a statute specifying eighteen years of active service consented to those terms, which moots the removal question entirely. The cost is that the reform takes eighteen years to bite. The benefit is that the core provision almost certainly survives, and if the retroactive severable clause is struck, the prospective regime remains standing rather than the whole statute falling.

This is a real trade of speed for durability, and given the fast-fragile geometry, durability is the right side of it.

### 5. Court expansion as deterrent: name the coercion problem

A public pre-commitment to expand if the Court strikes term limits is *itself* subject to the charge that it coerces judicial decision-making — and the Court can simply strike the statute and dare Congress to follow through.

**Tweak.** Pair the deterrent with structural insurance: aggressive severability clauses in every reform statute; a three-judge district court with direct Supreme Court appeal for challenges to the reform package, which speeds resolution and avoids forum-shopping; and bundling term limits into a larger judiciary bill (judgeships, ethics, case assignment) so striking the term-limits title carries collateral costs the Court would prefer to avoid.

The threat is the last line, not the first.

### 6. NPVIC: don't affirmatively seek consent

I had this backwards. Under *U.S. Steel v. Multistate Tax Commission*, compacts that do not encroach on federal supremacy require no congressional consent. **Affirmatively requesting consent concedes that it is required** and weakens the position if the request fails.

**Tweak.** If the votes exist, pass a resolution *confirming* the compact does not require consent — belt and suspenders, framed correctly. Otherwise, hold consent in reserve as a response to litigation rather than an invitation to it.

### 7. The 23rd Amendment rump: statutory, not just moot

I said the compact makes the three residual electors non-decisive. Better than that: the Twenty-third Amendment gives Congress full authority to direct the *manner* of appointment.

**Tweak.** The admission act should directly specify that the residual district's electors are appointed in accordance with the national popular vote winner, or not appointed at all. Contestable, but it converts a permanent constitutional anomaly into a solved statutory problem rather than a live one that a compact happens to neutralize.

### 8. Anti-impoundment: give standing to grantees, not Congress

Congressional standing is the weakest link after *Raines v. Byrd*. Congress rarely has the concrete, particularized injury Article III demands.

**Tweak.** Create a **statutory cause of action for the intended recipient** — the state, the grantee, the contractor. They have unambiguous injury, they are motivated to sue, and they do not carry the political baggage of an interbranch dispute. This is a straightforwardly better instrument.

### 9. Automatic continuing resolution: penalize Congress, not programs

An auto-CR at prior-year levels removes the shutdown threat but also removes any forcing function for appropriations at all — government runs on stale priorities indefinitely, which entrenches whichever coalition last passed a bill.

**Tweak.** Auto-CR at prior-year levels, paired with an **escalating congressional penalty**: no recess, no non-appropriations floor business, and suspended member travel until the bills pass. The penalty falls on the body that failed, not on the programs or the people who depend on them.

### 10. The funding base: mark-to-market, not a wealth tax

This is the most consequential correction in the review, because Section 11's wealth tax silently funds much of Section 10.

A wealth tax faces the direct-tax apportionment problem, which *Moore* declined to resolve; severe valuation difficulty for private assets; and a poor international record, with most European wealth taxes repealed.

**Tweak.** Substitute a package that is unambiguously constitutional income taxation and raises comparable revenue: **mark-to-market treatment of publicly traded assets for taxpayers above a high threshold, repeal of stepped-up basis at death, capital gains taxed at ordinary rates, and a strengthened corporate minimum.** Same distributional effect, a fraction of the legal risk, and no valuation regime to build.

### 11. AI dividend: pick the levy

I listed three options and declined to choose, which is not a plan. A data royalty requires inventing a property right in training data with enormous collateral consequences for fair use. A sovereign equity stake creates an unmanageable conflict between the government as regulator and as shareholder.

**Tweak.** A **levy on frontier compute or AI-attributable revenue, rebated per capita.** It uses existing tax machinery, requires no new property rights, scales with the thing being captured, and keeps regulator and beneficiary roles separate.

### 12. Local journalism: condition the credit on ownership

An uncomplicated payroll credit subsidizes exactly the hedge-fund-owned chains that are hollowing out local news.

**Tweak.** Condition eligibility on ownership structure — exclude private-equity-controlled chains, weight toward nonprofit and locally-held outlets — and add a one-time transition fund for conversion to nonprofit status. The goal is local journalism, not the balance sheets of its current owners.

### 13. Section 230: transparency and design, not liability

*Moody v. NetChoice* suggests algorithmic curation is protected expression, which makes amplification-based liability reform constitutionally fragile and likely to be enjoined for years.

**Tweak.** Pursue **transparency mandates, researcher access, default-setting requirements, and age-appropriate design** instead. Less satisfying, far more durable, and it survives First Amendment review.

### 14. National service: mixing is the mechanism

If the goal is the belonging argument, a national service program that places participants near home is a jobs program with a flag on it.

**Tweak.** Write **cross-regional and cross-class placement into the statute as a design requirement.** The civic benefit comes from who you serve alongside, not from the service.

### 15. Congressional capacity: pair it with the stock ban

"Congress votes itself more money" is politically radioactive on its own.

**Tweak.** Package staff funding with the congressional stock trading ban in a single bill. Accountability buys capacity. This is the pairing rule applied inside the structural track rather than across it.

# Part II — What the review surfaced as missing

### The critical one: presidential election method

**A multi-party legislature combined with a plurality-winner presidency is the most unstable configuration in comparative politics.** If STV works as intended and three to five parties emerge, and the NPVIC or a national popular vote amendment elects the president by simple plurality, the country can elect a president with 34% support facing a legislature in which no party holds a majority.

This is not a marginal risk. It is the predictable output of the reform package as currently written, and I built the entire agenda without noticing it.

**Required addition.** National ranked-choice voting or a two-round runoff for the presidency, written into the NPV amendment and into the compact's operative language. The compact currently awards electors to the national plurality winner. That was safe under a two-party system and becomes dangerous under exactly the system the rest of the agenda is designed to produce.

**Also add:**
- Presidential primary calendar reform — currently governed by party rules, which makes it the least accountable high-stakes process in American politics
- Fusion voting authorization, which offers a lower-cost path to multi-party dynamics and is currently banned in most states
- A constitutional convention *procedure* statute — Article V is silent on delegate apportionment and voting rules, and that gap is a crisis waiting for a trigger
- Emergency succession and continuity-of-government provisions for mass-casualty scenarios, which remain genuinely unresolved
- Statehood and territorial status for the remaining territories, sequenced rather than gestured at

# Part III — What the program would do

## Democracy

**Party system.** Five-member STV districts plus a larger House plus RCV in states produce a plausible three-to-five party legislature within two to three cycles. Expect a durable center-right party, a populist-right party, a mainstream Democratic party, a left party, and regionally variable others. Coalition formation becomes routine.

**Participation.** Automatic registration, restored preclearance, felony re-enfranchisement, and meaningful competition in every district plausibly add five to ten points of turnout — with the largest gains in currently safe districts, where roughly nine in ten voters presently have no consequential choice.

**Representation.** Every voter gains a representative they actually voted for. Gerrymandering becomes largely inoperative — not because it is banned but because it is arithmetically much harder in five-member districts.

**The honest cost.** Coalition government is slower and less legible than single-party control. Accountability diffuses. Voters who want to "throw the bums out" find it harder to identify which bums.

## Government and state capacity

**Congress recovers institutional weight.** Staff capacity, OTA, a functioning appropriations process, and 692 members with 480,000 constituents each is a materially different institution — closer to a legislature than a fundraising body. The paradox to watch: **larger chambers require stronger leadership to function**, so enlargement may concentrate rather than diffuse power unless the rules changes accompany it.

**The presidency is deliberately diminished.** Emergency powers sunset, impoundment is enforceable, the Insurrection Act is constrained, war powers default to Congress. This is the intended effect and it carries a real cost: genuine emergencies will be handled more slowly. Every constraint needs an explicit fast-path with a short sunset and mandatory reporting, or the reform will be broken the first time it binds badly.

**Delivery speed improves materially.** Civil service reform, permitting reform, and technology procurement are the difference between benefits arriving in nine months and twenty-six. This is the least visible cluster in the entire inventory and it gates roughly half of everything else.

## Economics

**Fiscal scale.** The delivery agenda runs roughly $600 billion to $1 trillion annually. The revised funding base — mark-to-market, stepped-up basis repeal, capital gains parity, carbon fee, AI levy, corporate minimum — plausibly raises $500 to $700 billion. **There is a gap, and it should be named rather than assumed away.**

**Growth effects, net positive.** Child care raises labor force participation. Non-compete elimination raises wages and firm formation. Permitting reform raises investment. Carbon pricing improves allocative efficiency. Antitrust enforcement raises dynamism.

**The sequencing risk, which is the most important economic finding here.** Large recurring transfers delivered into a supply-constrained economy is precisely the 2021–22 experiment. **The supply-side items — permitting, housing production, child care capacity, grid buildout — must lead or at minimum accompany the demand-side transfers**, not follow them. This directly contradicts the political logic in *The Calendar*, which front-loads dividends into the earliest vehicle because they are legible and recurring. That tension is real: the politically optimal sequence and the macroeconomically prudent sequence are not the same, and pretending otherwise is how a reform program produces the inflation that destroys it.

**Distribution.** Strongly progressive. Child poverty falls substantially — the 2021 credit cut it roughly 40% in a single year, and a permanent version compounds.

## Safety and security

**Domestic.** Insurrection Act reform, federal law enforcement identification requirements, and Posse Comitatus clarification materially reduce the risk of domestic military deployment against civilians. Election worker protections address a live and worsening threat.

**External.** War powers restoration slows unilateral military action, which is the point and also a real capability cost against fast-moving adversaries. Alliance architecture reform and rebuilt development finance are slow-acting soft-power investments with no near-term measurable return.

**AI.** Reporting thresholds and pre-deployment evaluation reduce catastrophic tail risk. The competitiveness objection is genuine and unresolved; the honest answer is that unilateral restraint has costs and international coordination is the only durable version.

**Climate.** Disaster response capacity and insurance backstops are the sharpest edge of the Double Unraveling — the point where climate damage converts directly into governance failure and lost legitimacy.

## Justice

**Voting rights.** Preclearance restoration plus a *Callais* response plus re-enfranchisement is the largest expansion of the franchise since 1965.

**A genuine tension worth naming.** STV usually improves minority representation in aggregate, but it eliminates the majority-minority district as a *guaranteed* mechanism. That will be read by some as trading a certain seat for a probabilistic one, and it is the reason the Congressional Black Caucus has historically been cool toward proportional systems. The §2 modeling must be commissioned before introduction, not after, and the answer has to be persuasive to the members whose districts are at stake.

**Courts.** Term limits produce a bench that tracks electoral outcomes over time rather than actuarial accident. Judgeships and random case assignment reduce forum-shopping and delay.

## Institutions

**Court legitimacy** likely improves under term limits and ethics reform, and would be damaged by expansion — which is the entire argument for the reciprocity test.

**Federalism** is the most under-examined cost in the program. Federal election standards, commission mandates, and conditional funding represent significant centralization. *NFIB v. Sebelius* makes the conditional-funding mechanism shakier than the inventory admits. The conservative objection here is principled and deserves a real answer rather than dismissal: the answer is that election administration for *federal* offices is an enumerated congressional power under the Elections Clause, and the program should stay scrupulously within that line rather than reaching for the spending power where it does not need to.

## Personal wellbeing

**Material.** Child poverty falls sharply. Health coverage expands. Utility burden falls. Wage floors rise.

**Time.** Paid leave and child care are, in practice, time transfers — the scarcest resource for working families and the one policy rarely addresses directly.

**Belonging** — Buttigieg's own frame, and the hardest to measure. National service with genuine mixing, civic infrastructure, and local journalism address the substrate of social trust rather than its symptoms. The honest assessment is that these are slow, weakly evidenced, and worth doing anyway because the alternative is treating the trust collapse as weather.

**Anxiety.** Reform periods are disruptive. A program this large generates real uncertainty for people whose lives depend on stable institutions, and the communication burden is not a footnote.

# Part IV — Adverse interactions

These are the failure modes that emerge from the program as a *system*, not from any single item.

**1. Multi-party legislature plus plurality presidency.** Already stated, and the most serious. Fixed only by presidential RCV, which must be added to the compact and the amendment both.

**2. Politically optimal and macroeconomically prudent sequencing diverge.** Front-loading legible transfers is right for 2030 and risks the inflation that ends the program. Supply-side capacity has to move in parallel, which costs floor days the calendar says are scarce.

**3. Reciprocity failure.** The entire architecture assumes reforms are accepted as neutral. If they are received as partisan capture, the next trifecta reverses and escalates, and the country ends up with a ratchet rather than a settlement. This is why the reciprocity test is load-bearing and why Court expansion has to stay a deterrent.

**4. Implementation overload.** Ninety statutes landing on an administrative state that is being simultaneously rebuilt is a recipe for visible failure — and visible failure in a delivery-dependent political strategy is fatal. Capacity reform is not one item among ninety; it is the precondition for the other eighty-nine.

**5. The Court strikes three things at once.** The FRA, term limits, and the commission mandate is a plausible single-term outcome. There is no contingency architecture beyond severability and the expansion deterrent, and there should be: state-level fallbacks pre-drafted for each federal reform, so that a loss in Washington converts into a campaign in the states rather than a dead end.

**6. Attention scarcity.** A president makes perhaps three things salient per year. Backlash tracks volume; reward tracks salience. A ninety-bill program maximizes the first and cannot move the second — which means the *communications* strategy is a structural component of the reform, not a wrapper around it.

---

## The bottom line

The instrument review changes perhaps fifteen items, one of them fundamentally: the funding base moves from a constitutionally fragile wealth tax to mark-to-market income taxation, which is the difference between a program that is financed and one that is litigated.

The impact assessment finds the program strongly net positive on representation, participation, delivery capacity, material wellbeing, and child poverty — and it surfaces one genuine design flaw that the domain-by-domain inventory concealed. **The reforms interact.** Proportional representation is good. National popular vote is good. Proportional representation plus a plurality-winner national popular vote is a structural instability that neither item contains on its own.

That is the argument for treating this as a system rather than a list, and it is the strongest reason to keep modeling it rather than merely arguing for it.
