# Talarico 2029–2037: A Scenario Model
2026-10-19 · @espen
An eight-year governing scenario built from a Talarico Senate win over Paxton in 2026. The modeled record at exit: child poverty down 44%, Social Security solvent to 2081 at 67 votes, US emissions 52% below 2005, and a structural fiscal gap of $1.2–1.5T over ten years. The recurring failures are construction capacity and a coalition whose beneficiaries do not vote in midterms.

## Legislative record, 2029–2037
Six reconciliation bills plus a floor agenda constrained by time rather than votes. The talking filibuster, enacted December 2029, inverts the burden: debate continues only while 41 senators are physically present to sustain it. That kills the costless filibuster and makes floor time the binding constraint — three or four major bills a year, each costing weeks of round-the-clock session.

## Fiscal accounting
**Headline gross spending is $13.8T over ten years. The honest structural gap is $1.2–1.5T, or about 0.4% of GDP.**
The difference is two pass-throughs. $3.4T is the carbon dividend, which appears as both revenue and outlay. $1.2T is Social Security, which is off-budget and self-financing. On-budget programmatic spend is **$9.2T**, roughly 2.7% of GDP annually.

### Design notes
Rates are the weak instrument and mostly do symbolic work. The top 1% pays an effective federal rate in the mid-20s because most of their income is not wage income — which is why mark-to-market is the single largest line at $1.1T and why capital gains parity, mark-to-market and stepped-up basis repeal have to move together. The behavioral response to all three simultaneously has never been modeled because it has never been tried; static scores could be off by a third either way.
The **small business package** (Recon III, 2031) is the frame's missing distinction between large concentrated capital and small productive capital: an 18% graduated corporate rate on the first $2M phasing out by $10M (−$180B); 199A restructured rather than capped, excluding financial services and passive entities; §179 expensing to $5M (−$90B); QSBS exclusion to $25M (−$70B); and a **payroll credit for hiring workers under 27 into first career-track positions** — 25% of the first $40K for two years, firms under $25M revenue (−$210B). That last provision is demand-side, which is the thing training programs structurally cannot be.
**The $400B reinsurance exposure is off-score and is the real fiscal risk.** CBO's baseline assumes an actuarially normal decade. A season with three major landfalls plus a Western fire complex plus a Midwest derecho draws $150–200B in one year.
For comparison: the 2017 tax law cost $1.9T and bought a one-year capex bump.

### The three durable failures
**Housing was never a money problem.** $95B does not put units on the ground before 2032. The binding constraints were preemption limits, municipal permitting, construction labor competing with the transmission buildout, and materials. The instrument used everywhere — conditional grants to states — fails precisely in the states that most need the thing. The FHIC (2031) was a supplement, not an institution.
**The entry-level labor market improved and never recovered.** The 2029–30 training programs worked, in construction, transmission and the trades: 340,000 apprenticeship starts in 2031 against 190,000 in 2028. They did not touch the entry-level problem, for four reasons — wrong population (credentialed BA holders, under 6% take-up), wrong timing (an apprenticeship started in 2030 journeys out in 2034, and cohort effects are permanent), wrong constraint (a demand hole inside firms, which no supply-side training fixes), and an instructor bottleneck at community colleges, since people qualified to teach industrial controls earn far more doing them.
**The coalition's beneficiaries do not vote in midterms.** Identified in the January 2030 Valley memo and never solved. In 2030, Democratic turnout ran 71% of 2028 volume in the highest-propensity districts and 44% in the districts with the highest child allowance uptake — a 27-point gap. In 2034 the DAB made it worse, paying 900,000 people who are by construction young and recently unemployed. Every program this coalition passes creates beneficiaries who don't vote.

### The special election finding
The November 2029 Texas special (Crockett loses to Patrick by 2.8 in the January runoff) inverted expectations twice: the suburbs held and overperformed, the Valley collapsed to a third of presidential volume. The correct model separates **composition** (the education realignment handed Democrats the highest-propensity bloc; structural, survives party control) from **motivation** (thermostatic, flips with it). Democratic special-election overperformance shrinks from ~+9 to ~+4 under their own president. It does not reverse.

## Emissions trajectory
The 2005–2028 period averaged about 0.9% of baseline per year. The 2029–2036 period averages 3.3% — roughly a 3.5x acceleration, and the fastest sustained decarbonization of a large industrial economy on record. It still misses the pre-existing 61–66%-below-2005-by-2035 commitment by a wide margin, and both of those are true at once.
| Sector, 2036 vs 2005 | Change |
| Power | −78% |
| Transport | −34% |
| Industry | −31% |
| Buildings | −24% |
| Agriculture | −29% |

Power does nearly all the work: the 2029 permitting and transmission law feeding a reformed interconnection queue, the 2030 credit restoration financing it, and the carbon price making the last coal and peaker retirements uneconomic. Transport is limited by fleet turnover — vehicles last fifteen years, so the 2032–36 sales shift only fully expresses itself in the late 2040s.
Agriculture is the sector the model had to be argued into. Left unconditioned it reaches only −22% by 2050 and becomes a third of residual emissions; conditioning the 2032 transition payments on enteric methane, nitrogen efficiency and manure systems gets it to −41% and costs Iowa in 2032 and 2034.
**Consumption-based emissions** fall further than territorial — about 51% below 2005 by 2036 — because the CBAM and the climate club price embodied emissions in imports. That is a real effect and the piece most accounting leaves out.
**Globally:** emissions plateau 2030–32 and are down roughly 49% from peak by 2050. Warming lands at **2.1–2.3°C by 2100**, against a 2.8–3.1°C counterfactual from the 2028 trajectory. Net zero domestically around 2054, with removal capacity overtaking a residual of ~730 MMT.
The honest framing: the difference between 2.1°C and 2.9°C is several hundred million people's habitability and the difference between West Antarctic collapse being a next-century problem and being underway. It is also a catastrophe measured against 2015.

## Courts and democracy
**Three appointments, and the Court only moves from 6–3 to 5–4.** Sotomayor retires June 2029 (confirmed 53–47), Kagan June 2031 (Ifill, 52–48), and Alito dies October 2033 (confirmed February 2034, 53–47). Thomas and Alito were never going to retire voluntarily under a Democratic president — Thomas declined at 72 under a Republican one, which is the actuarial proof.
Replacing Alito leaves five Republican appointees. What changes is the median, from Kavanaugh and Barrett to **Roberts**: narrow holdings, procedural off-ramps, institutional caution. Major questions and the non-delegation revival both survive with five votes.
There is no timing fight over the 2033 vacancy, because there is no argument available. The "McConnell rule" was a 2016 improvisation with a principle retrofitted onto it and abandoned in 2020. The operative principle is that the majority confirms when it has the votes — Kennedy 97–0 in February 1988, Barrett eight days before an election. Republicans slow-walk Judiciary for four months and change nothing.

### Court reform, refused three times
No expansion in 2029 (principle without opportunity), 2031, or October 2035 (principle with 56 seats and a one-vote Court in hand). The stated reason stays the same: I cannot argue the rules bind Vance and not me. What he does support — statutory ethics, prospective senior-status term limits, lower-court expansion — produces one win: **74 new judgeships in September 2031, 71–29**, the first bulk expansion since 1990.
Roberts is a negotiating counterparty, not a resignation prospect. He cannot control his successor, so leaving under a Democratic president moves the median away from himself. He wants the judgeships, can tolerate ethics, and is watching jurisdiction-stripping. What he delivers is a vote.

### The redistricting case
Oklahoma v. United States, decided June 26, 2032, **6–3, Roberts writing.** The law was drafted against its own vulnerability: a hard commission mandate regulates **who inside a state decides**, which has no direct Elections Clause precedent, while substantive criteria regulate **the map**, which does.
| Provision | Outcome |
| Mid-decade redistricting prohibition | Upheld |
| Substantive criteria (contiguity, compactness) | Upheld |
| VRA compliance requirements | Upheld |
| Three-judge-panel federal backstop | Upheld |
| Commission safe harbor (voluntary) | Upheld |
| Partisan-fairness numeric trigger | **Struck** on Rucho manageability grounds |

Net across the 2031 maps: a **12-seat structural improvement** against an expectation of 20. The fairness trigger was worth eight seats. Rucho's own invitation to Congress was the centerpiece of the government's argument, and striking the remedy Roberts himself pointed to was a legitimacy cost he declined to pay.
**WHPA upheld 5–4** the same month in Mississippi v. Becerra, on Raich commerce grounds, Roberts joining the four Democratic appointees. Thomas's dissent argues for overruling Wickard.

### The filibuster
The reform was a **talking filibuster, not a carve-out**: debate continues only while 41 senators are physically present, and when the minority can't produce them on a live quorum call, cloture ripens at a simple majority. It preserves the right to delay and publicize and destroys the costless signal.
The filter it creates sorts by whether the minority's position survives weeks of live television, not by subject. Bills they are embarrassed to block die (voting rights broke at 3:47am on day 19). Bills they are proud to block get harder — the Equality Act was pulled on day 12 and immigration failed in 2032, because sustained obstruction on those is free earned media.
**DC and Puerto Rico statehood, June 2033, 54–46 after 19 days.** Four senators, permanent, and five electoral votes that are the reason Pennsylvania is not decisive in 2036.

## The successor agenda, 2037–2041
**2036: Moore–Ocasio-Cortez 50.4, Sanders–Moreno 48.3. D+2.1. Electoral College 288–250.** Senate 53–47, House 222–213.
The primary is the argument deferred since 2029. Her case: eight years of transfers produced a country where child poverty is low, seniors are secure, and a generation aged 26–34 has no role — a dividend is what you pay someone to accept that they are not needed. His: delivery compounds, and a jobs program dies in eighteen months under a Republican trifecta. She takes 43% of delegates; he wins in April on South Carolina and the South; she takes the vice presidency because it is the shortest path to what she wants.
Moore runs 4 points ahead of Talarico's 2032 numbers with Black voters and 6 behind with white non-college voters and Latino men. **The church-and-hunger coalition was personal; the parts that transfer are about identity, not faith.**

### The agenda
  - **Housing as construction.** The FHIC becomes an institution: direct federal build authority at $90B a year, eminent domain extended from transmission to housing in designated shortage markets, federal preemption of local permitting above a density threshold where vacancy is under 4%, a standing workforce of 180,000 from the service corps. The fight Talarico would not have.
  - **Transitional Employment Guarantee.** Anyone under 30 unemployed 16+ weeks entitled to a funded position in care, resilience, retrofit or infrastructure at 110% of local median entry wage, two-year cap. ~1.4M positions, $62B a year. Her policy with his sunset and sectoral limits.
  - **Prosperity Fund at scale.** Equity-for-access extended to spectrum, federal R&D-derived IP, and interconnection at every scale. Dividend from $340 to **$1,150 per adult by 2041.** This is the resolution of the labor share problem, found four years late.
  - **Long-term care.** The 2032 benefit is means-tested and thin; the 2040s curve needs a universal one and a care workforce of ~4M, which makes immigration and care policy the same bill.
  - **Hard-sector decarbonization.** Procurement, standards, and direct investment in hydrogen, process heat, sustainable fuels and cement, plus $40B a year in removal — the 2054 net-zero date depends on capacity that does not yet exist.
  - **AI governance defense.** Statutory limits on federal use of model systems for surveillance, immigration enforcement and benefit adjudication without human review. Introduced 2039; never passes; polls at 38%.

### What it inherits and what happens
**June 2038, **Arizona v. HUD**:** preemption upheld 5–4 under the Spending and Property Clauses but limited to federally funded and constructed housing, which is ~10% of the market; eminent domain struck 5–4 on public use grounds, so the FHIC can build on land it controls and cannot assemble parcels. Target falls from 800,000 units to **~420,000 by 2045.**
**2038 midterm:** House lost, 204–231. The mechanism repeats a third time — the TEG pays 900,000 people who are by construction young and recently unemployed.
**2039: Thomas dies at 91.** Confirmed January 2040, 51–49. **The Court is 5–4 liberal for the first time since 1969.** Everything in the pipeline revalues in an afternoon, and a legal movement that spent sixty years building toward 2022 and held the majority for eighteen years loses it to actuarial chance.
The 2040 Republican nominee is not Moreno, who is 73. It is someone thirty years younger running on the question no Democrat has answered in twelve years: what a person is for, in an economy where the work is done.

## Analytical findings
These are the transferable results — the parts of the model that are about how American government works rather than about this candidate.

### Instrument design
**A check becomes furniture.** The child allowance and the carbon dividend both survive hostile Congresses for the same reason: taking away a payment people already receive is the hardest thing in American politics. The carbon dividend specifically is why a price escalating to $190/ton survives administrations that would otherwise repeal it. Design for irreversibility, not for elegance.
**Disbursement authority is a weapon.** Treasury's standing authority to pay the child allowance without an appropriation — a 2029 drafting decision nobody noticed for six years — is why the 2035 shutdown fails in seventeen days.
**Universality is what defeats the deservingness frame.** A means-tested child benefit is a welfare program; a universal one cannot be racialized. Same instrument, opposite politics.
**67 votes is a different asset than 54.** A 54-vote Social Security law is a repeal target for forty years. A 67-vote one is settled. The difference was worth delaying nothing, because the bipartisan path was available the whole time.
**Tax rates are the weak lever.** Mark-to-market is the single largest revenue line because most top-decile income is not wage income. Rates do symbolic work the frame requires and little else.

### Sequencing
**Delivery before democracy reform.** Leading with voting rights in February 2029 spends the honeymoon on a process fight with nothing in anyone's bank account, and the 2030 midterm loses forty seats instead of thirteen. The argument that wins later is "they are blocking what works," not "we changed the rules."
**A credible threat is worth more unused than used.** The untested economic carve-out sitting at 53 votes is what produced a 67-vote Social Security law. The same structure governs the Roberts negotiation: the stick makes the handshake. Spending it destroys it.
**Timing failures compound on a physics deadline.** The carbon price three years late, the Prosperity Fund four years late, agriculture never. None of those were judgment errors about what; all were errors about when, and on climate that is the whole thing.
**Capacity is the precondition, not a side project.** The first-term agenda was limited by a hollowed-out executive branch. The Federal Workforce Integrity Act (2031) plus AI-era administrative tooling is what made the second term's ambition possible, and it is the least-credited achievement of the period.

### Coalition mechanics
**Special-election overperformance is compositional, not thermostatic.** Democrats kept overperforming in 2022–24 while holding the White House, which falsifies the out-party model. The education realignment handed them the highest-propensity bloc, and that survives party control. Overperformance shrinks from ~+9 to ~+4 under their own president. It does not reverse.
**The beneficiary–voter asymmetry is the structural problem and it was never solved.** Low-propensity beneficiaries, high-propensity spectators, three midterms lost on the same mechanism (2030, 2034, 2038). Every program this coalition passes creates people who don't vote.
**The two-audience problem follows from it.** High-propensity voters respond to corruption, democracy and abortion. Low-propensity voters respond to checks and prices. The second group does not turn out for the first group's issues, which is the substantive case for automatic registration rather than the procedural one.
**Redistricting timing is not invertible.** A 2028 win runs into a 2030 census and 2031 maps drawn by legislatures elected in the 2030 midterm — the same trap Obama walked into in 2010, in the same year of the term. The census administration is a real prize; the maps are not.

### The climate finding
**Capital markets priced the physics before politics did.** The 2034 insurance collapse — Florida and Louisiana Citizens insolvent, reinsurance withdrawing from the Gulf, mortgage origination stalling — is what produced a bipartisan adaptation consensus. Not persuasion. The mortgage market.
And the risk that follows: **the adaptation consensus is stable.** A rich country can buy tolerable conditions for long enough that mitigation urgency dissipates while the countries that cannot afford it absorb the consequences. That is a coherent political equilibrium and the most likely one.

### The pattern, stated once
Transfers and rules: delivered fast and well. Capacity and construction: late and short. The government this administration rebuilt could administer anything it passed; it never became an institution that builds at scale. Housing is the monument.
