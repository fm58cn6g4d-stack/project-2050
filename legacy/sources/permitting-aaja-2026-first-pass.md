# The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act: What It Does, Whether It Passes, and How Much It Changes America's Energy and Climate Future

Odds are well below even that the Capito–Lee–Whitehouse–Heinrich permitting bill becomes law in the 2026 lame duck. My estimate is about 25–30%. But it matters far more than its odds suggest. It is the only realistic way for the next decade's climate ambitions to clear the permitting bottleneck. Without it, I estimate a 2029 Democratic climate bill would deliver only about half of its emissions potential by 2035.

## TL;DR

- **Passage:** I put the chance of lame-duck enactment at about 27% (range 20–35%). The Senate is the easier chamber, at about 50% for passage there. The House is the binding constraint. In a Sept. 30 statement, House Natural Resources ranking member Jared Huffman said the deal "raises significant questions and concerns that would have to be resolved before most Democrats could support it," and House GOP leadership hasn't committed to the Senate text. If both chambers pass it, Trump very likely signs it (about 85%). If it dies, the next realistic window is 2029, since leadership changes in January would break up the "Four Corners" deal.
- **What it does:** It is the biggest procedural rewrite of federal infrastructure law since NEPA. FERC gets real backstop siting power over interstate transmission, interregional planning becomes mandatory, and data centers must pay for the grid built to serve them. NEPA, CWA, ESA and NHPA challenges get a 150-day statute of limitations and remand without vacatur. Permits issued on or after Sept. 16, 2026 get certainty protection. It also clearly helps gas pipelines and LNG by stripping states' Section 401 leverage. On net I judge it clean-leaning, because the scarce input for clean energy is transmission and interconnection, not fossil permits.
- **Climate stakes:** Under current policy, after OBBBA, the U.S. is headed for 4.2–4.8 Gt in 2035, a 26–35% cut from 2005, per Rhodium Group's Taking Stock 2025 (Rhodium's July 2026 update projects 27–41% below 2005 by 2040). On my rough scenario modeling, the bill alone adds 2–4 points. A strong 2029 climate bill without it reaches roughly 40–46% below 2005 by 2035. With both, roughly 50–57%. Permitting reform doesn't cut much carbon by itself, but it multiplies the effect of every carbon-pricing, tax-credit and standards lever Democrats might pass later.

## Key Findings

1. **This is the most consequential permitting deal yet, and the hardest to pass.** The bill runs 417 pages and was negotiated by the chairs and ranking members of ENR and EPW. Its reach goes well beyond the Manchin–Barrasso Energy Permitting Reform Act of 2024 (EPRA), which cleared ENR 15–4 but never got a floor vote. IFP's Aidan Mackenzie called it "easily the best permitting bill we've ever gotten." The Conservation Law Foundation called it a "backroom energy deal" that "appears to adopt a fossil fuel industry wish-list."
2. **Transmission is the core of it.** The most important changes are these:
   - FERC's backstop siting authority no longer depends on DOE corridors. If states don't act within a year, FERC must approve a line that it finds in the national interest.
   - A national cost-allocation formula assigns costs to the customers who benefit.
   - Regional and interregional planning becomes mandatory, using common forecasts.
   - The federal right of first refusal is removed.
   - Interconnection must follow the SPP-style fixed-fee model.
   
   These go straight at the biggest physical bottleneck in decarbonization. According to ACEG/Grid Strategies' July 2025 "Fewer New Miles" report, which uses FERC data, the U.S. built 888 miles of 345 kV+ lines in 2024 (334 miles of 345 kV and 554 miles of 500 kV). The same report puts the need at roughly 5,000 miles a year, a benchmark "based on the U.S. Department of Energy's 2024 National Transmission Planning Study."
3. **The data-center provision is real, and it is mandatory.** The bill amends the Federal Power Act so utilities can charge large loads for both new and existing infrastructure. Large loads must also report their energy use every year. That makes it much stronger than the House-passed Ratepayer Protection Act, which passed 417–3 but was blocked in the Senate. Schumer's one-word critique of the RPA was "optional." PJM is the reason this matters politically: capacity prices went from $28.92/MW-day (2024/25) to the $333.44 cap (2027/28). PJM's Dec. 17, 2025 release said that auction came in "short of PJM's reliability requirement by 6,623 MW," the first time the entire RTO fell short. The 2028/29 auction then fell 6,831 MW short at the $325 cap.
4. **Permitting certainty is the Democrats' price, and it is weaker than it looks.** Agencies may not "revoke, rescind, withdraw, terminate, suspend, amend, or alter" authorizations in effect on or after Sept. 16, 2026. But there is a national-security / "new information" carve-out. And a "pattern of disparate treatment" claim requires a five-year pattern of delay that is shown to be intentional. Trump's offshore-wind stop-work orders already lost in court five times out of five. The bill's added value is mostly deterrence and a damages remedy, not a fast stop to executive obstruction.
5. **Fossil lock-in is real but bounded.** The changes to Section 401 and Nationwide Permits mainly help pipelines like Williams' Northeast Supply Enhancement and Constitution, which New York and New Jersey blocked in the past. Even so, the C2ES/Greenline modeling projects that gas "does remain the largest source of electricity generation in 2035." RMI's analysis of the comparable 2024 EPRA found net emissions reductions even in a worst case that included the fossil provisions.
6. **The Byrd-rule asymmetry decides the strategy.** A 2029 Democratic trifecta could pass a carbon fee, tax credits, grants and probably a border adjustment through reconciliation. It almost certainly could not pass permitting or FERC jurisdiction changes that way, because those are non-budgetary. Permitting needs 60 votes, and this bipartisan coalition exists now. Waiting for "a better deal" means betting on a filibuster carve-out or a new bipartisan alignment in 2029–31.

## Details

### 1. What the bill actually does, translated

Caveat on sourcing: I couldn't access the uploaded PDF directly. This section relies on the committee releases, the posted text, and detailed reporting from Heatmap, Canary Media, Utility Dive, Latitude Media, The Washington Sun/POLITICO, SWACCA and others. Where sources conflict, I say so.

| Title / provision | What it does | Real-world translation |
|---|---|---|
| **FERC backstop siting** (FPA §216 rewrite) | A developer can file at FERC at the same time as at the states. If states don't approve within one year, FERC must approve a line it finds in the national interest. DOE's NIETC designation step is removed. | SunZia is Heinrich's example. He told the Washington Times on Sept. 30, 2026 that the 550-mile line "took 17 years to permit and build" when it "should have taken probably five years." Under this bill, a Grain Belt Express–type line that is stuck in one state's commission gets a federal path after about 12 months. DOE's Aug. 12, 2026 cancellation of the Lake Erie–Canada, Southwestern Grid Connector and Tribal Energy Access corridors stops mattering legally. |
| **Interregional planning + cost allocation** | Regional and interregional planning is mandatory, with shared forecasts and formulas. The bill sets out six categories of benefits and a beneficiary-pays allocation, and removes the federal ROFR. | This effectively codifies FERC Order 1920 and goes beyond it. Order 1920 is still being litigated in the Fourth Circuit, where it faces post-*Loper Bright* attacks. A MISO–PJM or SPP–MISO seam project, which today has no agreed cost formula, would get one. |
| **Local "medium" transmission oversight** | States may regulate lower-voltage reliability projects within a single state, and can ask FERC to investigate. | This addresses the RMI/Brattle finding that about 90% of 2023 transmission spending went to lower-voltage reliability upgrades that face little prudence review. |
| **Interconnection reform** | Requires an SPP-style fixed-fee, planned interconnection process. | It could start clearing the 549 GW that has interconnection agreements but isn't built. That includes 256 GW of solar, 161 GW of storage, 76 GW of wind and 45 GW of gas (LBNL, end-2025). |
| **GETs / VPPs** | Utilities must study grid-enhancing technologies and reconductoring, and adopt them when benefits exceed costs or risk losing some of their return. Wholesale markets must admit VPPs and distributed resources. | Dynamic line ratings and advanced conductors can raise the capacity of existing corridors much faster than new lines can be built. |
| **Large loads / data centers** | Utilities can charge covered large loads for new and existing T&D and generation costs. Large loads must report energy use annually. | A 1 GW hyperscale campus in Loudoun County, or in AEP Ohio territory, pays for its own upgrades rather than spreading them across the PJM rate base. ERCOT matters less here, because most of it sits outside FERC jurisdiction (see Caveats). |
| **NEPA** | Two-year EIS and one-year EA deadlines, with consequences for missing them. Narrows "major federal action" so it excludes federal loans, certain grants, repairs, geothermal testing and in-right-of-way pipeline work. Agencies may ignore information that arrives after the NOI. Review can be skipped if a state or Tribe already reviewed the project. | For comparison, CEQ's 2020 study found a mean EIS time of 4.5 years and a median of 3.5. CEQ's 2025 update shows the median already fell to 2.4 years for 2021–2024 EISs, after the FRA 2023 deadlines took effect. The marginal gain is largest for the long tail: FHWA EISs averaged more than 7 years. |
| **Judicial review** | 150-day statute of limitations (down from 6 years) for NEPA, NHPA, CWA and ESA. Standing is limited to substantial commenters or directly harmed parties. Cases go straight to the courts of appeals. Remand without vacatur is required. | A pipeline or a transmission line can no longer be stopped by vacatur after it is built, which is what happened to MVP before Congress intervened in 2023. Opponents get a remand and agency re-analysis, but not an injunction that kills the project. |
| **CWA §401** | Applies a "directly caused" discharge scope to pipelines and transmission lines. Conditions must be "necessary" and "least burdensome." A waiver is final. Water quality is the only allowed ground; no air or climate considerations. | This removes the tool New York used against Constitution and NESE. Note the sources disagree on the deadline: Heatmap reports a one-year maximum, while SWACCA reports nine months. The text will settle it. |
| **CWA §404** | Projects affecting less than 2 acres are deemed "minimal." Nationwide Permit limits double for pipelines and transmission. Certain permits extend from 5 to 10 years. | Many linear crossings move from individual permits to general permits. |
| **ESA** | Consultation is capped at about 145 days and must be complete by the NEPA decision. Highway and transit projects within existing right-of-way are excluded. States with demonstrated capacity may take over consultations. | This is the provision Defenders of Wildlife and CBD oppose most strongly. A state like Utah or Wyoming could run Section 7 consultations itself. |
| **NHPA §106** | Consultation runs concurrently with NEPA and is time-limited, and the statutory Tribal role is kept. Narrows "traditional cultural property." Excludes purely visual or audible effects unless they significantly diminish a property. | The viewshed fights over SunZia, and the offshore-wind fights on Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard, lose much of their legal force. NATHPO opposes. |
| **Permitting certainty** | No new action to revoke or alter permits in effect on or after Sept. 16, 2026, unless needed to prevent "specific, urgent, substantial, and proximate harm" based on new information. One-year deadline to decide complete applications. A "disparate treatment" cause of action covers at least 46 project types, with damages paid from the Treasury Judgment Fund. | This would have been the legal answer to the December 2025 stop-work orders against Revolution, Sunrise, Empire, Vineyard and CVOW. It is unclear whether it reaches DOD/FAA airspace clearances, the current choke point for onshore wind. |
| **Geothermal** | Categorical exclusion for "observation test projects." "Casual use" is exempt from NEPA. Mandatory annual lease sales. Drilling permits on a 30-day completeness / 30-day decision clock. No federal drilling permit for exploration on non-federal land. | Fervo-style enhanced geothermal in Utah's Beaver County, near the Cape Station project, and in Nevada would face oil-and-gas-like permitting. That is a natural priority for Chairman Lee's home state. |
| **Hydro** | FLOWS Act provisions. Maintenance on existing facilities is exempt from FERC approval. Conditions must be "reasonably related." A micro-hydrokinetic (under 5 MW) pathway. | Relicensing of the existing fleet is the real prize, since much of the U.S. hydro fleet faces relicensing in the 2020s–2030s. |
| **Abandoned Hardrock Mine Fund; e-permitting** | Mandatory fund. Unified digital permitting portal, with the final version due Dec. 1, 2028. Duties apply only "to the maximum extent practicable." | The digitization deadlines are probably not enforceable. |

**Passenger rail and transit, the angle most coverage has missed.** Three provisions matter to an FRA/FTA program:
- The ESA exclusion for "highway and transit projects within existing right-of-way." Whether intercity passenger rail counts as "transit" is a drafting question worth pressing staff on.
- Narrowing "major federal action" to exclude federal loans and certain grants. If that reaches DOT credit programs like RRIF and TIFIA, it is a sleeper win for rail.
- The two-year EIS clock, which applies to FRA Tier II documents.

Corridor upgrades on existing rights-of-way are the biggest winners: NEC state-of-good-repair work, Raleigh–Richmond (S-Line), Chicago hub, Cascades. Greenfield high-speed rail benefits much less, because its binding constraints are capital, right-of-way acquisition and cost discipline, not NEPA. Permitting certainty covers permits, not grants. It would not have protected California HSR's terminated federal grant, or the Gateway funding fights, from executive action.

### 2. Legislative and statutory context

- **2015 FAST-41** created the Permitting Council and dashboard for covered projects. It worked well procedurally but was voluntary in substance.
- **2023 Fiscal Responsibility Act** made the first substantive NEPA amendments since 1970: page limits, 1- and 2-year deadlines, a lead agency, and a narrowed definition of "major federal action." It also forced completion of MVP.
- **2024 EPRA (Manchin–Barrasso)** cleared ENR 15–4 and died after the election. Disagreements over transmission with House Energy & Commerce were a key point of contention.
- **2024 *Loper Bright*** ended Chevron deference. FERC Commissioner Christie argued that Order 1920's chances in court were now "slim to none." That is exactly why codifying it matters.
- **2025 *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County*** told courts to defer to agencies on NEPA scope and limited "upstream/downstream" analysis. CEQ then rescinded its NEPA regulations. This bill codifies and extends both moves.
- **Section 401:** EPA's 2020 Trump rule narrowed state certification, the 2023 Biden rule restored it, and the Trump EPA's 2026 proposal narrows it again. The bill writes the narrowing into statute, ending the back-and-forth for pipelines and transmission lines.
- **SPEED Act (H.R. 4776):** passed the House 221–196 on Dec. 18, 2025, with 11 Democrats in favor. A last-minute GOP leadership amendment, demanded by anti-renewable conservatives, exempted Trump's actions against renewables from its certainty provisions. The House bill also excludes offshore wind from key reforms. The Senate bill reverses both, which is the main gap to close in conference.
- **The rupture and reset:** Trump suspended five offshore wind projects on Dec. 22, 2025. Reports put their combined size at nearly 6 GW (Canary) or 7 GW (Utility Dive). Whitehouse and Heinrich declared talks "dead in the water." Courts enjoined all five orders by Feb. 2, 2026, and a federal court separately vacated the wind-permitting moratorium. Talks resumed after Trump told Democrats that wind and solar would get better treatment. On Sept. 24, Thune said Republicans and the White House had "closed the deal" on text that "incorporated essentially all" of Democrats' asks. The deal nearly collapsed on the morning of Sept. 30 over offshore-wind language. Whitehouse still calls "regular order" for wind and solar "unresolved," though he describes the administration's offer as "a very reasonable opening proposal."
- **Committee positions:**
  - EPW Chair Capito wants it as "the first vote when we come back."
  - ENR Chair Lee says "there is nothing sufficiently objectionable in this bill that I think either party would want to change, regardless of the outcome of the election."
  - On the Democratic side, Whitehouse is expected to move to Judiciary, and Jeff Merkley, next in line at EPW, has raised concerns about weakened environmental review.
  - In the House, Natural Resources Chair Westerman calls the deal "an encouraging step." E&C Chair Guthrie's office pointed to his own POWER Act discussion draft. Natural Resources ranking member Huffman opposes it as written.

**Typical development timelines, from my synthesis of public data:**
- Utility solar: 3–5 years, dominated by interconnection.
- Onshore wind: 4–7 years, with DOD/FAA clearance now the choke point.
- Offshore wind: 8–12 years.
- Interstate HV transmission: 10–17 years. SunZia took about 17.
- Interstate gas pipeline: 3–5 years, or about 7 when contested (MVP).
- LNG terminal: 5–8 years.
- Hardrock mine: 7–10+ years.
- Conventional geothermal: 7–10 years. Enhanced geothermal could be faster.
- Hydro relicensing: 5–10 years.
- New nuclear: 8–15 years, with NRC licensing now governed by the ADVANCE Act and the bill largely silent.
- Major highway EIS: 7+ years.
- New HSR corridor: 15–20+ years.

Supply chain is a separate bottleneck that permitting reform doesn't solve. Large power transformer and gas turbine lead times are measured in years. Labor and tariffs constrain construction too.

**Possible vehicles.** If floor time collapses, attaching it to a year-end appropriations package or the FY27 NDAA is plausible. A 417-page jurisdictional rewrite usually draws objections from appropriators, though. Expect at least a trimmed transmission-plus-data-center title to be floated as a fallback.

**Should Democrats wait?** Huffman argues that "folding our hand now or in the lame duck makes no sense when we can keep working on this and, in a few more months, have a much better deal." I think that's wrong, for three reasons:
- A Democratic House can't move the Senate's 60-vote math, and it can't make Trump sign anything.
- Every month of delay is another month of OBBBA-era renewable projects stuck in queues without certainty protection.
- In a 2029 trifecta, the Byrd rule keeps permitting reform out of reconciliation.

The strongest counterargument is substantive, not tactical. The ESA state-delegation and NHPA narrowing provisions may cost more in biodiversity and Tribal-rights terms than the climate math captures. Amendments in November are the place to fight over those.

### 5. If enacted: implementation and effects

**Immediate term (2027–2028, rest of the Trump administration)**
- *Rulemakings:* FERC would have to write the interregional planning, cost allocation, interconnection, large-load and VPP rules, probably on 12–24 month statutory clocks. Statute insulates them from *Loper Bright* attacks better than Order 1920 is insulated. CEQ and the agencies would need to update NEPA procedures. EPA would conform its 401 rule. FWS/NMFS would write the state-assumption rules for ESA. BLM would set up the geothermal clocks and annual lease sales. The Army Corps would reissue Nationwide Permits.
- *Slow-walk risk:* A Republican-led FERC will move fast on large-load and gas-friendly items, and slowly or narrowly on interregional cost allocation. Interior and DOD can lean on the national-security / "new information" carve-out, a direction Energy Secretary Wright's drone-threat remarks already hint at. Disparate-treatment suits require a five-year pattern, so they mature only around 2028–2030.
- *Winners first:* Gas pipelines and LNG benefit immediately from the 401 changes, the 150-day statute of limitations and remand without vacatur, because they have FERC siting already. Geothermal and hydro benefit quickly. Transmission benefits slowly: the first FERC backstop filings would come in 2027–28, with approvals in 2028–29 and energization in the 2030s. Data-center cost allocation takes effect in PJM and MISO tariff cycles in 2027–28.
- *Litigation:* Expect constitutional and APA challenges from states (FERC preemption of siting), from Tribes (NHPA/ESA) and from NGOs (standing limits). The 150-day statute of limitations applies to project challenges, not to facial challenges of the statute.

**Medium term (2029–2032, possibly a Democratic administration)**
- A Democratic FERC and CEQ would inherit statutory tools that cut both ways. They could aggressively approve interregional lines through backstop siting and cost allocation, but could not revoke fossil permits issued after Sept. 16, 2026. The certainty provision protects pipelines from a future Keystone XL–style revocation, which is the GOP's stated reason for it. That trade is deliberate.
- First-wave backstop lines would likely be energized. Candidates: MISO–SPP and MISO–PJM seams, and Southwest lines from New Mexico and Wyoming wind to load centers. I estimate 345 kV+ additions could reach 1,500–2,500 miles a year by 2030–32, against roughly 500–1,000 without the bill.

**Long term (2033–2040s)**
- Durable statutory planning, together with beneficiary-pays allocation, could bring the grid back to its 1978–2020 average growth of about 2.3% a year. That is the pace Princeton REPEAT found necessary to deliver most of the IRA's modeled reductions.
- Pipelines and LNG terminals built in 2027–2032 will run into the 2060s–2080s. That is the lock-in cost.

**Counterfactual if the bill fails:**
- Order 1920 survives or falls in the Fourth Circuit.
- DOE's corridor program stays dormant, and backstop siting is effectively dead.
- Data-center costs are fought utility by utility, through state large-load tariffs and FERC co-location dockets.
- 401 policy keeps swinging by administration.
- Renewables remain exposed to executive hostility until 2029.

C2ES's commissioned Greenline modeling covers four regions serving more than half the country. It finds the bill enables almost 100 GW more wind, solar and storage by 2035, plus about 40 GW of interregional transfer capacity. It also finds a $7B reduction in delivery costs and $1.1B in lower residential bills. Treat these as advocacy-commissioned and regionally limited.

### 6. Layering a 2029 Democratic climate bill

*My method:* I start from Rhodium's post-OBBBA baseline. I scale each lever's standalone potential using published modeling (RFF, REPEAT, Rhodium, EMF-32, DOE/NREL), then apply a "realization factor" for permitting and transmission constraints. The realization factor comes from REPEAT's 2022 finding: "over 80% of the potential emissions reductions delivered by IRA in 2030 are lost if transmission expansion is constrained to 1%/year, and roughly 25% are lost if growth is limited to 1.5%/year." These are illustrative ranges, not model outputs.

#### Scenario comparison (net GHG vs. 2005)

| Scenario | 2030 | 2035 | 2040 | 2050 | Clean power additions (GW/yr, 2030–35) | 345 kV+ miles/yr (2030–35) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. No bill, no climate law (Rhodium baseline) | −27 to −33% | −26 to −35% | −26 to −41% | −30 to −45% | 35–50 | 500–1,000 |
| B. Permitting bill only | −28 to −35% | −29 to −38% | −31 to −45% | −35 to −52% | 45–65 | 1,500–2,500 |
| C. 2029 climate law, no permitting bill | −30 to −36% | −40 to −46% | −50 to −58% | −65 to −78% | 60–85 (queue- and transmission-capped) | 800–1,500 |
| D. Both | −31 to −38% | −50 to −57% | −63 to −72% | −85 to −95% | 90–130 | 3,000–5,000 |

**How to read this:**
- By 2030, the scenarios barely differ. Physical lead times of 5+ years in queues and 10+ years for lines dominate, and a 2029 law can't change 2030 much.
- The divergence opens by 2035, when a climate law plus permitting reform is worth about 10 extra points (about 0.6 Gt/yr).
- By 2040 the gap between C and D is 13–14 points. Transmission-dependent wind and solar, enhanced geothermal and electrified load all compound over time.
- B's standalone value of 2–4 points is consistent with RMI's estimate of about 6.5 Gt through 2050 for EPRA. That works out to roughly 10% of cumulative U.S. emissions over 2030–2050, and it accounts for fossil provisions.

**The bottom line on bottlenecks.** I estimate a 2029 climate law in scenario C realizes about 45–60% of its 2035 potential. With the permitting bill (scenario D) it realizes about 75–90%. Put another way, failing to pass this bill in 2026 costs the climate agenda of 2029 roughly 0.4–0.7 Gt/yr of reductions by 2035. That is about as much as California's and Texas's power sectors combined emit today. MIT CEEPR's July 2026 finding points the same way: OBBBA kept about 67% of the IRA's power-sector reductions over 2025–2035. Market and policy shocks matter, but physical deployment constraints set the ceiling.

#### Fossil lock-in: where the bill cuts against climate policy

- **Pipelines:** NESE (NY Harbor), Constitution (PA–NY) and MVP Southgate become much easier to build. Each adds about 0.5–2 Bcf/d of delivery capacity, with 40–60 year asset lives.
- **LNG:** NEPA narrowing, the 150-day statute of limitations and remand without vacatur protect DOE/FERC approvals. Examples include Venture Global's CP2 and Commonwealth LNG. The certainty clause also stops a future Democratic DOE from revoking export authorizations issued on or after Sept. 16, 2026.
- **Interaction with a 2029 climate law:** A carbon price or clean electricity standard would strand some of these assets. That is economically efficient but politically costly, and the certainty clause makes regulatory revocation impossible. Expect pressure to compensate owners or grandfather them. Gas power plants also gain from the reformed interconnection, and EPSA endorses the bill.
- **Net:** The clean-side bottleneck (transmission) is far more binding than the fossil-side one, because pipelines already have FERC siting under the Natural Gas Act. So on net the bill helps clean energy more than fossil energy, in line with RMI's worst-case finding for EPRA.

## Recommendations

1. **For Senate EPW/ENR staff (lame duck):**
   - Protect the transmission title and the large-load title above everything else.
   - Spend negotiating capital on tightening the national-security carve-out in the certainty section. Require a public, specific, time-limited finding, and add expedited review for DOD/FAA airspace clearances.
   - Accept narrowing amendments to ESA state assumption, such as capacity certification by FWS with revocation authority, to win progressive votes.
   - Clarify that intercity passenger rail counts as "transit" for the ESA right-of-way exclusion, and that DOT credit programs fall within the NEPA loan exclusion.
2. **For House Democrats:** Take the deal. The leverage you expect in 2027 doesn't exist in the Senate or the White House, and the Byrd rule closes off 2029. Trade votes for floor commitments on the wind and solar "regular order" language, not for reopening the text.
3. **For 2029 climate-bill planners:** Assume this bill's tools exist, or assume a power sector that can't absorb your credits. Sequence the climate bill to pair transmission funding with the new FERC authority. Put the carbon fee and dividend, restored 45Y/48E, CBAM and industrial grants in reconciliation. Put standards in regulation, which the post-*Loper Bright* statutory clarity in this bill does not help.
4. **For developers:** If the bill passes, file FERC backstop applications for stalled interstate lines in 2027. Document patterns of delay now, because the five-year pattern test looks back.

## Caveats

- **Text access and drafting gaps:** I couldn't read the uploaded PDF. Specific provisions come from the committees and from secondary reporting, and some details conflict, such as the 401 deadline (one year vs. nine months) and the size of the paused offshore wind fleet (about 6 vs. 7 GW). Negotiators say they expect changes in November.
- **ERCOT:** Most of Texas sits outside FERC's FPA jurisdiction. The FERC planning, interconnection and large-load mandates may not reach ERCOT's 190+ GW of large-load requests, while the NEPA, CWA and ESA reforms would.
- **Modeling:** The C2ES/Greenline numbers were commissioned by a supporter and cover four regions. The RMI/EPRA figures come via supporters' summaries. RFF's carbon-tax results predate the IRA and OBBBA. Some EMF-32, CBO and Treasury figures are secondhand. My scenario table combines these sources with judgment and should be read as order-of-magnitude.
- **Probabilities** depend on the midterm results, the lame-duck floor calendar, and whether the White House delivers credible "regular order" assurances on wind and solar. A single Trump post against wind could swing the odds by 10+ points.
- **Macro context:** An oil and diesel price shock tied to the Iran war (diesel near $7 a gallon, SPR releases) raises the political salience of energy affordability. That could help passage or crowd it off the agenda.
