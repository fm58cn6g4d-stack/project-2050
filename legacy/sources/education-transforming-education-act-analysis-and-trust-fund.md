# Transforming Education for the Future Act — Analysis and Trust Fund Design
2026-10-19 · @espen

## Summary of findings
Rep. Bonamici's Transforming Education for the Future Act is a 466-page bundle of roughly 22 existing Democratic bills, authorizing about $73B a year in fixed amounts plus mandatory pre-K and universal meals. Fully funded, it adds roughly $100-125B a year in new federal spending at first and $150-190B by FY31 — about double the Education Department's current discretionary budget.
This session extended it into a birth-through-college package (adding child care, a teacher pipeline, and higher ed) and designed a payroll-financed trust fund to carry the formula-driven pieces. Four conclusions:
  - **The viable contribution rate is 1.28 points** of an uncapped payroll tax, paired with a frozen estate exemption at 2009 parameters. That funds meals, IDEA at 40%, pre-K and child care, a $30B Title I floor, the teacher floor, PARA and school mental health staff — about $142B in 2030 rising to $300B by 2036.
  - **Benefits must arrive fast.** ESEA in 1965 reached districts in one summer; ESSER moved $122B in weeks. Formula money through existing pipes can start immediately. Only pre-K seats and new hires face real capacity constraints.
  - **Drafting choices mattered more than the headline rate.** Correcting the growth indices, sunsetting temporary programs, and freezing the estate exemption moved the required rate from 1.60 to 1.28 — more than the gap between any two politically plausible rates.
  - **Durability comes from structure, not balance.** Mandatory spending, permanent appropriation, per-child formulas, and a defined federal share are what survive hostile Congresses. A rate that turns out 0.15 points light is an arithmetic problem a later Congress can fix.
All figures are approximate, built from CBO and CRS published estimates plus our own projections. Nothing here is a CBO score.

## The bill as introduced
Three titles, 466 pages, about 22 bundled bills. Cosponsors include Wilson (American Teacher Act), DeLauro (CHARTER Act), Omar (universal meals), Beyer (Keeping All Students Safe), and McBath (PARA and READ). 13 cosponsors, endorsed by AFT and National PTA.
Money arrives two ways. Universal pre-K and school meals are **mandatory** — paid directly, no annual appropriation. Everything else is **authorized**, so it still depends on appropriators. Fixed-dollar authorizations alone total about $73B a year in FY2027, excluding the IDEA increase, the "such sums" programs, and the mandatory spending.

### Title I — A well-rounded education
  - **Project-based learning:** competitive grants up to $2M over 5 years, across civics, CTE, arts and humanities.
  - **Arts Education for All:** arts written into state plans, report cards (course offerings, ratios, instructional time), school improvement plans, Title II certification, afterschool, and juvenile justice programs.
  - **History education:** Black, AANHPI, Hispanic and Latino, and American Indian history added to American History and Civics grants and to NAEP.
  - **Community schools:** planning grants from $100K; implementation grants at least $250K per school per year for 5 years; up to 15% to states.
  - **READ Act:** literacy grants must reach every state in the bottom fifth on 4th-grade NAEP reading; states get NAEP results with state rankings within 5 days of release; the IES evaluation can't be canceled except narrowly.
  - **Assessment limits:** state tests can't be the sole factor in grade retention, diplomas, or teacher evaluations; schools can't be rated solely on test scores or graduation rates.
  - **Innovative assessment pilot:** cap rises from 7 to 20 states, demonstration period from 5 to 7 years, $100M a year, plus $100M for professional development on new testing models.
  - **Accountability:** subgroup reporting down to groups of 10; two new indicators (advanced coursework access, school climate via surveys); Title I school improvement set-aside from 7% to 10%; triennial federal audits of state Title I use; no exit from improvement status until the lowest-performing subgroups improve two straight years.

### Title II — School climate, educators, research
  - **Civil rights:** ED guidance on disparate impact in discipline, police in schools, and restorative practices; the Emergency School Aid Act desegregation program revived at $1B a year.
  - **American Teacher Act:** 4-year state grants establishing a **$60,000 minimum teacher salary** from 2027-28, CPI-indexed; at least 85% passes through to districts, priority to Title I and rural; states must submit a sustainability plan. Funded at "such sums."
  - **PARA Act:** allotments on the Title I formula for paraprofessional wages and retention bonuses ("such sums").
  - **Educator mental health (HHS):** $35M a year in grants plus a $10M awareness campaign, FY27-29.
  - **FACTS Act (fentanyl):** HHS pilot funding up to 25 school-health partnerships for 3 years, an interagency task force, naloxone in school-based health centers.
  - **Corporal punishment:** banned in any federally funded program, including by police and security guards, with a private right of action.
  - **Restraint and seclusion:** seclusion, mechanical and chemical restraint, and restraint restricting breathing banned, with a private right of action; $40M a year in state grants.
  - **Bullying:** a new ESEA Part G requiring policies with enumerated protected categories including sexual orientation and gender identity.
  - **Right to Read:** school libraries in state plans; liability protection for staff following state or local right-to-read policies; district assurances on students' First Amendment rights.
  - **NEED Act:** a National Center for Advanced Development in Education inside IES; expanded longitudinal data systems; an ed-tech clearinghouse rating vendors on effectiveness, privacy and data monetization.

### Title III — Socioeconomic barriers
  - **Protecting public schools:** repeals Sec. 70411 of P.L. 119-21 (the federal tax-credit scholarship) and the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program; bars moving ESEA, IDEA, Perkins or ESRA functions out of ED, including by interagency agreement.
  - **Maintenance of equity:** FY27-31, states can't cut high-need districts disproportionately; the highest-poverty districts can't fall below FY2025 per-pupil state funding.
  - **CHARTER Act:** charter schools can't contract with for-profit companies to run or manage the school (food, transport and payroll services excepted), effective 3 years after enactment.
  - **Universal preschool:** free for all 3- and 4-year-olds through HHS; 100% federal share FY27-29 stepping to 60% by FY33; at least 1,020 hours a year; staff pay on par with K-12; lead teachers need a BA within 7 years; at least 4% to tribes; localities can apply directly if their state opts out.
  - **Universal meals:** effective one year after enactment; free breakfast and lunch for every enrolled child; reduced-price and paid tiers eliminated; reimbursement at $2.80 breakfast and $4.63 lunch, indexed, with a bonus for 25% local sourcing; Summer EBT from $40 to $60 a month; unpaid meal debt reimbursed; a new Title I poverty measure to replace lost meal applications.

## Funding levels vs. current and pre-Trump baselines

The Trump-era cuts happened mostly outside appropriations — withheld funds, canceled grants, layoffs, program transfers — with the budget cuts proposed for next year. The House FY27 bill would give ED $70.7B, $8B (10%) below FY26, cutting Title I by nearly $1.9B and zeroing Titles II-A and III-A, Migrant Education, and Neglected and Delinquent.

**Fully funded, the bill adds about $100-125B a year in its first full year and $150-190B by around FY31** — roughly double ED's entire current discretionary budget, and well over $1 trillion per decade in new money. Those are increases above baseline, not totals.
The all-in federal total for everything the bill touches runs $230-280B a year at maturity against roughly $85-90B today, spread across ED ($79B now, toward $175B by FY33), USDA child nutrition (~$30B now, mid-$40Bs under universal meals), and HHS (Head Start $12.4B plus $2.5B staff pay plus a new preschool program).
**The meals estimate had to be revised upward.** Our first figure of +$13-18B was based on the bill's own per-meal rates, which are stale: it copies 2023 rates of $4.63 lunch and $2.80 breakfast, while current 2026-27 free reimbursement is $4.76-4.87 and $2.54-3.05, and the Senate's 2026 version uses $5.42 and $3.28. At the Senate rates, roughly 5.5B lunches and 3.2B breakfasts cost about $40B against roughly $20B today. With CACFP, summer meals, an expanded Summer EBT and commodities, the program is about $54B in current dollars — **an increment of +$22B, not $13-18B.** The rate fix roughly doubles the cost of the meals themselves.

## Changes from current law
**Education Department structure.** As of June 2026, ED had signed 14 interagency agreements with six agencies moving at least 148 programs; on 16 June it announced special education would move to HHS and civil rights enforcement to DOJ. The FY26 spending law neither unwinds those agreements nor prevents new ones. The bill voids transfers of ESEA, IDEA, Perkins and education research functions — but it does not cover civil rights enforcement, which rests on separate statutes, so the DOJ move survives. It also contains no staffing floor, despite what the summary document claims.
**Private school tax credit.** Current law gives a dollar-for-dollar credit up to $1,700 for donations to scholarship organizations, uncapped in total, in states that opt in, starting 1 January 2027. The bill repeals it "as if not enacted" — which gets messy if enactment comes after January 2027 and credits have already been claimed.
**Charter schools.** Federal law currently permits full for-profit management. The bill writes the ban into the definition used across all of ESEA and IDEA, not just the Charter Schools Program, so charters run by for-profit operators would stop being "charter schools" for federal purposes — with knock-on effects for Title I and IDEA status. Only contracts signed or renewed after enactment are covered, and the ban starts after 3 years.
**Testing and accountability.** Annual testing stays. States currently choose their own minimum subgroup size for reporting; the bill forces it to 10, so more subgroups are reported and more schools get flagged. The improvement set-aside rises from 7% to 10% — about $5.5B at $55B Title I, roughly four times today's amount. The limits on using tests as the sole factor for retention or school ratings largely codify existing practice; retention laws with good-cause exemptions would likely survive.
**Discipline and safety.** Corporal punishment remains legal in roughly 17 states, and there is no federal restraint-and-seclusion law and no federal anti-bullying statute. The bill creates all three, with private rights of action that raise districts' litigation exposure. Its bullying policy explicitly covers gender identity, directly opposite the administration's Title IX position.
**School meals.** Beyond the stale rates, ending free and reduced-price applications removes the poverty count many state school funding formulas rely on — which is why the bill has to invent a replacement Title I poverty measure.

## Drafting issues in the bill text
Worth flagging to the office.
  - **Missing infrastructure provisions.** Purpose (6) promises to rebuild school infrastructure; no section does it.
  - **Duplicate section numbers.** Secs. 30114 and 23707 both create a new ESEA "Sec. 8549D."
  - **Broken cross-references.** Parts 2-3 of Subtitle C cite Secs. 24236, 24311 and 24337; these look like they should be 23246, 23311 and 23337.
  - **Probably unintended cuts.** Migrant Education drops to $100M from $374.75M and the Charter Schools Program to $250M from $440M — both below recent appropriations.
  - **Expired dates.** The NEED Act and data systems authorizations start in FY2026, which ends within weeks.
  - **Stale meal reimbursement rates.** The bill's $4.63 lunch and $2.80 breakfast sit below current free-rate reimbursement, so per-meal payments would fall for many high-poverty schools. The Senate's 2026 version uses $5.42 and $3.28.
  - **Smaller glitches.** The Title II-A figure is missing its "$," and the IDEA amendment's "paragraph (2)" reference reads ambiguously.

## The politics
Bonamici has not formally announced a bid for ranking member. She told Politico she is "not ruling anything out" and is exploring her options. Rep. Jahana Hayes has already launched a formal bid against Bobby Scott, who is 79 and has led committee Democrats since 2015. That makes the bill easy to read as a platform: it bundles colleagues' bills, and she called it "a blueprint" on the House floor.
**What could actually pass, and how.** The package splits cleanly along procedural lines.
  - **Reconciliation-eligible (the money):** universal pre-K, universal meals and Summer EBT, Head Start pay, repeal of the private school tax credit, and IDEA if written as mandatory. Title I can be converted to mandatory money in reconciliation, as pandemic relief was.
  - **Not reconciliation-eligible (the mandates):** the corporal punishment and restraint bans, the charter definition, the subgroup-size and accountability changes, the library assurances, and the bar on moving programs out of ED. These need 60 votes or a carve-out.
A realistic 2029 outcome is most of the dollars and few of the mandates. Education rarely gets the first reconciliation slot, so enactment lands mid-to-late 2029 at the earliest. Expect the package to shrink in conference: Title I phased to $40-55B over five to eight years, the IDEA glidepath stretched, pre-K's 100% federal share trimmed.
**Restraint and seclusion can move separately.** The House passed a restraint bill in 2010, 262-153, and Congress deliberately left the issue out of ESSA in 2015. The blockage is institutional rather than ideological — superintendent and principal groups oppose absolute bans on liability and staff-safety grounds, while disability organizations are unified in favor. A passable version bans prone, supine, mechanical and chemical restraint; restricts rather than abolishes seclusion (imminent danger standard, time limits, monitoring, documentation); requires 24-hour parent notification and incident reporting; funds training and positive behavior supports; and drops the private right of action. Pair it with transparency measures only — anything touching school ratings or test use reopens the ESSA settlement and loses the members you need. The likeliest vehicle is an IDEA reauthorization, overdue since 2004.
**Conditioning the mandates on funding.** Attaching restraint and seclusion rules to IDEA rather than Title I is far stronger: the practices fall overwhelmingly on students with disabilities, so the nexus is direct, while Title I's relatedness argument is strained. Condition the rules on the incremental funds above the current 10-11% share, so a state declining loses the increase rather than its whole grant — that keeps it clear of NFIB coercion problems. Write it as a use-of-funds restriction, fund the training so there is scoreable spending, and keep the private right of action out of reconciliation.

## Additions to the package
Three additions, taking the package from K-12 to birth-through-college. Together they roughly double the bill: child care affordability $40-60B a year, pipeline $2-5B, higher ed $50-80B, on top of K-12's $150-190B — about **$250-330B a year at maturity**, or 0.8-1.0% of GDP.
**Workforce pipeline.** Sequencing matters more than the amount: teacher prep takes one to four years, so pipeline money must land two to three years ahead of the demand shock, not alongside it. The tools exist and are merely underfunded — Teacher Quality Partnership grants ($70M now; residencies cost $20-30K per candidate, so 50,000 residents a year is a $1-1.5B program), registered teaching apprenticeships (the cheapest para-to-teacher on-ramp, pairing naturally with the bill's PARA provisions), Hawkins Centers, Grow Your Own, and loan forgiveness. Caveat: the binding constraint on teacher supply is usually compensation, not prep slots, which is why the $60K floor may do more for the pipeline than pipeline programs do. Prep capacity binds mainly in special education, bilingual education and early childhood, where the BA requirement bites.
**Child care.** A pre-K expansion without an under-3 expansion actively worsens infant and toddler care by pulling staff toward the better-paying end — the standard critique of the Build Back Better design, and the strongest argument for treating them as one bill.
**Higher education.** Free community college, a larger Pell, loan restructuring. This is the piece that pulls toward different committees and a different vehicle. There is a real argument for a birth-through-12 reconciliation bill in year one with HEA reauthorization on its own track, rather than one omnibus with more floor days and more failure points.
**Baby bonds, considered and set aside.** Trump Accounts already give every citizen child born 2025-2028 a $1,000 federal deposit invested in index funds, with up to $5,000 a year in family contributions and $2,500 from employers; Treasury reported applications for about 3 million children. The 2029 expiry is a natural vehicle, and Booker's progressive version runs ~$60B a year at maturity. But accounts are asset transfers, not service funding — they pay no pre-K teacher — and the account frame is also the voucher frame: once money belongs to the child, directing it to a private provider is a short step. Keep the trust fund pooled and never described in account terms; leave baby bonds as a separate postsecondary instrument.

## Long-run effects, 2029-2050
**Timing.** Enacted mid-2029, first appropriations FY2030-31. Meals can start within months. Teacher money lands in year one through the bridge credit. Pre-K scales through 2033-34. IDEA reaches 40% around FY2035-37. The costs arrive first; the measurable benefits land as the 2032 cycle begins.
**Workforce — the deepest change.** By 2040 the package adds 600,000 to 1 million workers across pre-K, K-12 and child care, with a nominal teaching median of $80-90K. That changes who enters the profession, which is the channel with the longest lag and the largest ultimate effect: teaching turns over about 8% a year, so composition shifts take 12-15 years and show up around 2042-45. The risk runs the other way early — hiring 250,000 people fast means average quality dips before it rises, and that dip lands exactly when Congress starts asking whether the money worked.
**Student outcomes.** 2032-38: kindergarten readiness rises, vacancies in high-poverty and rural districts fall sharply, NAEP moves 2-5 points in low-income subgroups, graduation rates 1-3 points. 2038-49: for cohorts starting school around 2032, plausibly +0.3 to 0.5 years of attainment, +5-10% adult earnings for low-income students, a few points off adult poverty incidence, and college-going up 5-10 points among low-income students. Discount all of it by fungibility — states reallocate their own money, so the real effective increase is 60-70% of nominal.
**Families and the labor market.** The near-term macro effect comes from child care, not schools. Capping costs plus universal pre-K plausibly adds 1-1.5 million workers, mostly mothers of children under five, within three or four years. Meals, Summer EBT and child care affordability together take 1-2 points off child poverty — real, but a quarter of what the 2021 expanded child tax credit did. For a poverty number, the credit is the instrument, not schools.
**Durability.** Mandatory universal pieces become permanent: by 2040, universal meals and pre-K are roughly as untouchable as school lunch and Medicare Part D. Two or three Republican trifectas would trim — block grants, work requirements, a restored reduced-price tier — not end them. Discretionary pieces decay: Title I authorized at $55B erodes toward $30-40B within two or three cycles. The mandates are the most fragile thing in the package and the first to go unenforced. Likeliest 2049 steady state: federal share of K-12 revenue 15-18%, universal early childhood intact, accountability rules rewritten twice, higher ed reform partly undone at the next reauthorization.
**The biggest failure mode is not fiscal.** Visible costs arrive a decade before measurable benefits, and the quality dip during the hiring scramble hits exactly when critics are looking for evidence. A 2036 headline reading "$2 trillion later, NAEP is flat" would be technically accurate, analytically meaningless, and extremely effective. Design for durability by front-loading what shows results fast — meals, child care costs, maternal employment, vacancy rates — and build in independent evaluation with pre-registered expectations.
**What it does not touch:** housing segregation, school assignment, curriculum, phones, and post-pandemic absenteeism.

## Macroeconomics
**Labor supply is the strongest economic argument.** 1-1.5 million added workers is 0.6-0.9% of the labor force, but the marginal workers are disproportionately mothers returning at lower hours and wages, so the output effect is about **0.3-0.5% of GDP, or $110-180B a year** in mid-2030s dollars — arriving within four or five years, since the constraint is care availability rather than skills.
**Revenue feedback.** At a combined effective rate of 20-25% including payroll taxes, that yields **$25-45B a year in federal revenue**, plus $10-15B state and local and reduced benefit spending. The child care and pre-K components, at $55-85B combined, recoup roughly a third to half their cost at maturity. The K-12 and higher ed pieces have no comparable near-term feedback.
Two caveats: CBO's conventional estimates won't credit most of this, so the official price tag looks worse than the economics; and subsidy phase-outs create marginal tax rates on second earners that claw back part of the gain.
**What cuts against it.** The first few years run the wrong way — demand rises faster than supply, wage floors raise costs, so expect prices and waitlists before access around 2031-34. Some of the measured GDP gain is accounting, shifting unpaid household care into the paid market. And deficit financing offsets it: 15-20 points of debt-to-GDP over two decades reduces long-run output by roughly 1-2%. Penn Wharton's modeling of universal pre-K found essentially no long-run GDP effect once financing was included. **Deficit-financed, the long-run GDP effect is plausibly near zero; financed by taxes on high-income consumption or inheritance rather than capital, it stays positive.**
**Long-run human capital.** Effects reach GDP in the 2045-2060 window: if low-income students see 5-10% higher earnings and are 30-40% of a cohort, that is +0.5 to 1% of GDP by 2060, phasing in at two to five hundredths of a point a year.

### Why 7-to-1 does not hold at scale
The sign is robust; the magnitude is not. Honest scaled estimates land nearer 2-to-1 or 3-to-1. Reasons, in rough order of weight:
  - **The counterfactual changed.** Perry Preschool ran in Ypsilanti in the early 1960s with 123 children whose control group got essentially nothing. Today about two-thirds of 4-year-olds are already in some program, so the real treatment is "public pre-K instead of existing care," not "instead of nothing."
  - **Tiny samples.** Perry n=123, Abecedarian n=111. Heckman's own reanalysis keeps the returns significant with enormous confidence intervals.
  - **Unscalable intensity.** Perry used master's-level teachers, ~5-to-1 ratios and weekly home visits, run by the researchers who designed it. Universal pre-K runs 8-to-1 or 10-to-1 with no home visits.
  - **Heterogeneity.** Perry targeted the most disadvantaged; universalizing adds children whose counterfactual is already a decent preschool, so average return falls as coverage rises — mechanically.
  - **Crime benefits don't transfer.** Much of Perry's measured benefit was avoided crime in a 1960s high-crime setting, valued on contested assumptions; some of it is transfer rather than output.
  - **Scaling degrades quality** by construction, and **discount rates** do a lot of work: benefits 30-40 years out look very different at 3% than at 7%.
The scaled evidence: Head Start benefit-cost ratios usually land at 1.5-3; Boston raised college enrollment with no lasting test score gains; Tulsa stayed positive through high school; **Tennessee's statewide program turned negative by sixth grade**; Quebec's universal child care produced worse long-run behavioral and health outcomes at low quality. Quality determines whether you land near Boston or near Tennessee, and that spread is wider than the spread between any two plausible funding levels.
One thing cuts the other way: Perry and Abecedarian never counted maternal employment, because they were too small to change anyone's work decisions. A universal program does, and that return arrives in five years rather than forty.

## Financing architecture
**Convert the formula-driven pieces to mandatory spending.** Meals are already an open-ended entitlement paying per meal served — the model to copy. Pre-K and child care work as state entitlements with federal reimbursement per eligible child. IDEA is the easiest conversion, since the Huffman bill already drafts the "hereby appropriated" language. Title I has precedent in the mandatory Pell add-on: a guaranteed floor with discretionary appropriations layered on top. Leave competitive and innovation programs discretionary — they need flexibility and buy little durability.
**Byrd constraints.** Provisions can't increase deficits beyond the budget window (which is why BBB's pre-K sunset created a cliff), and conditions whose budgetary effect is "merely incidental" get stripped. Three responses: offset inside the window so the program can be permanent; set a longer window in the budget resolution; or accept a sunset but align every expiration date so you get one high-salience fight with a broad coalition rather than three separable ones. Spending conditions generally survive — quality standards attached to pre-K dollars should hold; a freestanding restraint ban won't.
**Statutory PAYGO.** OMB keeps 5- and 10-year scorecards; a debit triggers a January sequester of non-exempt mandatory programs. It has never actually triggered — the 2017 tax law, ARP and the 2025 reconciliation bill all had their scorecards wiped. The catch: a wipe needs 60 votes, so the party that just passed a partisan bill must ask the minority to cancel the consequences. Assume a wipe is needed and that it costs something in the following year's appropriations. The House PAYGO rule and the Senate's pay-go point of order bite earlier.
**Why a trust fund.** Federal trust funds are accounting devices, not savings accounts, but they provide three things: a contributory frame that changes the politics of cutting; a permanent appropriation, so a hostile Congress can't defund by inaction; and a constituency for the revenue itself. The Highway Trust Fund is the cautionary case — it has needed general fund transfers since 2008 because the gas tax hasn't risen since 1993.
A trust fund does not insulate against reconciliation: Congress can amend the authorizing statute with 51 votes regardless. Two design points matter more than the label — **index the revenue to something growing at least as fast as wages**, and **give the fund a general-fund backstop** so a recession doesn't force a mid-year cut.
**The framing question.** "Better education for our country's children" is an easier universal ask than "early childhood." K-12's legitimacy is settled and the lifecycle logic works — a worker pays in for forty years and draws for eighteen per child, close enough to the Social Security bargain. Early childhood carries an unresolved values fight about whether public money should fund institutional care, which is why it polls well in the abstract and fragments on delivery. Folding both under one banner — school starts at three — puts early childhood inside an institution people already accept.
**The blended structure:** a 1.0-1.4 point uncapped payroll contribution dedicated to the entitlement payments, plus estate tax at 2009 parameters with stepped-up basis ended, dedicated to facilities, pipeline and reserve. Volatile revenue funds non-recurring uses. Keep the payroll piece structurally separate from Social Security, since reconciliation bars changes to Title II of the Social Security Act. Call it a contribution, not a tax, and name the fund for what it buys.

CRFB's underlying figures: a one-point uncapped payroll tax raises $1.3 trillion over ten years, one point on all brackets $1.2 trillion, top four brackets alone $300 billion, and a surtax above $100k/$200k $560 billion per point. CBO scored a 5% broad VAT at $3.05 trillion and a $25/ton carbon tax at $730 billion.
**Which base fits.** Costs are 70-80% labor and grow with wages, so payroll and broad income bases match almost automatically. A carbon tax shrinks as the thing taxed disappears. An FTT is volatile and partly avoidable. Estate revenue is lumpy and planning-sensitive. Capital-based taxes also fall hardest in recessions, exactly when you cannot cut child care slots. And payroll is the only option producing the contributory frame — high-income surtaxes fund the program without building that constituency.
**The central tension:** the base that fits the spending is the one that is politically closed. A commitment not to raise taxes below $400,000 rules out broad payroll, broad income and VAT. Funding $290B entirely above $400k needs a 5-point high-income surtax plus an FTT plus estate reform plus corporate increases, stacked — four fights, and a program whose funding falls in every recession.

### Indexing the estate component
Estate revenue is driven by the exemption threshold, and the threshold's growth rate determines the revenue's growth rate. It is currently indexed to prices, which is why the estate component was modeled growing at 3% against wage-indexed spending at 4%. **A slower-growing exemption produces faster-growing revenue**, because estates grow with asset values at 5-6% a year while the threshold creeps up more slowly, so more estates cross it and taxable amounts above it expand.
  - **Freeze the exemption in nominal terms** at 2009 parameters ($3.5M, unindexed) — revenue then grows at roughly the rate of wealth accumulation, about 5%. This is the version assumed in the model below.
  - Index to prices minus a point — about 4%.
  - Index to wages — this shrinks real revenue over time, and is the one to avoid.
Moving the estate component from 3% to 5% growth on a $45B base is worth about $10B a year by 2039 and $25B by 2049. Freezing is automatic and needs no future vote, but it is a quiet compounding tax increase and will be the first piece amended. The general rule: **at least one revenue source must grow as fast as wage-indexed spending.** Payroll does by construction; the estate component only does if the exemption is held down.

## The trust fund model
**Scope.** Inside the fund: meals, CACFP and Summer EBT; IDEA at 40%; pre-K and child care; a $30B Title I floor; the teacher floor; PARA and school mental health staff; the pipeline. Outside: Title I above the floor, ESEA competitive programs, higher ed — roughly $90-120B more, mostly new, on the discretionary track. So about 60% of the package becomes structurally protected and 40% stays vulnerable.
**Assumptions.** Nominal dollars. Payroll base $135B per point in 2030, growing 4.0% (3.7% compensation per worker, 0.3% employment). Estate revenue $45B in 2030 growing 5% on a frozen exemption. IDEA glidepath 15% in 2031 to 40% in 2037. Pre-K federal share 100% through 2034, then stepping 90/85/80/75/70/65 to **60% in 2041** and holding. Interest earnings excluded.
**Spending growth rates** are the correction that mattered most. Applying 4% — the payroll base's growth — to everything was wrong: spending is per-unit cost times number of children, and the school-age population is falling about 0.5% a year, with the 3-4 cohort falling faster (births down about 17% since 2007).
| Component | Index | Rate |
| Meals | Food-away-from-home CPI × meals served | 2.1% |
| IDEA | Self-adjusting: 40% of actual per-pupil spending | 3.7% |
| Pre-K + child care | Per-child cost with pay parity × cohort | 3.2% |
| Title I floor | Per-child wage cost × poverty count | 3.2% |
| PARA + mental health | Staffing cost × enrollment | 3.2% |
| Teacher grants | Declining by design | falls |

**Permanent vs. temporary.** Carrying temporary programs as permanent was the second major error. Permanent: meals, IDEA, pre-K and child care at a stepped share, the Title I floor, PARA and mental health, a ~$4B residual teacher equalization. Temporary: the bridge credit (2030-31), teacher grants (peaking 2033, ending 2037), the pipeline surge (tapering 2034-37), and pre-K's 100% federal share (2030-34). Sunsetting these correctly was worth about 0.2 points of payroll.

Federal pre-K spending goes flat for roughly seven years from 2035, because the declining share exactly offsets enrollment and wage growth. The real cost transfers to states, whose share rises from zero to about $39B a year by 2041 and $57B by 2049.
**Speed.** Everything that can start in year one should. ESEA passed in April 1965 and money reached districts that fall; Head Start went from announcement in February 1965 to serving over half a million children that June; ESSER moved $122B in weeks. Meals can go universal mid-year — it is administratively simpler than tiered eligibility. Title I goes straight to the full $30B floor, IDEA starts at its 15% step rather than transferring current funding, and the pipeline is front-loaded above its steady-state level so cohorts enrolling in fall 2030 finish in 2031-32, when pre-K hiring peaks. Only pre-K seats — facilities and licensing — are a genuine capacity constraint. The Head Start precedent is the caution: its 1965 summer launch produced quality problems that shaped criticism for decades, so speed is right for formula money through existing pipes and dangerous for new seats and new hires.

## Contribution rates and the stress test
The fund runs surpluses while pre-K and IDEA ramp, spends them down through the build-out, and rebuilds once the pre-K federal share has stepped to 60% and teacher grants have ended.

The meaningful threshold sits between 1.20 and 1.25 — below it you are financing a structural gap, above it a timing gap. That is about $33 a year for a median earner. The long-run breakeven is about 1.27, so **1.28 is the minimum rate that never goes negative**; 1.35 is the comfortable one, and the gap between them is roughly $60 a year per worker. Above 1.30 you are buying insurance at the cost of visible surpluses in the 2040s that invite raids.

### Recession stress test, 2036-38
The worst plausible timing: the fund is thinnest, pre-K is mid-step-down, and the program is established enough that failure is visible but not old enough to be untouchable. Unemployment rises 2.5 points peaking in 2037; payroll revenue falls 5% in 2037 and recovers over two years; district revenue bottoms in 2038 on the usual lag, so stabilization runs 2037-39.

**Total borrowing $89B, fully repaid by 2043.** Stabilization costs $52B over three years, already netted down from about $70B gross. The entitlements never miss a payment — stabilization is the piece that runs on borrowed money, which is the right ordering. The recession costs about $117B in terminal reserve, leaving 2049 near $297B rather than $414B.
At 1.35 the reserve bottoms at $46B in 2039 and never hits zero: the extra 0.07 points is what buys recession-proofing, about $46 a year at a median wage. A severe recession of 4 points instead would mean $150-180B in borrowing and repayment stretching past 2047 — still solvent, but carrying fund debt for a decade, which is when a hostile Congress could demand program cuts as the price of extending the authority.
**The answer is statutory borrowing authority with a repayment schedule written into the original act,** so a draw executes the plan rather than requesting a bailout, and the debt has a visible end date.
