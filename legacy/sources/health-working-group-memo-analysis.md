# House Democrats' Health Care Working Group Memo: Catalog and Scenario Analysis

Oct 11, 2026 · @espen

## Bottom line

The memo's 96 recommendations sort into three very different agendas, and only a 2029 trifecta reaches the one that changes coverage. Before then, Democrats can enact the cheap bipartisan third and, with the Senate, buy temporary relief on ACA premiums.

| Scenario | In reach | Likely enacted | Ten-year gross spending | Ten-year net of memo offsets | Coverage effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. House only, 2027-28 | 30 of 96 | 12 to 18 | $40B to $90B | About neutral | None |
| 2. House and Senate, 2027-28 | 52 of 96 | 27 to 38 | $200B to $450B | -$100B to +$250B | 4M to 6M fewer uninsured, temporarily |
| 3. Trifecta, 2029-30 | 71 of 96 (86 without the filibuster) | 50 to 65 | $3.6T to $4.7T | +$2.1T to +$3.9T | 14M to 16M fewer uninsured by 2034 |

Scenarios 1 and 2 use the budget windows of their source scores. Scenario 3 is restated for a 2030-39 window, which is why it is larger than the sum of the source scores. If the filibuster is changed, a go-big bill reaches $5.5T to $7.3T gross over 2030-39 and leaves 7M to 10M uninsured.

- **Scenario 1** delivers permanent telehealth, Medicare Advantage prior authorization rules, drug patent reform, a second round of PBM changes and most of the food-safety section. Patients keep paying 2026 marketplace premiums and Medicaid work requirements start on schedule.
- **Scenario 2** adds a two- to three-year restoration of ACA credits at about 60% odds, riders that stop the 340B rebate pilot and WISeR, protected NIH funding, and a Medicare Advantage coding fix used as the offset. H.R. 1 stays on the books.
- **Scenario 3** repeals H.R. 1's health cuts, makes the credits permanent, caps traditional Medicare cost sharing at $5,000 and probably adds hearing and vision. More than half of the ten-year cost restores 2024 law. Medicare for All, Medicare for Kids and the 8% income cap do not pass.

The memo's largest gap is fiscal: it names $1 trillion or more over ten years in possible Medicare Advantage, drug and hospital savings but commits to none of them and proposes no revenue. Abortion, contraception, IVF, noncompete and antitrust items cost little and depend entirely on the filibuster.

## What the memo is

The memo is a menu, not a plan: 96 policy recommendations backed by about 120 named bills or policy ideas, sorted into three clocks, with no cost estimates, no offsets, and no ranking inside each clock. It is dated September 1, 2026, addressed to Leader Jeffries, and reports 93 meetings with 139 Members and 145 stakeholder groups.

Three features shape everything that follows.

- **The "100 hours" list is a House floor schedule, not an enactment forecast.** It holds the two largest spending items in the document (H.R. 1 repeal with permanent ACA credits, and Medicare dental, vision and hearing) alongside rule disapprovals and funding riders. A House majority can pass all 25; almost none become law in 2027 on that vote alone.
- **Roughly a third of the named bills have a Republican co-lead.** Lawler, Fitzpatrick, Joyce, Harshbarger, Bilirakis, Miller, Kiggans, Malliotakis, Massie, Cassidy, Collins, Grassley and Cornyn all appear. That bipartisan third is the realistic 2027-28 agenda.
- **The memo defers the hard choice.** Universal coverage is a first-year "comprehensive examination" of Medicare for All and three public option models, not a recommendation. It names 114 Medicare for All cosponsors and stops.

The memo's own savings claims are thin: $124 billion to $250 billion for No UPCODE, $4.7 billion for RADV extrapolation, and $2.5 trillion for the Save MEDICARE Act, each over ten years. The last figure is about double the $1.2 trillion to $1.3 trillion in total ten-year Medicare Advantage overpayments that [CRFB projects](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/new-data-suggests-ma-overpayments-13-trillion-over-next-decade) from MedPAC data, so treat it as unverified.

## Catalog of recommendations

All 96 recommendations are listed below with the vehicle the memo names, the memo's clock, and the earliest scenario in which each could realistically become law. Fiscal figures are ten-year federal effects; "approx." marks figures recalled from earlier scores rather than checked for this document, and "unscored" means no public estimate exists.

**Tier key**

- **T1**: enactable with a Democratic House, Republican Senate and President Trump (bipartisan bill or must-pass rider)
- **T2**: needs a Democratic Senate and appropriations leverage
- **T3**: needs a trifecta; fits budget reconciliation (51 Senate votes)
- **T3F**: needs a trifecta plus 60 Senate votes or a filibuster change (non-budgetary)
- **T4**: stretch even with a trifecta

## Component assessment

About a dozen items carry nearly all the money and coverage; the other 80-plus are cheap, regulatory, or rights-based. The memo's real fiscal shape is a $1.5 trillion restoration, $0.6 trillion to $0.8 trillion in Medicare upgrades, $1 trillion or more in possible savers, and an unpriced set of coverage expansions, all over ten years in source-year scores.

| Component | Ten-year federal effect | What it changes | Path |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Repeal H.R. 1 Medicaid and CHIP provisions | Cost $911B net, 2025-34 | 7.5M fewer uninsured in 2034; ends work requirements that start January 1, 2027 | Reconciliation |
| Repeal H.R. 1 marketplace provisions and 2025 integrity rule | Cost $272B plus $40B | About 3.2M more insured in 2035 | Reconciliation |
| Permanent enhanced premium credits | Cost $350B | 3.8M more insured in 2035; benchmark premiums 7.6% lower | Reconciliation, or temporary deal sooner |

Figures for the first three rows are from [CBO's September 2025 analysis](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61734) and [Georgetown CCF's summary of CBO's final H.R. 1 scores](https://ccf.georgetown.edu/2025/08/14/new-cbo-health-coverage-estimates-of-budget-reconciliation-law/).

Five judgments on design.

1. **The savers are real but the memo does not commit to them.** Medicare Advantage overpayment is [$76 billion in 2026 per MedPAC](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/feds-will-overpay-medicare-advantage-plans-76b-year-medpac-estimates). Capturing half would cover most of dental, vision, hearing and the cap. More than half of beneficiaries are in Medicare Advantage, so every dollar saved shows up as a benefit or premium change for a voter.
2. **New Part B benefits raise Part B premiums.** Enrollees pay about 25% of Part B costs. A dental, vision and hearing benefit near $60 billion a year in the 2030s adds roughly $18 a month per enrollee unless offset. The memo is silent on this.
3. **The Byrd rule splits the drug and cost-sharing agenda.** In 2022 the parliamentarian removed the commercial insulin cap and commercial inflation rebates from the Inflation Reduction Act. The same fate awaits the commercial $2,000 cap, EpiPen caps, free primary care visits and no-cost maternity care without 60 votes.
4. **Several vehicles are stale or thin.** Bridge to Medicaid runs 2026 through 2028. The residency bill starts in FY2026. Fifteen listed bills have one or zero cosponsors. Eight are "to be introduced."
5. **Some items pull against each other.** The pharmacist-services bill is backed by CVS Health, McKesson and Cardinal Health, which Section III proposes to break up. Nurse ratios and nursing home staffing mandates raise costs for the rural hospitals other sections try to rescue.

## Scenario 1: Democratic House, Republican Senate, President Trump (2027-28)

A House majority alone enacts the bipartisan third of the memo and little else: roughly 12 to 18 of the 30 T1 items, near budget-neutral, with no change to the H.R. 1 coverage losses. Markets put a Democratic House at about 85% and treat the Senate as a coin flip, so this is the floor case, not the central one.

**What the House can do alone.** Pass all 25 "100 hours" items for the record, hold oversight hearings on WISeR, ACIP and NIH terminations, and write its positions into House appropriations bills. None of that is law.

**What can actually be signed.** Three vehicles matter.

1. **A late-2027 health extenders package.** Medicare telehealth flexibilities expire December 31, 2027, and health center funding runs year to year. That forces a bill. The [February 2026 package](https://www.americanactionforum.org/insight/health-care-extenders-key-provisions-in-the-consolidated-appropriations-act-2026/) is the template: it carried PBM reform, telehealth and health center money with Trump's signature.
2. **Annual appropriations.** The House can decline to fund things, but a Republican Senate and a veto pen cap what riders survive.
3. **Stand-alone bipartisan bills** with Republican leads in both chambers.

| Likely enacted | Possible | Passed by House only |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Telehealth made permanent or extended five years or more | Two-year partial restoration of ACA credits with income cap (about one in three) | H.R. 1 repeal |
| Medicare Advantage prior authorization rules (72-hour decisions, reporting) | PBM-pharmacy separation | Abortion, contraception, IVF rights |
| Patent package: pay-for-delay, product hopping, citizen petitions | No UPCODE as an offset | Dental, vision, hearing; $5,000 cap |
| PBM round two for federal employee plans | H-1B fee exemption for clinicians | Drug negotiation expansion |
| Step therapy exceptions; copay assistance counted | Baby food heavy-metal limits | Dream and Promise Act |
| Pharmacist test-and-treat; kidney care; breakthrough devices | Site-neutral expansion (CMS is already moving by rule) | WISeR, 340B and public charge blocks |
| Low-wage-index fix; lab payment fix; DSP code; nursing and medic studies | One-year physician pay patch | Home care wage rule; sensitive locations |
| Infant formula testing; supplement listing; Food is Medicine pilots | Glyphosate funding limit | Medicare Advantage overhaul |

**Topline.** New spending of roughly $40 billion to $90 billion over ten years, mostly telehealth, physician patches and health centers, offset by patent, PBM and site-neutral savings. Add $50 billion to $60 billion in total, about $25 billion to $30 billion a year, if a two-year ACA credit deal happens.

**What patients see.**

- Coverage: no improvement. Medicaid work requirements begin January 1, 2027. [Manatt projects](https://www.manatt.com/insights/white-papers/2026/cms-medicaid-work-requirements-rule-a-50-state-analysis-of-additional-coverage-losses-ffy-2027-2034) 2.8 million Medicaid losses in FY2027 under the CMS rule, rising to 9.2 million by 2034.
- Marketplace: enrollment stays near 17.5 million, down from 22.1 million; the 114% average jump in premium payments and the $1,000 rise in deductibles persist unless the credit deal lands.
- Medicare: about 35 million Medicare Advantage enrollees get faster prior authorization answers; telehealth stops being a cliff.
- Drugs: generics and biosimilars arrive somewhat sooner; patients on copay cards stop paying their deductible twice.

**Realism check.** The fall 2025 shutdown over ACA credits ended without them. Holding one chamber improves the odds but does not change who signs. The memo's Section VII is the best fit for this scenario: it is cheap, popular, and aligned with what HHS says it wants.

## Scenario 2: Democratic House and Senate, President Trump (2027-28)

A Democratic Congress turns the memo's defensive items into law through spending bills and makes a temporary ACA credit restoration more likely than not, but still cannot repeal H.R. 1 or add Medicare benefits. Everything in Scenario 1 carries over. The gain is 15 to 20 more items from T2, most of them riders and partial funding.

**What changes with the Senate.** Democrats write both chambers' appropriations bills, so the President must sign Democratic text or own a shutdown. They also control the floor, confirmations and the conference on every must-pass bill. What does not change: the veto, and a 60-vote Senate for anything outside reconciliation.

**Reconciliation becomes available and useless for enactment.** Democrats could pass H.R. 4849 through both chambers with 51 Senate votes and draw a veto. That has value: it forces a CBO score, tests which Medicare Advantage and drug offsets hold 218 and 51, and sets the 2029 bill in type. It restores no one's coverage.

| NIH funding floors with anti-impoundment language | Home care wage rule blocked by rider | Drug negotiation expansion |
| No UPCODE and site-neutral payment, used as offsets | PBM-pharmacy separation | Dream and Promise Act; sensitive locations |
| Physician pay indexed at a partial rate (MEI minus 1) | 1,000 to 2,000 new residency slots | Break-ups, private equity limits |
| Health center increase (not doubling); Navigator funding restored | Larger, retargeted rural fund | Coverage gap; HCBS mandate |
| Title X funds released; 12-month postpartum coverage required | Dyes out of school meals; front-of-package labels | Universal coverage models |

**The plausible trade.** Trump officials have been hawkish on Medicare Advantage coding, and CMS is expanding site-neutral payment by rule. A deal that pairs those savers with ACA credits and physician pay gives each side something to claim. It is the one large bipartisan bargain in the memo's material.

**Topline.** Gross new spending of roughly $200 billion to $450 billion over ten years: ACA credits $60 billion to $100 billion in total for a two- to three-year extension, Medicaid delays $50 billion to $150 billion across the window, the rest physician pay, health centers, residency slots and rural money. Offsets of $175 billion to $425 billion from coding, site-neutral and patent provisions. Net effect ranges from slightly deficit-reducing to about $250 billion in new deficits.

**What patients see.**

- Marketplace: most of the 4.6 million who left return while the credits last. A 60-year-old couple just over 400% of poverty gets back a premium cap near 8.5% of income. The cliff returns when the extension ends.
- Medicaid: a statutory fix to the CMS rule would avoid about 1.8 million of the 8.2 million average annual coverage losses Manatt projects. The requirement itself stays.
- Safety net: 340B hospitals and health centers avoid fronting cash to manufacturers; rural hospitals see more targeted relief.
- Medicare: standalone Part D premiums stay near $36 a month instead of jumping; Part B premiums fall slightly as coding payments shrink, then rise slightly from physician indexing.
- Net coverage effect: the projected 14 million increase in the uninsured is trimmed by 4 million to 6 million in 2028-29, temporarily.

## Scenario 3: Democratic trifecta (2029-30)

A trifecta makes the restoration and the Medicare upgrades enactable in one reconciliation bill costing $3.6 trillion to $4.7 trillion gross over 2030-39, or $360 billion to $470 billion a year on average, and leaves the memo's universal coverage section as unlikely as it is today. With a Senate majority of 50 to 53, the binding limits are the Byrd rule, the Medicare Advantage lobby, and the price tag.

**Three tracks run at once.**

1. **Executive action, weeks one to twelve.** A Democratic administration ends WISeR and the 340B rebate pilot, withdraws the public charge rule, reconstitutes ACIP, restores the medical-debt credit rule, the junk-plan limits, home care wage protections and the sensitive-locations policy, and releases NIH and Title X funds. About ten memo items need no vote.
2. **Reconciliation, months three to nine.** Everything in T3.
3. **Sixty-vote or post-filibuster bills.** Everything in T3F. If the filibuster stands, none of the rights or market-structure bills pass and the Hyde amendment stays in appropriations.

**What patients see by 2031.**

- Coverage: about 14 million fewer uninsured in 2034 than under current law, plus up to 1.4 million from the coverage gap. The uninsured count returns to roughly its 2024 level of 26 million to 27 million instead of climbing toward 40 million (approx.).
- Marketplace: premium payments fall back by about half on average; subsidized share returns above 90%; benchmark premiums run 7.6% lower.
- Traditional Medicare: no one pays more than $5,000 a year in Part A and B cost sharing. Hearing aids and glasses are covered within two years; dental takes longer because few dentists bill Medicare today.
- Medicare Advantage: fewer zero-premium plans and slimmer extras as payments fall toward parity. This is the main visible loser.
- Drugs: 50 negotiated prices a year instead of 20. The $2,000 commercial cap and $35 commercial insulin happen only with 60 votes.
- Maternity and reproductive care: Momnibus grants and 12-month postpartum coverage arrive; abortion, contraception and IVF rights and the $3,000 maternity cost-sharing ban depend on the filibuster.
- Long-term care: HCBS waiting lists of 600,000 begin to shrink; no new universal benefit.

**Timing problem.** By January 2029 work requirements will have run for two years, the enhanced credits will have been gone for three, and some rural hospitals will have closed. A law signed in mid-2029 restores eligibility for 2030. Re-enrollment took years after the ACA and will again. Closed hospitals do not reopen.

## Scenario 3F: going big after a filibuster change

With majority cloture and a unified caucus, four packages become live, and the two most plausible are the ones that regulate prices and add public coverage beside employer plans rather than replacing them. Each sits on top of the Scenario 3 reconciliation bill. Costs below are incremental to that base.

**Assumptions.** 52 to 54 Democratic senators, a House majority near 230, and a rules change that lets legislation pass with a simple majority after extended debate. Procedure stops being the limit. Money, hospital revenue and floor time take its place.

**Where 2029 would differ from 2009.** There is no bloc like Lieberman, Nelson and Lincoln. Medicare Advantage overpayment and site-neutral payment are consensus positions. Insurers, PBMs and private equity have few defenders in either party. What has not changed: 164 million people hold employer coverage, hospitals are the largest employer in many districts, and every expansion needs taxes the memo never names.

| B. Public option guarantee | Medicare-rate public plan on and off the marketplaces; automatic enrollment; richer cost-sharing help; employer "firewall" removed | Costs $1.6T to $2.2T | 7M to 10M | 35% |
| C. Medicare ladder | Medicare at 60; Medicare for Kids to 18; Medicare at Home; full dental | Costs $3.4T to $4.7T | About 21M | Age 60: 40%. Kids: 15%. Home care: 30% |

### A. Prices and power

This package is the cheapest to the Treasury and the most expensive to industry. It is every T3F item in the catalog, moved as three or four bills.

- **Contents.** Negotiated drug prices and inflation rebates extended to private plans; $2,000 commercial drug cap; $35 insulin, EpiPens and inhalers; a 200% of Medicare ceiling on commercial hospital prices with rural and safety-net exceptions; Break Up Big Medicine; PBM-pharmacy separation; private equity and corporate-practice limits; noncompete ban; junk-plan rules; three free primary care and mental health visits; no cost sharing for maternity care; data-broker ban. Abortion, contraception and IVF rights and the Dream and Promise Act move separately.
- **Cost.** [CRFB estimated](https://www.crfb.org/papers/capping-hospital-prices) that the hospital cap alone cuts deficits by $216 billion, commercial premiums by $899 billion (about 6%) and cost sharing by $99 billion over 2021-30. The savings reach the Treasury because lower premiums become taxable wages. The benefit mandates push premiums up about 1%, by my estimate.
- **For people.** Family employer premiums run $2,000 to $2,300 a year lower than otherwise by the early 2030s (approx.), showing up over time as wages. No one with private insurance pays more than $2,000 a year for drugs. A birth costs about $3,000 less out of pocket. Clinicians can change jobs. Coverage does not expand.
- **Who loses.** Hospitals give up roughly $150 billion a year in commercial revenue in the 2030s. UnitedHealth, CVS and Cigna divest providers and pharmacies within a year. Expect litigation over divestiture and rate limits.

### B. Public option guarantee

This is the shortest route to near-universal coverage and the memo's most defensible reading of "commit to universal coverage." It combines the Medicare-X and CHOICE bills with Easy Enrollment.

- **Contents.** A public plan paying near Medicare rates, sold nationwide and open to workers offered employer coverage; automatic enrollment of the uninsured into Medicaid, a zero-premium plan or the public plan; benchmark raised from silver to gold; premiums capped at 8% of income for everyone.
- **Cost.** Urban Institute's 2019 modeling of this design put new federal spending at $1.5 trillion over 2020-29, or $122 billion in the first year. Moved to 2030-39 and net of what the base already funds, that is $1.6 trillion to $2.2 trillion. A public plan at Medicare rates lowers the subsidy bill.
- **For people.** [Urban estimated](https://commonwealthfund.org/publications/issue-briefs/2021/jun/how-auto-enrollment-can-achieve-near-universal-coverage) that comprehensive auto-enrollment covers 24.6 million of the uninsured. Those left are mostly undocumented residents. Public plan premiums run 10% to 25% below private ones (approx.). Typical marketplace deductibles fall by more than half.
- **The stretch version** is the memo's 8% cap on premiums plus out-of-pocket costs across all payers. Urban's richer-subsidy variant adds about $0.85 trillion over 2030-39. Reaching out-of-pocket costs and employer plans adds perhaps $1.5 trillion to $2.5 trillion and has no worked design.
- **Who loses.** Insurers lose share in concentrated markets. Hospitals lose again on public plan rates. Some people are enrolled and billed for premiums they did not choose.

### C. Medicare ladder

This package expands the program by age from both ends. It is the most expensive per newly insured person, because most people it enrolls already have coverage.

- **Medicare at 60.** $155 billion over six years, or $300 billion to $360 billion over 2030-39. CBO expects 7.3 million enrollees, of whom 0.4 million were uninsured. Early retirees save thousands; employers shed their costliest workers; hospitals lose commercial rates on a high-use group.
- **Medicare for Kids to 18.** My rough range is $2.5 trillion to $3.6 trillion over 2030-39, net of the federal Medicaid and CHIP dollars it replaces, or about $1.4 trillion if states keep paying their share. Running it to 26, as the memo proposes, would cost $4.0 trillion to $5.5 trillion. It covers 4.4 million uninsured children, ends churn for 35 million on Medicaid, and removes dependent premiums for working parents.
- **Medicare at Home.** $550 billion to $700 billion over 2030-39 for a narrow design. It gives middle-income seniors a home care benefit without spending down to Medicaid.
- **Odds.** Medicare at 60 is the likeliest single addition to any package. The children's program has no bill text, and states would fight over the Medicaid dollars.

### D. Medicare for All

Single payer remains out of reach even under these assumptions. [CBO's 2020 analysis](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/56811) put new federal spending at $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion in 2030 alone, with national health spending between $0.7 trillion lower and $0.3 trillion higher. Out-of-pocket costs nearly vanish and coverage is universal. Over 2030-39 the cost is $40 trillion to $60 trillion. The financing requires a broad-based tax, and the bill has 114 House cosponsors against a majority of 218.

## Method and uncertainties

The scenario numbers are assembled estimates with wide error bars, and the odds are judgment. Five caveats matter most.

- **Budget windows.** Scenarios 1 and 2 use source-year scores, with windows from 2020-29 to 2026-35. Scenario 3 and the go-big packages are restated for 2030-39 using CMS growth projections: 5.8% a year for national health spending, 7.8% for Medicare, 5.2% for private insurance and 6.4% for Medicaid.
- **Unscored items.** The $5,000 cap, HCBS mandate, Medicare for Kids, the 8% cap, Medicaid dental and the commercial drug provisions have no public score. My ranges for them are rough. Most recalled figures have now been checked against sources; those still marked "approx." are order-of-magnitude judgments without a checked source.
- **Interactions.** Repealing H.R. 1 changes the cost of the coverage gap fix and auto-enrollment. Medicare Advantage cuts change Part B premiums and plan benefits in ways that depend on insurer response.
- **Senate math.** Scenario 3 assumes 50 to 53 Democratic senators. At 50, one senator sets the size of the bill, as in 2021-22, when dental and vision were dropped first and hearing after.
- **Provenance.** I found the June 23, 2026 announcements of the working group but no published reporting on this memo, so the analysis rests on the attached text as given.

**Where the memo's own figures look shaky.**
