# The go-big bill: detail and financing

The most plausible maximal bill costs $5.5 trillion to $7.3 trillion gross over 2030-39 and leaves $3.6 trillion to $6.1 trillion to finance after its own offsets. Taxes on high incomes and corporations alone can cover the low end. Covering the high end takes a broad-based tax or a smaller bill.

This tab assumes a 2029 trifecta, 52 to 54 Democratic senators and a filibuster change, as in Scenario 3F on the main tab. All dollar figures are ten-year totals for 2030-39 unless marked per year. Ranges are my estimates built from the sources listed, not official scores.

## The bill by title

| Title | Main provisions | Gross cost or savings, 2030-39 | Vehicle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I. Restore | Repeal H.R. 1 Medicaid and marketplace cuts; permanent enhanced ACA credits; close the coverage gap | Costs $2.25T to $2.65T | Reconciliation |
| II. Medicare upgrades | $5,000 cap in traditional Medicare; dental, vision and hearing; physician pay indexed to inflation; eligibility at 60 | Costs $1.2T to $1.6T | Reconciliation |
| III. Public option guarantee | Medicare-rate public plan on and off the marketplaces; automatic enrollment; richer cost-sharing help; employer firewall removed | Costs $1.6T to $2.2T | Regular order |
| IV. Medicaid, long-term care, safety net | HCBS down payment and care workforce; children's continuous coverage; immigrant waiting period; Medicaid dental; health centers doubled; 14,000 residency slots; Momnibus; Title X | Costs $0.4T to $0.85T | Reconciliation |
| V. Prices and market power | Commercial drug negotiation, rebates and $2,000 cap; hospital prices capped at 200% of Medicare; PBM and insurer divestitures; noncompete ban; no-cost primary and maternity visits | Saves $350B to $450B | Regular order |
| VI. Medicare payment reform | Medicare Advantage coding and benchmark changes; 50 negotiated drugs a year; site-neutral payment | Saves $0.8T to $1.4T | Reconciliation |

Abortion, contraception and IVF rights, the Dream and Promise Act and the food-safety bills move as separate bills. They carry little or no federal cost.

## What households see

| Household | Today | Under the bill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marketplace enrollee | Premium payment roughly doubled in 2026; deductible up about $1,000 | 2025-level premiums restored; public plan priced below private plans; gold-level cost sharing |
| Family with employer coverage | $26,993 average premium in 2025 | Premium about 6% lower, worth $2,000 to $2,300 a year by the early 2030s; $2,000 drug cap; no-cost maternity care; option to take the public plan |
| Traditional Medicare enrollee | No limit on cost sharing; no dental, vision or hearing | $5,000 cap; hearing and vision by 2031, dental later; Part B premium up about $18 a month |
| Medicare Advantage enrollee | Extra benefits funded by overpayment | Fewer zero-premium plans and slimmer extras; traditional Medicare becomes a closer substitute |
| Adult aged 60 to 64 | Employer plan, marketplace or uninsured | Medicare eligibility |
| Low-income adult in a non-expansion state | No affordable option | Zero-premium coverage, enrolled automatically |
| Medicaid expansion adult | Work requirement from 2027 | Requirement repealed |

## Who pays before any new tax

The bill's own offsets fall on three industries. Hospitals lose roughly $150 billion a year in commercial revenue in the 2030s and more as the public plan grows. Medicare Advantage insurers lose $50 billion to $100 billion a year. Drug makers lose $20 billion to $35 billion a year across Medicare and commercial markets. Each has a rural, safety-net or innovation argument that Congress will be asked to buy back, which is why the offset range is wide.

## The financing gap

The gap is $3.6 trillion to $6.1 trillion, or $360 billion to $610 billion a year, about 0.8% to 1.3% of GDP. The memo proposes nothing to fill it.

### Three ways to close it

| Financing package | Components | Raises, 2030-39 | Share of $3.6T gap | Share of $6.1T gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Top-end only | Medicare and investment tax changes; corporate rate to 28%; 39.6% top rate; minimum tax above $100M; buyback and corporate minimum taxes | $3.8T to $4.1T | 100%-plus | 62% to 67% |
| B. Health-financed | Medicare and investment tax changes; 1% health payroll contribution; cap on the employer exclusion | $3.7T to $3.8T | 100%-plus | About 61% |
| C. Full coverage | Package A plus the 1% payroll contribution and the exclusion cap | $6.5T to $6.9T | 100%-plus | 100%-plus |

Three cautions.

- **Package A uses general-fund revenue that other priorities also want.** The same provisions are the usual pay-fors for the child tax credit, child care and housing. Social Security, Medicare hospital insurance and highways draw on separate dedicated channels.
- **Package B breaks the $400,000 pledge.** A 1% payroll contribution costs a worker earning $60,000 about $600 a year, against roughly $2,000 in lower family premiums if the hospital cap holds. The exclusion cap and the hospital price cap pull the same lever, so combining them raises less than the sum.
- **Nothing here pays for the larger packages.** Medicare for Kids or Medicare at Home would each need another broad-based tax.

### Shrinking the gap instead

| Change | Effect on the gap, 2030-39 |
| --- | --- |
| Capture all Medicare Advantage overpayment, not a third to 60% of it | Lowers it $0.6T to $1.1T |
| Drop Medicare at 60 | Lowers it $0.3T to $0.36T |
| Start dental after the budget window | Lowers it about $0.3T |
| Reference-price drugs to other countries, as H.R. 3 did | Lowers it perhaps $0.3T to $0.5T, my estimate |
| Finance part by borrowing | Any share; 0.8% to 1.3% of GDP a year if all of it |

A bill that drops Medicare at 60, delays dental and takes Package A is about 95% financed at the midpoint of the ranges without a broad-based tax.
