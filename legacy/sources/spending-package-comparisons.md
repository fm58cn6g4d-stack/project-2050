## The alternatives

**Current commitments** carry the selected working spending agenda. Its financing is still illustrative.

**Focused recovery** lowers AI transition support from $120B to $80B, participation from $100B to $70B, additional rental assistance from $135B to $100B, and the climate-credit envelope from $900B to $700B. It preserves the adopted million-voucher base, full education scope, health restoration, Social Security benefit design and new development programs. The four cuts total $305B. They would mean less transition assistance, fewer or less generous participation supports, fewer additional successful leases and a narrower climate subsidy offer. Their distribution cannot be inferred from fiscal savings. The $700B climate figure is anchored to the domain's restoration-only comparator, but this screen does not adopt that comparator's loss of refundability: preserving low-income access would require targeting other eligibility or subsidy amounts and a fresh score.

**Infrastructure rebalance** reduces the climate-credit ceiling to $600B and allocates $100B to grid delivery, adaptation and the workforce from 2032, for $200B net window savings. This is a budget allocation to test, not evidence that $100B buys the required infrastructure or compensates for lost emissions reductions. The replacement is modeled as permanent; the inherited credit profile has a different maturity path. This distinction materially reduces long-run savings. The component portfolio and benefit/cost analysis must precede a recommendation to adopt it.

Neither alternative trims the shared $100B delivery contingency simply because its individual uses remain uncertain. Nor does it call relocation of costs to a trust fund a saving.

**None passes the model's debt-nonincreasing test throughout 2045–56.** The existing-reforms case keeps already-modeled corporate/high-wealth reforms and removes additional broad and upper income contributions. The upper-income comparator adds four points using the existing $100K single/$200K joint threshold illustration; it is not exclusively a tax on the wealthy. Both retain the unselected illustrative education/SS payroll parameters and $300 adult credit. These cases are not a finalized progressive revenue envelope. Downside uses the model's existing receipt/cost assumptions, not a probability forecast.

The finding is consequential: cuts of this scale do not solve the permanent financing problem. It would be premature to reduce housing or participation benefits merely to improve a ten-year headline while leaving long-run solvency unresolved. My present preference is to investigate the climate reallocation for substantive value, preserve housing/participation as priorities, and complete the corporate/capital/high-income revenue reconciliation before recommending the focused package. If additional credible revenue is insufficient, larger permanent choices must be made openly with Espen.

## Interpretation and next decisions

The grid investment may improve supply and bills, but no dynamic growth receipts or unmodeled health/emissions savings are credited. Credit ceilings may require statutory eligibility changes; they are not automatic savings from simply appropriating less for uncapped tax credits. The retained historical component profiles are planning proxies. Borrowing cost responds through the shared debt model.

Household results from the earlier tax screen do not apply to these spending packages. This comparison deliberately makes no new D3 claim. It needs counts of people losing or waiting for benefits, worker/nonworker incidence, regional housing constraints, employer wage incidence and climate bill effects. The same unit costs and take-up assumptions must be used for both the retained and removed benefits.

The next useful comparison should test additional corporate/capital-income receipts against one larger permanent spending redesign, not generate dozens of arbitrary percentage cuts. Health and education are large enough to matter, but neither should be scaled indiscriminately: health components must separate price reductions, coverage and added benefits; education must preserve the current-law/gross-fund reconciliation. Espen will decide any strategic downsizing after those comparisons.
