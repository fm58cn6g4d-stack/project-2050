## 2. The political scenario — treat these as fixed

Do not re-litigate these. They are the stipulated world:

1. **Late 2026 — a "BUILD America 250"-style surface transportation reauthorization passes.** The IIJA's Division J advance appropriations expired September 30, 2026; a CR (P.L. 119-103) runs to December 11, 2026. This bill is negotiated under *divided-to-unified-Republican* government and must be bipartisan. It resolves the authorization cliff but does **not** solve the Highway Trust Fund, whose fuel taxes expire September 30, 2028 and which goes insolvent in FY2028. Assume it is a conventional reauthorization: generous to highways, modest and formula-driven for transit, nothing structural.
2. **Late 2026 — permitting reform passes**, resembling the recent bipartisan Senate bill. Assume meaningful NEPA timeline discipline and judicial review limits, not wholesale categorical exclusion.
3. **November 2026 — Democrats win the House, and possibly the Senate.** Divided government for the final two years of the Trump administration (2027–28). Nothing major passes in this window. It is a *positioning* window: hearings, messaging bills, committee reports, and the groundwork for 2029.
4. **November 2028 — Democratic trifecta.**
5. **2029–30 — one or two reconciliation packages**, climate- and fair-economy-focused. Byrd-constrained. The rail work assumes **$48.50B flows to rail** through this channel.
6. **2031 — the full surface transportation reauthorization.** This is where the rail package (ARCA) lands, and where the transit package should land its own centerpiece.
7. **2036 — the following reauthorization**, modeled as a second decade.

So you are designing a **multi-package sequence across four legislative vehicles**, not one bill: the 2026 bipartisan reauthorization, the 2027–28 positioning window, the 2029–30 reconciliation, the 2031 reauthorization, and the 2036 reauthorization. What goes where is a design question and a core part of the deliverable. Byrd-rule feasibility governs what can go in reconciliation; bipartisan tolerance governs what can go in 2026.

## 3. What already exists — the rail package you must cohere with

### 3.1 The package itself

**The American Rail Competitiveness Act of 2031 (ARCA)** — the rail title set of the 2031 reauthorization. Roughly thirteen titles plus a fiscal-envelope section and a second-decade section.

Canonical authorization total: **$254.03B over FY2031–35**, by year 39.95 / 47.29 / 52.07 / 56.13 / 58.59. Of that: Title II $59.95B, Title III $160.45B, Titles IV–XIII $33.63B. Plus a $25B readiness reserve and **$48.50B from the 2029–30 reconciliation**, for **$302.53B across FY2029–35**. The second decade (FY2036–45) is modeled at **$450B**.

Selected sections worth knowing: sec. 310 terminals ($20.20B), sec. 311 federally initiated corridors ($6.00B), sec. 312 long-distance implementation ($12.00B), sec. 313 corridor eligibility, Title X (interstate compacts, including advance congressional consent at sec. 1003), Title XI (operator-neutral access at 1101, horizontal separation as a design standard at 1104), Titles XII–XIII.

### 3.2 The fiscal architecture — this is the part you must not break

**SEC. 101 creates a reformed Surface Transportation Trust Fund with a rail account inside it.** Rail does *not* get a standalone trust fund. Three reasons: the FY2028 HTF insolvency and fuel-tax expiration is a forcing event that must produce a revenue bill, and a standalone fund has to find its own; the Byrd rule bars general-fund-to-trust-fund transfers, so a standalone fund can't be seeded through reconciliation; and the precedent is exact — the **Mass Transit Account was created in 1982 (P.L. 97-424) as the price of passing a five-cent fuel tax increase.** Transit didn't get its own trust fund; it got an account, and that account has outlived every authorization since.

**The load-bearing provision is SEC. 101(d):** no reduction to the highway or transit accounts, and the rail account must be derived from *newly enacted* revenue. Without it the bill reads as a raid on transit and dies. **Your package must be drafted so that it is the beneficiary of 101(d), not its victim — and you should consider whether the transit package wants a mirror-image protection of its own.**

**The revenue package** (legislated at the FY2028 forcing event):
- Fuel taxes retained and indexed
- EV fee $130/yr, PHEV $35/yr, escalating toward $200
- Per-mile fee phased 0.6¢ → 3.6¢ from FY2029 (anchor: 2.4¢/mile replaces all current HTF revenue, ~$44B)
- Aviation domestic segment surcharge $4 → $12 → **capped at $16** (anchor: $1/segment ≈ $0.70B/yr; the cap exists because the existing $5.30 segment fee is CPI-indexed and stacking puts a passenger at $24.47/segment by 2045 even at $16)

**Rail account shares of the STTF:** 8% FY2029–31, 15% FY2032–35, 22% FY2036–40, 28% FY2041–45.

**The fiscal discipline rule:** the combined transportation general-fund draw — rail's gap plus the HTF bailout — must not exceed its IIJA-era peak of **0.192% of GDP**. The rail program peaks at 0.143% in FY2029 and fits in every year. Rail's dedicated-funding share runs 19% → 33% → 77% → **101% by FY2041–45**.

**Parity target:** rail + transit converging to 1.00 of highway funding by FY2045, with a 0.2%-of-GDP reporting trigger.

Baseline for all of this (FY2026 enacted): FHWA obligation limitation **$62.657B**; transit formula **$14.641B** plus annual CIG **$1.7B** = **$16.341B** recurring; FRA passenger rail base **~$2.6B**. That is **24 : 6.3 : 1** on recurring money, or **4.0 : 1.3 : 1** counting IIJA. Rail and transit together are **30% of highways** recurring, **58%** with IIJA. The post-IIJA rail reversion is about a 95% cliff.

### 3.3 The transit/rail split — the handoff you are receiving

This is the assumption that connects the two packages, and **it is the single thing most likely to need revision based on your work.**

The parity envelope is a *combined* rail + transit number:
- **FY2031–35 combined: $279.6B** (~$56B/yr average)
- **FY2036–45 combined: ~$854.7B**

The recommended split is **60/40 transit/rail in FY2031–35, converging to 55/45 by FY2041–45**, anchored on the IIJA-demonstrated ratio (~$21.6B/yr transit vs ~$13.2B/yr rail) rather than on a need estimate — observed congressional behavior is a stronger anchor than any model. The historical range runs 86/14 (FY2026 recurring) to ~60/40 (IIJA).

At 60%, transit's FY2031–35 envelope is **$167.8B, about $33.6B/yr** — roughly double its current recurring base.

**The framework behind the split, which you should test rather than assume:**

- **Transit's need is a flow; rail's is a stock.** Transit's dominant driver is state of good repair — a backlog in the neighborhood of $180B that does not retire when funded, plus post-2020 operating gaps. It is a treadmill that grows with the asset base and cannot be deferred without service failures. Rail's dominant driver is construction: lumpy, front-loaded, finite per corridor, and schedule-slippable.
- **Therefore: transit gets a dollar floor, rail gets the residual.** Build transit's floor from (a) indexed formula base, (b) a legislated SGR amortization schedule, (c) CIG at a stated level. Rail takes what the growing envelope leaves, and **rail bears the forecast risk.**
- **Rail's share rises then falls.** It cannot absorb a large early share — the corridor pipeline delivers 19 corridors by FY2031 in both the base and accelerated cases, because trains already funded and building cannot be accelerated. The taper is post-2045 on current modeling but the trigger should be legislated now; that is what buys transit's consent to the rail surge.
- **The pivotal question is not the ratio.** It is **49 U.S.C. 5307's bar on federal operating assistance for large urbanized areas.** That is a statutory question, not a funding-level question, and it moves the split more than any need estimate. If you recommend opening operating eligibility, the combined operating gaps of the major systems add several billion a year to transit's floor and rail's residual shrinks to match. **Resolve operating eligibility before fixing the split.**
- **Pressure relief:** don't anchor on "transit = urban, rail = intercity." Every new rail corridor generates station-access demand. An explicit eligibility letting rail capital fund station-area access and feeder service — or a small shared account — converts part of the split fight into a joint claim.

If your analysis says the split should be 65/35 or 55/45 or time-varying in a different shape, **say so and show the work.** I would rather revise the rail package than carry a number I can't defend.

### 3.4 Statutory moves the rail package makes (for consistency checking)

- **49 U.S.C. 25101(h)** — Corridor ID eligibility: strikes the 750-mile ceiling and the "formerly operated by Amtrak" limb (at sec. 313; note an earlier duplicate at 301(c) was consolidated away)
- **49 U.S.C. 24712(g)(2)** — the actual operating-commitment chokepoint (*not* 24911, a correction made mid-project)
- **49 U.S.C. 22908(e)** — the restoration/enhancement operating ramp: (e)(1) six years non-renewable, (e)(2) six simultaneously active nationwide, (e)(3) a 90/80/70/60/50/30 federal share schedule
- **49 U.S.C. 24308** — Amtrak's privilege package: (a) compulsory access at incremental cost with an STB order in 90 days, (c) preference "by or for Amtrak", (d)–(e) compelled speeds and frequencies, (f) OTP damages. Title XI converts this to **operator-neutral access** by search-and-replace rather than by building a new regime.
- **49 U.S.C. 24711** and **49 CFR part 269** — the competitive operations pilot, whose filing window closed around March 4, 2018 under §269.7(b)(1)
- **49 U.S.C. 24905(c)** — the NEC Cost Allocation Policy as a de facto access-charge regime
- **PRIIA §209** (uncodified, a note under 49 U.S.C. 24101) and **49 U.S.C. 24712(a)(7)(A)** bloc-voting committees
- **49 CFR part 238** Tiers I/II/III, **83 FR 59182** (Nov 21, 2018), §238.201(b)(2) and Appendix G, §238.703(b), §238.705, and the validated-model gate

Evidence the rail work leaned on: Mizutani, Smith, Nash and Uranishi (2015) — vertical separation break-even at **62.72 train-km per route-km**, and horizontal passenger/freight separation yielding a **24% cost reduction**. The package therefore *rejects* Network Rail-style vertical separation and uses operator-neutral access plus horizontal separation as a design standard. On permitting: NEPA reform's cost effect decomposes into five channels totaling **8–15% of capital**, against two counter-channels (acceleration premiums and design-immaturity change orders).

- **5309** Capital Investment Grants — New Starts, Small Starts, Core Capacity; the ratings process; federal share limits; the Expedited Project Delivery Pilot. Plus **49 CFR part 611**.
- **5310** enhanced mobility, **5311** rural, **5312** research, **5324** emergency relief

- **5323(j)** Buy America and **49 CFR part 661**; **5323(a)(1)(D)** charter and school bus rules

- ADA paratransit obligations, **49 CFR part 37**

Data sources to pull from rather than estimate: the **National Transit Database**, FTA's apportionment tables, the DOT **Conditions and Performance** report for the SGR backlog, CIG project profiles and the annual CIG report to Congress for the capital pipeline, and agency budget documents for the operating gaps at the systems in crisis (WMATA, BART, SEPTA, MTA, CTA and others — identify the real list rather than taking mine).

## 5. The design questions I want answered

1. **Operating assistance.** Should the package open federal operating eligibility for large urbanized areas, and if so how — permanently, conditionally, with a maintenance-of-effort requirement, with a performance or fare-recovery condition, time-limited, or as a formula tier? What does it cost, and what does it do to the transit/rail split? What's the counterargument from the people who built the bar in the first place?
2. **State of good repair.** Is a legislated amortization schedule the right instrument? Over what period, against what backlog measure, and with what accountability for agencies that defer anyway? Does 5326's asset-management regime need teeth?
3. **CIG reform.** The ratings process and the local-match structure are the main throttle on new fixed-guideway capacity. What changes, and does any of it survive a bipartisan 2026 vehicle?
4. **The capital pipeline.** Which projects and systems actually expand, on what clock? Mirror the rail work's central insight: **planning and capital delivery run on different clocks**, and you cannot accelerate what is already funded and building. Expect your near-term service numbers to be insensitive to funding increases, and expect that to be a finding rather than a disappointment.
5. **Package sequencing.** What goes in the 2026 bipartisan bill, the 2029–30 reconciliation, the 2031 reauthorization, and 2036? Run the Byrd-rule test explicitly: new mandatory general-fund spending, excise taxes to a trust fund, FCRA credit subsidy, and terms and conditions on mandatory outlays are permitted; general-fund-to-trust-fund transfers, obligation-limited contract authority, reauthorizing annually-appropriated programs, and slow-spending programs are not.
6. **Contract authority vs. appropriations.** Which instrument for which program, and does the NEPA "substantial Federal control and responsibility" / predominant-control problem bite the way it does on the rail side? Formula apportionment with state or local project selection is the usual escape; check whether it works here.
7. **Labor and workforce.** 5333(b), operator shortages, and the apprenticeship and wage questions. Don't leave this as a gesture.
8. **The split recommendation.** Your own number, with the work shown, and an explicit statement of what it does to ARCA's 2.27× parity ratio in FY2031–35.

## 7. How to proceed

1. **Gather data first.** Verify the statutory map and pull the real numbers before you touch any document or output format. Do not open an output-format skill or start drafting until you have the substance. (The one exception: if you're going to produce the bill architecture doc, create its skeleton — title and section outline — as your first call, then research, then fill it.)
2. **Then give me a plan** before you build: what the packages are, what goes in which vehicle, what you'll model, what products you'll produce, and what you expect the hard problems to be. I'd like to react to that before you spend a lot of effort.
3. **Then execute**, in whatever order makes sense, keeping me posted briefly.
4. **Then audit.** Run a pass across everything for errors, inconsistencies, stale figures, and duplication, and present corrected products. On the rail side this pass caught $6.4B of double-counting, three coexisting generations of totals, a misattributed statutory citation, a fee increase that failed a stacking check, and a program that didn't exist. Assume yours will catch comparable things.

## 8. How I like to work

**Challenge me.** The rail work improved most at the points where I was told I was wrong. I claimed permitting reform had no cost effect and was pushed back on with a mechanism I hadn't considered; I floated an aviation fee level that failed a simple stacking check; I had a statutory citation wrong for several turns. If a number or a premise of mine doesn't survive contact with the evidence, say so plainly rather than building on it.

**Verify arithmetic in code.** Don't do fiscal tables by hand.

**Be concrete about statute.** "Amend 5307 to permit operating assistance" is not a provision. Give me the amendment.

**Flag assumptions as assumptions**, and tell me which ones the conclusions are actually sensitive to. The 60/40 split is a good example: it's load-bearing, it's an assumption, and it was labeled as such.

**Don't pad.** Dense is fine; filler isn't.
