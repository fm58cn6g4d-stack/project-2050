# Domain file: Courts and administrative law (CRT)

Canonical. This file covers staging inventory section 4, which is pointers. Its input is sources/courts-rebalancing-the-three-branches-2026.md, which governs on legal analysis; where this file differs from that review it is because a Governing Decision or a design rule applies, and it says so.

All figures are ten-year totals for 2030-39 in trillions. Basis tags: **sourced**, **scaled**, **estimate** (mine). Survival tags follow D1: ✔, ◐ (needs D2; F is decided), ✖.

**Tiers.** T1 integrity and capacity first, T2 statutory repairs, T3 structural reform used deliberately. The tiering is the file's main argument and comes from the Rebalancing review's sequencing: Package 1 builds the good-faith record that lowers the escalation cost of Package 3. Every row carries aggressive severability and a legislative record, per design rule 7.

## The one-bill question

The ledger merges Supreme Court ethics into the courts package (cut C11), giving one judiciary bill at 6 floor days. This file keeps that, with a caveat worth stating: CRT-1, CRT-2, CRT-3 and CRT-6 are the items with actual Republican sponsors or supporters (Tillis on judge shopping, Young and Lankford on judgeships, the Modernization Committee's unanimous OTA vote), and CRT-15 is the item that makes the package partisan. Bundling them means the term-limits fight sets the vote count for the judgeship bill.

| Option | Design | Effect | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CRT-P1 (chosen) | One judiciary bill: T1 items plus CRT-15 | 6 floor days; term limits carried by the package | estimate (ledger) |
| CRT-P2 | Two bills: a T1 integrity bill on the bipartisan fast track, then CRT-15 separately | Probably 1 day plus 6; the T1 record is built before the structural fight, which is the Rebalancing sequencing | estimate |
| CRT-P3 | T1 only in the first Congress; CRT-15 in 2033 | Lowest risk; D1 says no 2033 queue, so this is a departure from a Decision | estimate |
| CRT-P4 (Talarico stress test) | Split CRT-1's phased judgeships into a stand-alone bill aimed at 60 votes; the rest as P2 (integrity bill, then CRT-15) | Takes one item off the contested floor list. The 2024 bill passed the Senate by unanimous consent before the veto; the model passed 74 judgeships 71-29 (scenario). Phasing across terms is what makes Republican votes plausible | estimate; scenario |

**P4 combines with P2.** P4 is not an alternative to P2 but a refinement of it: the judgeships leave the integrity bill and seek 60 votes, early, while the integrity bill and CRT-15 follow P2's order. The risk is that judgeships were the integrity bill's best bipartisan sweetener; without them, CRT-2 to CRT-6 carry less cover.

**Recommendation: P2.** It is the only version that follows the source's own sequencing argument, and it costs about one extra floor day under the merge and fast-track options Espen already accepted (C27). P1 is kept as chosen only because it is what the ledger currently carries; this is a live choice, not a settled one.

## T1 — Integrity and capacity

### CRT-1. Phased judgeships

**Design.** 66 district judgeships (63 permanent, 3 temporary) on the JUDGES Act lineage, with effective dates straddling at least two presidential terms.

**Why phased.** The 2024 bill (S. 4199) passed the Senate by unanimous consent and the House 236-173, and was vetoed Dec 23, 2024 — the clearest available demonstration that each party supports judgeships only when it expects to fill them. Phasing across terms is the reciprocity answer and the reason this row is the package's good-faith opener.

**Cost.** About $0.01T, range $0.00T to $0.02T (estimate): judgeships, chambers staff, courthouse space. No comprehensive judgeship legislation since 1990 and no new district judgeship since 2003, so the Judicial Conference's own request is the size.

### CRT-2. Judicial ethics and recusal

**Design.** A binding code with disclosure, gift, travel and amicus-funding rules. Recusal motions are decided by the Court's own members, with published reasons. Enforcement runs through disclosure, referral to Congress and appropriations conditions — not outside sanctions.

**Why that enforcement design.** This is the file's main departure from the old inventory, which proposed an enforceable outside recusal review panel. Outside override of a recusal decision is precisely where the Court's resistance is likeliest: Justice Alito's stated position is that no constitutional provision gives Congress authority to regulate the Supreme Court, "period" (WSJ interview, July 2023). Congress has applied 28 U.S.C. sec. 455 and the Ethics in Government Act to the justices for decades and they have complied, so disclosure-side rules sit on settled ground; the sanctions side does not. Polling is the strongest on this list: 89% support a strict ethics code (Marquette, Sept 2-9, 2026).

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Legal risk | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CRT-2A (chosen) | Binding code, self-decided recusal with published reasons, enforcement by disclosure and appropriations | Low-Medium | sourced (Rebalancing #8) |
| CRT-2B | Outside recusal review panel (SCERT Act, S. 1814 / H.R. 3513 as introduced) | Higher; the likely point of invalidation | sourced |
| CRT-2C | Office of Investigative Counsel reporting to Congress (H.R. 7692) | Higher | sourced |
| CRT-2D | Disclosure and gift rules only | Lowest; drops the binding code | estimate |

### CRT-3. Random assignment and venue

**Design.** Statutory district-wide random assignment for civil actions seeking to bar or mandate state or federal action, codifying the Judicial Conference's March 2024 policy — which is guidance only, and which the Northern District of Texas declined to adopt. Amend 28 U.S.C. sec. 1391(e) so challenges seeking relief beyond the parties go to D.D.C. or to three-judge district courts under sec. 2284, with direct appeal.

**Note on self-interest.** This is also the forum rule for challenges to this reform package itself (Instruments #5). It concentrates the stakes in D.D.C. rather than removing them — a shift, not a cure, and it should be described that way rather than as neutral housekeeping.

### CRT-4. Emergency-docket transparency

**Design.** Written explanation and recorded votes for any grant of a stay or injunction; no precedential weight for unexplained orders (answering *Trump v. Boyle*, 2025); codified stay standards on the *Nken* baseline; public docket statistics.

**The number.** Federal-government grant rates: 46% in Trump's first term, 31% under Biden, and 67% (SCOTUSblog) to 83% (Ballotpedia) in the second Trump term, depending on how cases are counted. The counting caveat belongs in the findings, not buried.

**Vulnerability.** Vote disclosure and some written explanation are probably accepted in practice. Dictating the substance of reasoning is the vulnerable part, and the predictable failure mode is boilerplate.

### CRT-5. Article I immigration court

**Design.** The Real Courts, Rule of Law Act of 2026 (H.R. 7836, Lofgren/Raskin/Johnson/Goldman, Mar 5, 2026; S. 5094), creating a court under Article I that is not an agency of, and is independent of, the executive branch, with trial, appellate and administrative divisions on the Tax Court model. Plus restored Article III habeas and pattern-and-practice jurisdiction curtailed by the REAL ID Act and 8 U.S.C. sec. 1252, with de novo review of law and, after *Wilkinson v. Garland* (2024), mixed questions. Due-process additions: appointed counsel for children and people with serious mental illness, access to A-files, limits on in absentia orders, bond hearings.

**Slaughter design.** *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 29, 2026) overruled *Humphrey's Executor*, so removal protection is the vulnerable point. The design (1) places the court clearly outside the executive, as the bill does; (2) gives fixed terms with for-cause removal on the Tax Court analogy; and (3) carries an express fallback folding the court into the judicial branch if the removal protections are invalidated. Public-rights doctrine covers immigration (*Crowell*; *Jarkesy* distinguished public rights), so the non-Article III forum itself is permissible.

**Scale.** About 3.1 million active cases pending at the end of August 2026 (TRAC); immigration judges fell from 726 (Feb 2025) to 520 IJs and 33 ACIJs (Feb 2026); OBBBA capped immigration judges at 800 while funding 10,000 ICE officers; 94% of asylum cases denied in June 2026. Counts differ by source and date (NPR, TRAC/EOIR, AIC) — the caveat travels with the figure.

**Cost.** About $0.03T, range $0.02T to $0.06T (estimate; unscored). A court of this size with appointed counsel for two populations is the only genuinely expensive item in this file. **Flagged: this needs a real score.** It pairs with ledger row IMM-1 (the immigration bill, -$0.14T), and the two should be scored together.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Cost | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CRT-5A (chosen) | Article I court plus expanded Article III review | 0.03 | estimate |
| CRT-5B | Expanded Article III habeas and de novo review only, no new court | ~0.01 | estimate |
| CRT-5C | Fold immigration adjudication into Article III entirely | Much higher; 700-1,000 Senate confirmations | estimate |

**Why A.** B is cheap and leaves adjudication in the executive. C is constitutionally cleanest and practically daunting — and it would let one president seat the entire corps. The Rebalancing review's judgment is to enact A now with B's review expansion and treat C as a long-term aspiration.

### CRT-6. Judicial records and cameras

**Design.** A Presidential Records Act-style regime for justices' working papers with a long embargo (12-20 years or after death), live audio and video of arguments, and searchable electronic disclosure reports. A prospective statute with a long embargo likely survives; *Nixon v. Administrator of General Services* (1977) is the precedent for the underlying move.

## T2 — Statutory repairs

### CRT-7. Review channeling and the Corner Post fix

**Design.** Channel pre-enforcement review of rules into one court of appeals (the D.C. Circuit) with a short filing window, on the Clean Air Act sec. 307(b) and Hobbs Act models; bar collateral attacks in enforcement proceedings; override *Corner Post* so claims accrue at publication rather than at a plaintiff's injury; state the channeling expressly so *Axon* carve-outs for structural challenges hold rather than swallowing the rule.

**Why top-tier.** This is the Rebalancing review's single strongest statutory repair: the precedent is strong (*Yakus* upheld the type), the constitutional clarity is high, and it addresses the combined effect of *Loper Bright* and *Corner Post*, which together let any rule be challenged anywhere, at any time, by a newly formed plaintiff, before a chosen judge.

**Risks.** Short windows cut off later-injured parties, raising due-process arguments; concentrating review makes the D.C. Circuit's composition a political prize. Both are real and neither is disqualifying.

### CRT-8. Answering Loper Bright

**Design.** Statute-specific express delegations drafted into every statute the agenda touches — "the Secretary shall define…" — rather than a general APA deference mandate. *Loper Bright* itself recognized that Congress may expressly delegate discretionary authority subject to constitutional limits, so this fits the Court's own stated terms.

**The general codification is held as a messaging option.** The Stop Corporate Capture Act and the 2025 EXPERTS Act (and the Thriving Economy map's "Restore Chevron" bill) are the general version. A broad "courts shall defer" statute invites a direct fight with a Court that has just held interpretation to be the judiciary's core function, and has a meaningful chance of being narrowed or struck down.

**Dependency.** Express delegations require drafting capacity that does not currently exist. This row is unenforceable without domain-capacity.md's CAP-2 and CAP-3. That is the strongest single argument in the project for funding congressional staff.

### CRT-9. Remedies after CASA

**Design.** Authorize vacatur under APA sec. 706 (the Court has left open whether "set aside" means universal vacatur), specify when universal relief is available, and route such cases to three-judge courts with expedited review.

**Why symmetrical.** *Trump v. CASA* (June 27, 2025) rests on statutory interpretation, so Congress holds the pen and the Court invited the legislation. A symmetrical rule binds both parties' administrations, which satisfies reciprocity; the House's No Rogue Rulings Act approach (restricting relief outright) does not.

### CRT-10. Penalties after Jarkesy

**Design.** Let agencies seek civil penalties in district court, or restructure penalties within the public-rights exceptions. Second wave.

### CRT-11. Docket reform

**Design.** Restore some mandatory jurisdiction (over invalidations of federal statutes, or circuit splits), add a certiorari screening division drawn from circuit judges, and limit certiorari before judgment. Precedent is strong: the 1925 Judges' Bill and the 1988 Supreme Court Case Selections Act.

**The number.** 56 signed-opinion cases in OT2024 against more than 160 a year in the 1980s (Chemerinsky via SCOTUSblog).

**Caution.** Mandatory jurisdiction can expand the Court's policy reach as easily as discipline it. This is a row where the reciprocity test cuts against the obvious version.

### CRT-12. Specialized Article III regulatory court

**Design.** A Court of Regulatory Appeals on the Federal Circuit model, with exclusive jurisdiction over rule challenges, paired with CRT-7. Article III rather than Article I, which avoids the *Northern Pipeline*, *Stern* and *Jarkesy* private-rights limits.

**Cautionary case.** The Commerce Court (1910-13) was abolished after controversy — Congress can unmake a court it dislikes, which cuts both ways on durability. Capture by repeat players is the Federal Circuit critique.

### CRT-13. Circuit realignment

**Design.** Treat circuit design as a caseload question on neutral criteria: population and filings per judgeship. The Ninth Circuit has 67.1 million people and 29 judgeships; the Fifth has 17 (CRS R48242).

**Reciprocity.** A split drawn for ideological balance invites retaliation, and splitting the Fifth to separate Texas would concentrate rather than dilute one state's influence unless paired with new judgeships. Stated criteria first, lines second.

### CRT-14. Statutory repairs (pointer)

Each repair lives in its own domain file: the VRA rewrite in domains/domain-democracy.md (DEM-1 Title III); reproductive rights in domains/domain-rights.md (RTS-1); Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act sec. 111 clarifications in domains/domain-climate.md; agency adjudication at CRT-10. Listed here so the courts domain's coverage is visible, with the common drafting requirement: each repair needs the findings record that the post-*Chevron* Court demands.

## T3 — Structural reform

### CRT-15. Judicial term limits

**Design.** 18-year terms with a vacancy every two years; prospective, applying to newly appointed justices; a severable retroactive provision so invalidation of the retroactive piece does not take the prospective scheme with it; Article III tenure and salary preserved through senior status (the 28 U.S.C. sec. 371 analogy); and **no deemed Senate consent**, which is the most vulnerable design element because the Appointments Clause requires actual advice and consent.

**The trade.** Prospective application bites after about 18 years. That is speed traded for durability, and it is the right trade under the durability rule — but it should be said out loud, because a reform whose first effect lands in 2047 is not a response to the current Court.

**Support.** 79% (Marquette, Sept 2-9, 2026). Pairs with the constitutional amendment (staging 17.2, Welch-Manchin 2024 and H.J.Res. 174), which is the durable backstop and the good-faith signal.

**Options.**

| Option | Design | Risk | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CRT-15A (chosen) | Prospective statute, severable retroactive provision, plus a proposed amendment | Uncertain but defensible | sourced |
| CRT-15B | Applied to sitting justices | Likely hostile response under "during good Behaviour" | sourced |
| CRT-15C | Amendment only | Highest clarity, lowest probability | sourced |
| CRT-15D | Khanna design (H.R. 1074: nine most junior justices hear cases, deemed consent) | Deemed consent is the weakest link | sourced |

### CRT-16. Court expansion

**Decision (October 26).** Remove the automatic expansion response to invalidation of CRT-15, following acceptance of the strategic review. Do not make Court size contingent on the Court reaching the government’s preferred legal conclusion. Retain prospective term limits, amendment development, ethics, neutral assignment and adequate judgeships. Any subsequent expansion or structural response requires a fresh democratic decision. The former deterrent rationale is historical, not a current commitment.

**Why not an opener.** It fails the reciprocity test as a first move, and the coercion objection to the contingent version is named rather than solved (Instruments #5). Congress's authority is clear — the Court's size changed seven times between 1789 and 1869 — so the risk is political, not legal: tit-for-tat expansion is easy to foresee. Marquette (Sept 2026): 53/47 overall, 35% of Republicans, against 79% for term limits and 89% for ethics. A last line, not a first.

### CRT-17. Jurisdiction-stripping restraint

**Design.** A declared norm, not a statute: the majority states that it will not strip jurisdiction over categories of constitutional claims. Executive and rhetorical, so ✔ at 51.

**Why declare it.** Stripping carries very high escalation risk, and it sends cases to state courts, which may be worse. The declaration is a reciprocity deposit that costs nothing and is a commitment to reciprocal judicial rules; CRT-16’s automatic contingency has been removed.

### CRT-18. Impeachment standard

**Design.** A declared standard: impeachment is reserved for corruption and serious misconduct, never for rulings. The Chase acquittal (1804-05) is the norm; 2025 calls to impeach district judges who ruled against the administration are the reason to restate it.

### CRT-19. State judicial selection

**Design.** End partisan judicial elections; reform state supreme court selection. State track, ✔ at 51, continuous.
