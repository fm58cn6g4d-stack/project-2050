# Budget model benchmarks: results

Role: the current result of every benchmark of the federal budget model (piece B4 of `models/tools/budget/WORKFLOW.md`, PR #104 until it merges). Status: generated by `models/tools/budget/test_benchmarks.py` on every run of `models/tools/run-all.sh`; do not edit by hand. The same results are in `benchmarks.json` for other tools. What the benchmarks mean, and the model's limits, are in `MODEL-CARD.md`.

**How to read it.** *pass*: within the stated tolerance. *fail*: outside it; the miss column gives the size. *pending*: there is no target yet, because the source isn't in the library or the model can't compute the quantity. A miss is reported, never fixed by changing the model or the tolerance.

17 benchmarks: 13 pass, 3 fail, 1 pending. 2 of the passes (PS-3a, PS-3b) are partly in-sample: their level was calibrated to the same CBO figures they are checked against, so only their timing is tested.

**Milestone status: not passed.** PS-4, SE-1, SE-2, TF-2 are not yet resolved, so B4 does not yet meet the workflow's "benchmarks before trust" condition. These results are a diagnostic report; readings that depend on the open benchmarks stay out of decision and public figures (`MODEL-CARD.md`, "What is not yet validated").

## Baseline

### BL-1. The baseline equals CBO's February 2026 projections plus the documented adjustments

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** B2's 3 reproduction tests: 3 pass
- **Target:** every cell of models/data/fiscal-baseline.csv, FY1962-2056
- **Tolerance:** exact (the file's three decimals; the unrounded values are the same floating-point numbers)
- **Source:** CBO, *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036* and *The Long-Term Budget Outlook: 2026 to 2056* (February 2026), from CBO's data files; the FY2026 actuals and the ADJ-T1 and ADJ-T2 tariff adjustments in models/data/budget/post-february.csv
- **Why:** B2's test (models/tools/budget/test_data.py, class Reproduction, run-all step 5b) rebuilds the adopted baseline from the data layer alone. This benchmark runs that test and reports it; it does not repeat it. One baseline, reproduced exactly, is the foundation every other figure stands on.

## Accounting identities

### ID-1. Deficit equals outlays minus revenue, every year and scenario

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** 3.64e-12 ($B, largest gap)
- **Target:** 0.00
- **Tolerance:** $1,000 (1e-6 billion): floating-point error only
- **Source:** Accounting definition (CBO's deficit is total outlays less total revenues)
- **Why:** The fiscal model builds the deficit from its parts in two places; they must agree to rounding error. Checked for all 10 scenarios, FY2026-2056.

### ID-2. The program's added debt grows by exactly its added deficit (spending, revenue and interest)

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** 1.93e-12 ($B, largest gap)
- **Target:** 0.00
- **Tolerance:** $1,000 (1e-6 billion)
- **Source:** Accounting definition: debt(t) = debt(t-1) + deficit(t) + other means of financing; the program has no other means of financing
- **Why:** Checked on both bases (unified and general fund) for every scenario. The program's debt is what decides the D4 guardrails, so it must not leak or gain anything outside its own deficits.

### ID-3. Debt held by the public: the residual after deficits is CBO's own other means of financing, in every scenario

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** 3.91e-11 ($B, largest gap from CBO's residual)
- **Target:** 0.00
- **Tolerance:** $1,000 (1e-6 billion)
- **Source:** CBO February 2026 ten-year and long-term projections of debt and deficits, as stored in the adopted baseline (models/data/fiscal-baseline.csv, to $1 million)
- **Why:** CBO's debt grows by more or less than its deficits because of other means of financing (mainly the cash flows of federal credit programs such as student loans). The model takes CBO's debt path, so it carries that residual. The test is that the residual is exactly CBO's, FY2027-2056, in every scenario: the tariff adjustments, the program and the interest-rate cases add none.
- The residual itself (reported, not tested): -349 billion in total over FY2027-36; largest single year 2035, -69.7 billion. FY2026 is left out because its debt is the preliminary actual (models/data/budget/post-february.csv). The model carries the gap between that actual and CBO's projection for FY2026 (+305 billion) into every later year, with interest, as a deficit, not as other financing; so CBO's FY2027 residual is measured from CBO's own FY2026 projection.

### ID-4a. Interest on the program's borrowing follows from its debt and the stated rates

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** 0.00 ($B, largest gap)
- **Target:** 0.00
- **Tolerance:** $1,000 (1e-6 billion)
- **Source:** The model's stated rate rules, CONVENTIONS.md section 10: ASM-RATE-MARGINAL, ASM-RATE-LT, ASM-RATE-HALFYEAR, ASM-RATE-SHIFT; CBO's February 2026 rates (3-month bill, 10-year note, average rate on debt)
- **Why:** Recomputed here from the written rules, independently of fiscal-model.py's code: interest = rate x last year's added debt + rate x half of this year's added deficit, at the higher of CBO's average rate and a 40/60 bill-note blend through 2036 and the baseline's implied rate after, plus one point from 2030 in the R1 cases.

### ID-4b. The baseline's net interest is consistent with its debt and CBO's average interest rate

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** 0.10 (points, largest gap (implied minus CBO))
- **Target:** 0.00
- **Tolerance:** 0.25 percentage point in every year FY2027-36
- **Source:** CBO February 2026 ten-year file: net interest, debt held by the public, average interest rate on debt
- **Why:** Net interest divided by last year's debt (the implied rate) should sit close to CBO's own average rate. They differ by definition: CBO's rate is on debt held by the public averaged over the year, while net interest also nets other interest paid and received, and last year's debt is lower than the year's average. A quarter point allows for that and would still catch a units or year-alignment error.
- Largest gap in FY2036: implied 4.04%, CBO 3.939%.

### ID-5. Nominal equals real times the deflator, and share of GDP times GDP (B2's two conversions)

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** 2.07e-16 (largest relative error)
- **Target:** 0.00
- **Tolerance:** relative error 1e-12
- **Source:** models/data/budget/deflators.csv (CBO's CPI-U and GDP price index) and the baseline's GDP; data.to_real and data.to_pct_gdp
- **Why:** Every real-dollar and share-of-GDP figure must come back to the same nominal dollars. Checked on total outlays, FY1962-2056, for both price indexes and two base years.

## Published scores

### PS-1. JCT: extending P.L. 119-21's four household deductions; the library's timing of a permanent revenue provision

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** FY2030 65.0, FY2031 67.3, FY2032 69.9, FY2033 72.8, FY2034 75.9 ($B)
- **Target:** FY2030 68, FY2031 69, FY2032 70, FY2033 71, FY2034 73
- **Tolerance:** 5% in each year
- **Source:** JCT estimates in CBO's letter of August 4, 2025 (publication 61466, SRC-1093), Table 2, from the PDF in Espen's November 8 upload (PR #121)
- **Why:** The library doesn't estimate how much a provision costs: it takes a total and spreads it over the years. So the test of the library is the spreading. The FY2030-34 total JCT gives ($351 billion) goes in; the library's method (provisions.profile_path) with the project's default spec for a revenue row (full from the start, growing with individual income tax receipts, permanent: ASM-PROF-REVENUE) puts out the yearly amounts, compared with JCT's in every year. Five percent allows for JCT's rounding to the billion and the different baseline (January 2025). Over a five-year window it can't tell growth rates apart by less than about two points a year, so the growth gap below is reported as a finding, not tested.
- Largest yearly gap: FY2030, -4.5%.
- JCT's amounts grow about 1.8% a year, FY2030-34; the default spec grows 4.0% a year, the speed of individual income tax receipts. A likely reason: the deductions are fixed dollar amounts ($25,000 of tips, $12,500 of overtime, $10,000 of car-loan interest, $6,000 for each senior), so their cost would grow more slowly than the tax base; whether the statute indexes them was not checked here. For a provision like this the default spec front-loads too little and back-loads too much.
- For comparison only (not the test): the same spec growing with CPI-U gives FY2030 67.1 and FY2034 73.4.

### PS-2. CBO: interest cost of extending P.L. 119-21's ten temporary provisions, FY2025-34

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** 80.20 ($B)
- **Target:** 71
- **Tolerance:** 15%
- **Source:** CBO's letter of August 4, 2025 (publication 61466, SRC-1093): debt service $789 billion with the extension, $718 billion without; Table 2 for the deficits, every year FY2029-34; from the PDF in Espen's November 8 upload (PR #121)
- **Why:** Tests the model's interest method (fiscal-model.py, debt_roll: market rates on new borrowing, half a year of interest on each year's new deficit) against CBO's own debt-service estimate for the same deficits. JCT's deficits go in, year by year; the model's interest comes out. Fifteen percent allows for one known difference: CBO used its January 2025 interest rates and the model uses February 2026's. (Until the PDF arrived, FY2031-33 were set equal; Table 2's own years now go in.)
- Model interest by year: FY2029 0.8, FY2030 3.8, FY2031 8.9, FY2032 15.2, FY2033 22.1, FY2034 29.5.
- The model is +13% from CBO. A higher figure is the cautious direction: the model's rate on new borrowing is at least the market blend.
- Table 2 gives the debt service only for the act and the extension together (by year: FY2026 14 rising to FY2034 155), so the extension's own $71 billion is CBO's two totals differenced, not a yearly series.

### PS-3a. CBO: discretionary defense outlays from budget authority (February 2026 baseline)

- **Result:** pass
- **Model:** +0.9% (largest yearly gap)
- **Target:** CBO's outlays, FY2029-2036
- **Tolerance:** 5% in each year
- **Source:** CBO February 2026 baseline by budget account (US-CBO/cbo-data spending_detail, models/data/budget/cbo-spending-by-function.csv)
- **Why:** Tests B3's spend-out method (provisions.cbo_discretionary_outlays, on B2's CBO-calibrated shares, ASM-SPENDOUT) against CBO's own conversion of budget authority into outlays: CBO's projected discretionary defense budget authority, account by account, goes in; outlays FY2029-2036 come out. Five percent in each year is about twice the fit error of B2's historical timing (2.2% of mean outlays), so it allows for the fit and would catch a wrong rate. **This is partly in-sample.** B2 set the shares' overall level (one scale factor, 1.009) so that, over these same years, they give CBO's outlays. So the total is fitted, not tested; what is tested is the year-by-year timing, which comes from OMB's history (FY1993-2019), not from CBO.
- Over FY2029-2036 the method gives -0.01% of CBO's total (fitted). Largest year FY2035: 1077 against CBO's 1068 billion.

## Sensitivity

### SE-1. Interest rates 0.1 point higher on all debt from FY2027: added net interest

- **Result:** fail. Miss: ten-year total +31%; 5 of 10 years outside 25% (largest FY2027, +131%)
- **Model:** FY2027-36 total 502; FY2027 32, FY2036 72 ($B)
- **Target:** CBO: total 383; FY2027 14, FY2036 62
- **Tolerance:** ten-year total within 15%, and each year within 25%
- **Source:** CBO, How Changes in Economic Conditions Might Affect the Federal Budget: 2026 to 2036 (publication 62257, April 2026), Table 3, on the February 2026 baseline; Espen's November 8 upload (PR #121)
- **Why:** The model's stress cases (DEC-R1, BASE-R1) raise every rate by one point from FY2030. Here the same method, reproduced exactly (it gives BASE-R1 to the dollar), is run on CBO's own scenario: every rate 0.1 point higher each year, on CBO's baseline, from FY2027 (the model's first year it can shock). That compares like with like, with no scaling. CBO says its rules of thumb are roughly symmetrical and scalable up to one point. The total gets 15% for CBO's rounding and the model's simpler rate rule; each year gets 25%, because the stress case is read year by year (the guardrails in 2035, 2045 and 2056), so timing matters as well as the total. Revenue effects (Federal Reserve remittances, under $5 billion a year in CBO's table) are left out of both sides.
- Model by year: FY2027 32, FY2028 36, FY2029 39, FY2030 43, FY2031 47, FY2032 51, FY2033 56, FY2034 61, FY2035 66, FY2036 72.
- CBO by year: FY2027 14, FY2028 20, FY2029 25, FY2030 31, FY2031 36, FY2032 41, FY2033 46, FY2034 51, FY2035 57, FY2036 62.
- Why they differ: the model applies the higher rate to all of last year's debt at once (fiscal-model.py, baseline()), so its first-year effect is 0.1% of about $32.4 trillion. CBO phases the higher rate in as maturing debt is refinanced, so its effect starts lower and grows as the debt turns over. The model is higher in every year, by most at the start, which is the cautious direction. Whether to phase rates in is a method choice for B6, not a change made here.
- In the model's own +1 point case (BASE-R1), the same method adds $387 billion in FY2030, the first year, and $6211 billion over FY2030-39.
- Cross-check: CBO's 2025 workbook (61183, January 2025 baseline) gives +13.34 billion in FY2026 for the same scenario. Those are the figures in Espen's old CBO scorer (SRC-0203), so that table came from it.

### SE-2. Slower growth (productivity or labor force 0.1 point a year slower): added deficits

- **Result:** fail. Miss: -100% for both: 0 against 317 and 166
- **Model:** 0 for both (the model has no growth response) ($B)
- **Target:** CBO, FY2027-36: productivity 317, labor force 166
- **Tolerance:** ten-year total within 15% for each scenario
- **Source:** CBO, How Changes in Economic Conditions Might Affect the Federal Budget: 2026 to 2036 (publication 62257, April 2026), Table 2; Espen's November 8 upload (PR #121)
- **Why:** The model takes CBO's baseline revenue and spending as given in dollars. It has no input for slower growth, so its added deficit is zero where CBO's is $317 billion (productivity) and $166 billion (labor force) over FY2027-36. That is a missing capability, not a calibration error: building a growth response is for B6 or later, calibrated to these tables. Fifteen percent is the same allowance as SE-1's total.
- Program rows indexed to GDP would also shrink with slower growth; the model has no way to feed that in either.
