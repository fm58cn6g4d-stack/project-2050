A day in 2050

Picture an ordinary town on an ordinary day in 2050. Nobody in it is rich. Most people work, some are raising children, some are caring for a parent, a few have just lost a job. What is different is that the floor under them holds.

Every scene below is imagined: the people are composites, not real people, and the town is not a real place. But each scene shows one thing that would be different because of a law Project 2050 would pass, starting in January 2029, and each one says when that law begins, what it costs, and where it still falls short. Open any scene to see the details, the hard questions and the evidence behind every figure.

  • An imagined scene. The people and the town are composites. Nothing here is a forecast.
  • Our analysis, not an official score. Costs and effects are the project's own estimates on its own models, marked "on our estimates" or "about". No official scorekeeper has scored the plan.
  • Dated promises. Each scene shows only what its law has started by 2050, and says when each part begins.
  • Every figure traced. A small number after a sentence leads to the passage in the project's records that the figure comes from.
  • Laws that need votes. Most of these laws need a supermajority in the Senate or a change to its rules; each scene shows what happens if its law passes.

6:30 am · A kitchen · A secure floor

The money comes on the first

The phone buzzes before the coffee is ready: the child benefit is in, as it is on the first of every month. Tasha never applied for it. It started from her children's birth records, and it comes whether her hours were good this month or not.

Next door, Ana is home with her new baby on paid leave, at most of her pay, and her job is waiting for her when she goes back.

What made the difference

What changed

  • A monthly child benefit for working and middle-class families: $300 a month for each child from 6 to 17 and $360 for each child under 6, in 2025 dollars and rising with prices.1 Every family below about $150,000 for couples ($112,500 for single parents), adjusted for inflation, gets the full amount, however little the parents earn.2
  • It starts from records the government already holds, so families do not have to apply. A parent confirms a letter instead of filling out a form.
  • Twelve weeks of paid leave for a new child, a family member's serious illness or your own, run by Social Security, paying 85% of the first part of your wages, with your job back if you have been on it at least 90 days, once the 2030 Social Security act passes.3,4,5
  • Workers without children get a real earned income credit, and food help cut by the 2025 law is restored.6 A Social Security act promises full benefits on time, with a stronger minimum benefit and credit for caregiving years.

When it begins

  • The child benefit and the workers' credit from 2030.7
  • Paid leave from 2031; the contributions that pay for it start in 2030.4

What it costs

  • On our estimates the child benefit costs about $1.85 trillion over 2030-39, and the whole floor about $298 billion in 2040 on the general fund, before the plan's financing.8
  • On our estimates a worker earning $60,000 pays $180 a year toward paid leave to start.9

The honest limit

  • On our estimates child poverty falls to about 9% (somewhere between 8% and 12%), from about 13.5-14.5% under the law as it will stand in 2029. That is a large cut, not an end to child poverty.10
  • Couples above about $210,000 get somewhat less than the credit they would otherwise get.11

Hard questions

Won't people stop working?

Studies of the monthly payments families got in 2021 found no effect on employment. Even the economists who expect some parents to leave work find child poverty falls substantially.12

Why do higher earners get less?

Because it is a benefit for working and middle-class families. The full amount goes to every family below about $150,000 for couples, and it falls gradually above that.2

7:45 am · A child care center · Care: child care and pre-K

Drop-off, and nothing to pay

Mia runs in ahead of her mother. Tasha pays nothing: her family's income is below 85% of the state's median.1

Her teacher, Maria, once earned what a fast-food job pays. Now she is paid like the teachers at the elementary school down the street.2

What made the difference

What changed

  • Child care is free for families at or below 85% of their state's median income, and no family pays more than 7% of its income for a child too young for school, with no income limit.1,3
  • Pre-K is free for every 3- and 4-year-old, in schools, Head Start, centers and family child care alike.4
  • Child care and pre-K staff earn at least $20 an hour in 2026 dollars, rising with wages, and early educators reach pay parity with elementary teachers.5,2

When it begins

  • Free care at or below 85% of median income, and free pre-K for 4-year-olds, from the 2030-31 school year; the 7% cap from July 2032; free pre-K for 3-year-olds by 2033-34.6
  • The $20 floor for child care staff from the 2030-31 school year, and pay parity by the 2036-37 school year.5,2

What it costs

  • Child care, pre-K, long-term care and the care workforce together cost about $750 billion in 2040 on our estimates, about 1.4% of the economy, as priced before the financing settlement moved more of long-term care onto the states. Most of it replaces what families now pay themselves or give in unpaid care.7

The honest limit

  • Free care reaches about half of families with young children, on our estimates; everyone else pays up to 7% of income.8,3
  • School-age care is not made free: children 5 to 12 keep today's child care block grant.9

Hard questions

Why not free child care for everyone?

Free for every family would cost more and would mostly help families who can already pay. Capping everyone else at 7% of income means no family faces a cliff.3

Why should parents who aren't working get child care?

Most families with young children already have every parent working. A work test mostly catches parents who are looking for work or in school, and it adds paperwork every time a job changes.

9:00 am · A clinic · Care: health

No bill at the desk

Rosa, who waits tables, sees a doctor about a cough that won't quit. She shows her plan card, and there is no bill at the desk. She was enrolled automatically from her tax return, and she pays no premium and no deductible.

Down the hall, Jay gets his school checkup. No charge for that either: primary care and prevention are free for everyone.

What made the difference

What changed

  • One public health plan for everyone who lives here with legal or registered status: hospital, doctor, prescriptions, mental health and addiction care, maternity, reproductive care including abortion, dental, vision and hearing. No premiums, no deductibles, no networks, and enrollment happens automatically.
  • No charge at all for primary care, prevention, maternity, mental health or chronic-condition drugs, and none for anyone under twice the poverty line. Above that, small copays for other care, capped at about $1,000 a year on our working figure.1
  • Hospitals are paid a fair rate, on a five-year glide to about 1.15 times Medicare's, where employer plans paid 254% of Medicare in 2022.2

When it begins

  • From 2029 the 2025 law's Medicaid cuts are undone. From January 1, 2031, everyone uninsured, everyone who buys their own insurance and every child is enrolled, and people in traditional Medicare get a $5,000 yearly cap on what they pay.3
  • People on Medicaid and with job-based coverage move in over 2033 to 2035, and everyone is in by January 1, 2035.3

What it costs

  • On our estimates the country spends about $491 billion a year less on health care at full scale, while households stop paying about $1.55 trillion a year in premiums and out-of-pocket costs.4
  • Premiums give way to a health contribution on income: in the plan as it stands, 3.80% from 2035, with nothing owed at or below 138% of poverty, rising to the full rate at 250%.5
  • The federal budget's cost rises by far more than the country saves, because the government now pays what premiums used to. The health contribution and the taxes in the money scene, at the end of the day, pay for it.

The honest limit

  • People with no immigration status get emergency care, not the plan, so we don't say every person in the country is covered.
  • Taxes go up for most people, even as premiums and deductibles go away.

Hard questions

Will my taxes go up?

Yes, for most people. Premiums, deductibles and most copays go away, and a contribution on income takes their place, with nothing owed at or below 138% of poverty.5 Across the whole plan, on our estimates, every fifth of households below the top comes out ahead on average.6 The money scene at the end of the day shows how.

Will I wait longer for care?

Waits may grow at first, as the Congressional Budget Office expects for any single-payer plan. The plan starts with smaller groups, keeps a small copay where care is scarcest, pays hospitals above Medicare's rates, and pays doctors at Medicare's fee schedule with extra for primary care and rural practice.

10:30 am · A living room · Care: long-term care

Help at home, savings intact

An aide arrives to help Ruth through her morning. Her son Sam is at work. Nobody asked Ruth to spend her savings down first: long-term care is part of the public health plan, with no asset test.

What made the difference

What changed

  • Help at home and the care part of nursing-home stays are part of the single public health plan, for anyone of any age who needs help with three daily activities or has a serious cognitive impairment, with no asset test and no estate recovery.1
  • The top tier of help at home is funded at the level of the care part of a nursing-home stay, so a person who needs the most help can get it at home.
  • Home care and nursing home workers paid with public money earn at least $20 an hour in 2026 dollars, rising with wages.2

When it begins

  • A five-year bridge from 2030: more money for home care, with waiting lists reported; estate recovery ends, and Medicaid's savings limit rises to $10,000 for a single person.3
  • The $20 floor for home care and nursing home workers from January 2030.2
  • The benefit from January 2035, with new users phased in over its first two years, the heaviest needs first.1

What it costs

  • Part of the care package's cost of about $750 billion in 2040, on our estimates.4 The states keep paying what they spend on long-term care today.5

The honest limit

  • Whether a family member can be paid to give care depends on the rules in their state today, until a national study reports.6
  • It is not free long-term care: there are capped copays above twice the poverty line, and nursing-home residents pay a capped share of the care and room and board from their income.

Hard questions

Will my parent lose their home to pay for a nursing home?

No. The plan pays for most of the care, with no asset test. Residents pay a capped share, and room and board, from their income, keep a protected allowance, and a spouse at home keeps the income they need.

Won't care workers be hard to find?

Yes, and it is the hardest part. Pay comes first, because it has the best evidence for keeping workers, and new users of help at home are phased in, the heaviest needs first.

12:00 pm · A warehouse break room · Work

A raise that keeps up

On her break Tasha reads her pay stub. The wage floor went up again this January, as it does every year with the median wage, so a raise never has to wait for Congress again.

Her coworkers voted in their union by signing cards, and their first contract came on a deadline instead of after years of delay.

Across the street, Luis took a job paying $24 an hour after his plant closed, against his old $32. Wage insurance pays him $4 for every hour he works, for two years.1

What made the difference

What changed

  • The federal minimum wage rises in yearly steps to $17.00 an hour, then rises each year with the median wage, and never falls.2
  • A union is certified when a majority of workers sign cards, and can still choose an election, held within 20 days. Bargaining starts within 10 days, with mediation and then binding arbitration on a timetable.3
  • Penalties of up to $50,000 per violation, sought in federal court, and workers can sue on their own if the labor board does not act.4
  • Wage insurance: a worker who loses a job held three years and takes a lower-paid one gets half the hourly drop for each hour worked, up to $10,000 a year, for two years, unless they were fired for misconduct or quit without good cause.1

When it begins

  • $9.50 on January 1, 2030, then yearly steps to $17.00 on January 1, 2035, then with the median wage.2
  • Card check and first-contract deadlines when the labor act is signed, about mid-2029 on the plan's timeline.5
  • Wage insurance and stronger unemployment insurance in every state from January 1, 2031.6

What it costs

  • About $0.22 trillion over 2030-39 and about $31 billion a year from 2040 on the federal budget, on our estimates, assuming no recession.7 Bigger unemployment benefits are paid by employers' state taxes, which roughly double.

The honest limit

  • The rules ride the labor act, the hardest vote in the plan: it needs 60 Senate votes or a change to the Senate's rules, and if it is signed after June 30, 2029, every wage step moves later.5
  • One group keeps less of a raise for a while: on our estimates a single adult buying marketplace coverage in a state that has not expanded Medicaid keeps about 39% of a raise to $12.50 in 2032, against 59% under 2029 law, and 52-59% from 2035. The fix is still open.8

Hard questions

Won't a higher minimum wage cost jobs?

It may cost some. The Congressional Budget Office put $15 by 2025 at 1.3 million to 1.4 million fewer jobs; other careful studies find little or no loss in the range states have tried.9 The floor reaches $17 in 2035, when on our estimates it is about half the national median wage, in yearly steps, with a pause if unemployment is rising.10

Doesn't card check end the secret ballot?

No. A union can still choose an election, held within 20 days, and mandatory anti-union meetings end.3

2:00 pm · An apartment · Housing

A lease of their own

Carmen and her two children spent a year on Tasha's couch after an eviction. Now they have their own lease: a rent award, with someone to help them search, and rent they can pay.

Upstairs, Denise, a home health aide whose hours were cut, found a notice on her door last month. The emergency fund paid her back rent after checking that the landlord really owns the building, and she got legal help to answer the notice, one of the things the fund can pay for.

What made the difference

What changed

  • A million new rent awards from 2030 to 2033, paid as permanent spending so they never lapse in a budget fight, first for people with no home of their own: people who are homeless, families doubled up with others, and people leaving prison, a hospital or foster care with nowhere to go.1
  • A standing emergency fund for back rent, utilities, a deposit or moving costs, up to four months of the area's rent in a year, with legal help besides.2
  • Housing First: a home without first proving sobriety or entering treatment, with services offered.
  • A program for the poorest families with children, enrolling up to 300,000 families a year within a capped budget, with a right for the whole group named in law as a goal for the 2040s.3
  • Homes built and repaired that stay affordable for good, repairs for low-income owners, and property-tax help for older owners in states that join.

When it begins

  • Rent awards from 2030, 250,000 a year through 2033.1
  • The emergency fund from 2030, with $15 billion in its first two years.2
  • The families' program from 2034, and a review in 2041 of making it a right.3

What it costs

  • About $0.81 trillion over 2030-39 and about $110 billion in 2040 as first priced, on our estimates. Capping the families' program in the financing settlement lowers the 2040 figure by about $18.6 billion.4

The honest limit

  • A single adult who pays most of his income in rent, but is not homeless, not disabled and not raising children, has no funded route to rent help beyond the emergency fund in a crisis.5
  • Vouchers help only if families can use them. The plan assumes 90% of awards lead to a lease; the closest recent program reached 67% after about two years. That is the plan's weakest number.6
  • The program for the poorest families is capped: families compete by need, not by right, until a later Congress votes to make it a right.3

Hard questions

Does this end homelessness?

No one should promise that. For a family that gets a voucher, the effect is large and well tested. How far the national count falls is unknown, because people keep losing homes.

Will landlords take the vouchers?

Housing agencies are paid their full fees for the new awards so they can recruit landlords, search help comes with every award, and a national rule would make it illegal to turn someone away just for paying with a voucher. That rule needs a regular bill to pass.

4:00 pm · A front porch as a storm comes in · Clean power

Storm season, dividend day

Linda and Ray watch the storm roll in from their porch. Their Climate Dividends arrive with their Social Security, four times a year, and there has never been a carbon charge on their gas bill.

Sam is out in the storm with his line crew. When the coal plant he worked at closed, he trained as a lineworker at 90% of his old pay, and the county got its lost tax revenue back, a little less each year, while it rebuilt.1

What made the difference

What changed

  • Power plants and large factories pay for the carbon they put in the air, and most of the money comes back to every adult as a Climate Dividend, paid four times a year, tax-free, with the first payment going out before the price begins. There is no carbon charge on your gas bill or at the pump.2
  • Clean power and the grid built faster: a clean electricity standard of 80% by 2035 and 100% by 2040, and a public builder for needed lines no one else will build.3
  • Help with energy bills that comes to you: a credit for households already getting SNAP, SSI or energy help, with no new application.
  • Money for every state and tribe to prepare for disasters, and a federal backstop for insurers in a catastrophic year.
  • A guarantee for workers when a mine, power plant or refinery closes: up to 90% of their old pay for two years, in a new job or in training, and lost local tax revenue replaced in full at first, then a little less each year for ten years.1

When it begins

  • The carbon price from 2030, at $25 a ton, reaching $150 by 2037.2
  • The bill credit from the winter of 2030-31.4
  • The worker guarantee for closures from 2030.1

What it costs

  • About $1.73 trillion over 2030-39 on our estimates, a little below what the plan first set aside for these purposes, and about $84 billion in 2040.5
  • On our estimates the dividend is about $405 an adult in 2035, in that year's dollars; it moves with what polluters pay, and shrinks as pollution falls.6

The honest limit

  • Electricity costs somewhat more while the clean grid is built: on our estimates the average price is about a tenth higher than under current law by 2040. On the carbon price and the dividend alone, on our estimates, the lowest-income two-fifths of households come out ahead on average, nationally, though some in states that burn the most coal may be behind.7
  • The clean electricity standard is a target. On our estimates the grid reaches about 73% clean power in 2035 and 87% in 2040, held back by connections to the grid, and suppliers that fall short pay a capped fee.8
  • It does not reach the 2035 climate goal, a 61-66% cut from 2005, on its own: on our estimates carbon emissions from energy fall about 41% below 2005 by 2035.9
  • The worker guarantee covers closures, not every oil and gas job.

Hard questions

Isn't this a carbon tax?

It is a price on the biggest polluters: power plants and large industrial facilities. Households pay no charge of their own, and most of the money goes back to every adult, with the first payment made before the price begins.

Didn't Canada try this and drop it?

Canada dropped its charge on consumers and kept the price on large emitters. This plan is the part Canada kept.

5:30 pm · The courthouse steps · Immigration

Earned, not given

Tomas, a cook who has lived here for decades, stands on the courthouse steps with his citizenship papers. He earned his green card after a fingerprint check and a criminal check, proving his years here from tax records the government already held.

Sam's wife, Elena, takes the photo. She came later. She registered once sign-ups opened, worked legally on her filing receipt, and got her green card when she reached ten years here.

Inside, a family that crossed the border in last spring's surge hears the decision on their asylum claim, a few months after they filed, not years.

What made the difference

What changed

  • Earned legal status: people who have lived here ten years or more get a green card after fingerprints and a criminal check, and so do Dreamers and people who have held temporary protection. Everyone else here on the day the law is signed can register, work legally and be protected from deportation while they reach ten years.1
  • Legal lines fixed: more family and employment green cards, and spouses and children of permanent residents no longer wait in line.
  • Trained asylum officers decide claims within 180 days of filing, and in about 90 days in a surge, with more officers instead of a ban on asking for protection.2
  • ICE is replaced, enforcement is focused by law on threats to security, serious crime and recent unlawful entry, and no more than 30,000 people are held on average, outside a declared surge.3

When it begins

  • Filing for legal status from late 2030.4
  • Workplace checks with E-Verify no earlier than October of the year after the law passes, and only once an independent inspector confirms legal status is up and running.5

What it costs

  • About $0.15 trillion over 2030-39 and about $22.5 billion in 2040, on our estimates.6

The honest limit

  • Surges will still happen. The plan meets them with more officers and a faster track, not a promise that they stop.
  • Not everyone who qualifies will apply; our estimates assume about 70% do.7

Hard questions

Isn't this amnesty?

It is earned status, by two routes. Dreamers, people who have held temporary protection, DACA or parole, and farm, care and construction workers with a work history get a green card on approval, after fingerprints and a criminal check.1 Everyone else here earns it after ten years' residence, under the registry already in immigration law, whose date has been stuck at 1972 since 1986.8

Will my information be used to deport my family?

No. The law bars using registration or program records for immigration enforcement, with penalties, except under a criminal warrant for a named person.

7:00 pm · The public library · Democratic renewal

A meeting that anyone can join

Carmen's daughter Leah is at her first town meeting. She is newly old enough to vote, and she is already registered: the state did it from the records it holds and mailed her a notice.

The candidate speaking tonight raised her money from small gifts, matched six to one: a $25 gift brought her campaign $175.1

Earlier, Tasha called Social Security about her mother's benefits, and someone picked up. The office is staffed again.

What made the difference

What changed

  • Registration done by the state from records it already holds, with a mailed notice and a way to decline; any current government, tribal, student or benefit card as identification, or a signed statement; mail voting without an excuse and at least two weeks of early voting.
  • Preclearance returns for places with a record of recent violations, and until the next census redistricting no state may redraw its congressional map in the middle of a decade.
  • Small in-state gifts up to $200 matched six to one for House and Senate candidates who opt in.1
  • No individual stocks for members of Congress and top officials, without a forced sale; a binding code for the Justices and eighteen-year terms for future Justices, when the politics allow.
  • The agencies every other promise runs through are rebuilt, about 54,000 added staff across six of them, with money released as each agency hires.2

When it begins

  • The voting floor from the first federal general election at least 180 days after the democracy act: November 5, 2030, if it is law by May 9, 2030.3
  • Matched small gifts from the 2032 elections on the plan's working timeline.1
  • The agency rebuild in the first budget act of 2029.2

What it costs

  • About $503 billion over 2030-39 and $51.6 billion in 2040, on our estimates, about three-quarters of it the agency rebuild.4

The honest limit

  • Most of this needs 60 Senate votes or a change to the Senate's rules.5
  • Some of it may be struck down in court. Each risky part is written to fall alone, so the rest stands.
  • No turnout gain is promised: each rule's effect comes from separate studies, and rules interact.

Hard questions

Isn't the stock ban a forced sale?

No. A covered official complies by holding diversified funds, by a qualified blind trust, or by selling, with no immediate tax on a sale made to comply.

Why rebuild the agencies first?

Because every other promise runs through them. A child benefit, a health plan or a rent award is only as good as the office that delivers it, so the money is released as each agency hires against a published path.

10:00 pm · A night bus · Transportation Optional scene

The route that survived

Carmen rides the bus into the city for her night shift at the hospital. The route survived because the city kept its own funding to qualify for help with running costs, and the bridge on the way was repaired, on a list of bridges the state publishes.

What made the difference

What changed

  • Repair first, with states publishing which roads and bridges are falling behind: a program to rebuild the Interstate and its bridges, help to keep transit running in big cities, and passenger rail paid out only as projects prove they are ready.
  • The gas tax rises with prices, plus ten small steps, then five more over 2040-44, each pausing when gas prices spike.1 Electric cars pay by the mile, at what an efficient gas car pays, read from the odometer, with no tracking device.
  • The heaviest trucks pay by weight and miles, instead of a flat $550 that hasn't changed since 1984.2

When it begins

  • The new fuel and mileage charges start first; the 2031 transportation law takes effect on October 1, 2031, with its outright ban on collecting location data.3

What it costs

  • For a typical gas car, federal fuel tax goes from $88 a year today to $225 in 2036, on our estimates; an electric car pays $141.4

The honest limit

  • Our figures assume people don't drive less when fuel taxes rise, so the revenue is an upper bound.
  • The plan narrows the Interstate's repair gap; it does not close it.

Hard questions

Will the government track my car?

No. The charge reads only the odometer, the readings are private tax information, and the law bans collecting location data outright and deletes readings after three years.3

Night · The end of the day · Fair financing

The money, told plainly

The lights go out across town. Here is what all of it costs, who pays, and who comes out ahead.

What made the difference

What changed

  • The top end and corporations pay first: the corporate rate rises to 28%, the top income tax rate returns to 39.6% for the highest earners, a new 45% bracket applies to incomes in the millions, and the 2025 law's tax cuts are reversed at the top.1
  • On our estimates the top 1% and corporations bear 65% of the plan's new general revenue over 2030-39, leaving out the health contribution that replaces premiums.2
  • Premiums give way to a health contribution on income: 3.80% from 2035 in the plan as it stands, with nothing owed at or below 138% of poverty, rising to the full rate at 250%. A separate payroll contribution of 1.65 points funds the education fund.3,4
  • Spending gives way too, across the whole federal budget: the plan drops its own $300 adult credit, the states keep paying their full current share of long-term care, defense is held flat after inflation after 2036 and restructured, crop insurance is reformed for the insurers and the largest farms, and a tax break for real-estate investors ends.5
  • From 2040 to 2056, on our estimates, the plan's new spending is covered every year by its taxes, its savings elsewhere in the budget and the states' payments, by a thin margin at first. That is not the same as a balanced federal budget.6
  • The plan would repeal the debt limit in 2029, so it no longer stands in the way of paying bills Congress has already run up.7

What it costs

  • Who comes out ahead: on our estimates every fifth of households below the top comes out ahead on average, over 2030-39 and in each of the years we checked. These are averages: many households within each fifth differ.8
  • The financing settlement doesn't touch Social Security, which has its own act and its own financing.9

The honest limit

  • On our estimates the margin is thin until the mid-2040s, and the plan's first decade runs short, most in its later years as programs reach full scale.10
  • If health spending runs above its path, or the wealth tax is struck down, a backstop written into law raises the health contribution a quarter point at a time to cover it.11
  • Some of the money rests on working assumptions about how much the new taxes raise; if they raise less, a later Congress has to find the difference.
  • These are our analysis on the project's own models, not official scores.

To come: the path of the national debt under the plan, once it is confirmed.

Take-home pay

To come: how many workers take home more or less once the health contribution replaces premiums.

Later revenue

To come: whether and how the plan raises more revenue in later years, and how the health contribution moves.

Hard questions

Is it paid for?

On our central estimates, yes, from 2040 on: every year the plan's new spending is covered by its own taxes, savings and state payments, with the top end and corporations paying first.6 But until the mid-2040s the margin is thin enough that any one of the plan's risks could erase it, and its first decade runs short.10

Who pays?

Corporations and the highest earners first. Then a health contribution on income, which replaces premiums and deductibles and exempts people at or below 138% of poverty.3 Then savings across the rest of the federal budget, defense included.

What it is for

  • Freedom you can use. A right matters more when you have the time, security and support to exercise it: to care for someone, change jobs, learn, make a home and take part in your community.
  • A society that cares for its people. Children should grow up without poverty. Illness, disability and aging should bring dependable support, and the people who give care deserve the pay and respect that make good care possible.
  • Power that answers to people. Voting should be easy and secure, money should not drown out citizens, and the officials who serve the public should not profit from what they know.
  • Responsibility across generations. Pay honestly for what we promise, say who pays, and leave the next generation a cleaner country and a government that works.

How it happens

  • Build a shared purpose. Start with the changes people want in their own lives, and organize around a program people can understand and help shape.
  • Elect a coalition for change. Turn that purpose into the power to govern, and press every candidate to say how they will deliver it.
  • Make the promises real. Pass the laws, staff the agencies and publish the counts, so people can see whether each promise is kept.
  • Keep showing up, and keep asking. Check the results, say what isn't working and change it. A plan that can't be questioned can't be trusted.
