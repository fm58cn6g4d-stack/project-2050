Maya and Sam are raising two young children, with Sam's parents a few blocks away. Carol and Dev are getting ready to retire, and their grown son Eli lives with them. 6:45 am · A job site at the edge of town · Work

A trade, and a contract

Jordan is in the second year of a registered apprenticeship, wiring a new battery building for the power grid. His sponsor is paid for each apprentice it starts and each one who finishes, and part of that money bought his tools and his bus pass.

His crew voted in their union by signing cards, and their first contract came on a deadline instead of after years of delay.

What made the difference

What changed

  • The federal minimum wage rises in yearly steps to $17.00 an hour, then rises each year with the median wage, and never falls.1
  • A union is certified when a majority of workers sign cards, and can still choose an election, held within 20 days. Bargaining starts within 10 days, with mediation and then binding arbitration on a timetable.2
  • Penalties of up to $50,000 per violation, sought in federal court, and workers can sue on their own if the labor board does not act.3
  • Wage insurance: a worker who loses a job held three years and takes a lower-paid one gets half the hourly drop for each hour worked, up to $10,000 a year, for two years, unless they were fired for misconduct or quit without good cause.4
  • One apprenticeship line built with employers in construction, energy, care and advanced manufacturing: sponsors are paid for each apprentice they start and each one who finishes, and part of each grant pays for apprentices' tools, transport, child care and mentors, toward a target of 600,000 new apprentices a year by 2034.5

When it begins

  • $9.50 on January 1, 2030, then yearly steps to $17.00 on January 1, 2035, then with the median wage.1
  • Card check and first-contract deadlines when the labor act is signed, about mid-2029 on the plan's timeline.6
  • Wage insurance and stronger unemployment insurance in every state from January 1, 2031.7

What it costs

  • About $0.22 trillion over 2030-39 and about $31 billion a year from 2040 on the federal budget, on our estimates, assuming no recession.8 Bigger unemployment benefits are paid by employers' state taxes, which roughly double.

The honest limit

  • The rules ride the labor act, the hardest vote in the plan: it needs 60 Senate votes or a change to the Senate's rules, and if it is signed after June 30, 2029, every wage step moves later.6
  • One group keeps less of a raise for a while: on our estimates a single adult buying marketplace coverage in a state that has not expanded Medicaid keeps about 39% of a raise to $12.50 in 2032, against 59% under 2029 law, and 52-59% from 2035. The fix is still open.9
  • The apprenticeship target is a goal, not a forecast: nothing in the evidence yet shows 600,000 a year can be reached, and the count is published each year so everyone can see.5

Hard questions

Won't a higher minimum wage cost jobs?

It may cost some. The Congressional Budget Office put $15 by 2025 at 1.3 million to 1.4 million fewer jobs; other careful studies find little or no loss in the range states have tried.10 The floor reaches $17 in 2035, when on our estimates it is about half the national median wage, in yearly steps, with a pause if unemployment is rising.11

Doesn't card check end the secret ballot?

No. A union can still choose an election, held within 20 days, and mandatory anti-union meetings end.2

12:30 pm · An urgent care clinic · Care: health

Stitches, and a small copay

Jordan slices his hand on a panel edge and gets three stitches at urgent care. He has been in the public plan since he was a kid and has never seen a premium or a deductible. Because he earns more than twice the poverty line, he pays a small copay, capped for the year.

What made the difference

What changed

  • One public health plan for everyone who lives here with legal or registered status: hospital, doctor, prescriptions, mental health and addiction treatment, maternity, reproductive care including abortion, dental, vision and hearing. No premiums, no deductibles, no networks, and enrollment happens automatically.1
  • No charge at all for primary care, prevention, maternity, mental health or chronic-condition drugs, and none for anyone under twice the poverty line. Above that, small copays for other care, capped at about $1,000 a year on our working figure.2
  • Hospitals are paid a fair rate, on a five-year glide to about 1.15 times Medicare's, where employer plans paid 254% of Medicare in 2022.3
  • Today's Medicare moves into the plan: its separate parts, Medicare Advantage and Medigap end for covered care on January 1, 2035, and so do Medicare's premiums.4

When it begins

  • From 2029 the 2025 law's Medicaid cuts are undone. From January 1, 2031, everyone uninsured, everyone who buys their own insurance and every child is enrolled, and people in traditional Medicare get a $5,000 yearly cap on what they pay.5
  • People on Medicaid and with job-based coverage move in over 2033 to 2035, and everyone is in by January 1, 2035.5

What it costs

  • On our estimates the country spends about $491 billion a year less on health care at full scale, while households stop paying about $1.55 trillion a year in premiums and out-of-pocket costs.6
  • Premiums give way to a health contribution on income: 3.80% from 2035, with nothing owed at or below 138% of poverty, rising to the full rate at 250%.7 In later years the rate can move a quarter point at a time under the health fund's own rule (pending). It goes into the health fund and can pay only for health care, like the Medicare payroll tax it replaces.8
  • The federal budget's cost rises by far more than the country saves, because the government now pays what premiums used to. The health contribution pays part of that, and the general fund pays the rest, from the taxes in the money scene at the end of the day.8

The honest limit

  • People with no immigration status get emergency care, not the plan, so we don't say every person in the country is covered.
  • Taxes go up for most people, even as premiums and deductibles go away.

Hard questions

Will my taxes go up?

Yes, for most people. Premiums, deductibles and most copays go away, and a contribution on income takes their place, with nothing owed at or below 138% of poverty.7 Across the whole plan, on our estimates, every fifth of households below the top comes out ahead on average.9 The money scene at the end of the day shows how.

Will I wait longer for care?

Waits may grow at first, as the Congressional Budget Office expects for any single-payer plan. The plan starts with smaller groups, keeps a small copay where care is scarcest, pays hospitals above Medicare's rates, and pays doctors at Medicare's fee schedule with extra for primary care and rural practice.

4:00 pm · The job site, as a storm comes in · Clean power

Storm call

The crew ties down before the storm. Jordan's Climate Dividend landed last week, as it does four times a year: power plants and big factories pay for the carbon they put in the air, and most of that money comes back to every adult.

What made the difference

What changed

  • Power plants and large factories pay for the carbon they put in the air, and most of the money comes back to every adult as a Climate Dividend, paid four times a year, tax-free, with the first payment going out before the price begins. There is no carbon charge on your gas bill or at the pump.1
  • Clean power and the grid built faster: a clean electricity standard of 80% by 2035 and 100% by 2040, and a public builder for needed lines no one else will build.2
  • Help with energy bills that comes to you: a credit for households already getting SNAP, SSI or energy help, with no new application.
  • Money for every state and tribe to prepare for disasters, and a federal backstop for insurers in a catastrophic year.
  • A guarantee for workers when a mine, power plant or refinery closes: up to 90% of their old pay for two years, in a new job or in training, and lost local tax revenue replaced in full at first, then a little less each year for ten years.3

When it begins

  • The carbon price from 2030, at $25 a ton, reaching $150 by 2037.1
  • The bill credit from the winter of 2030-31.4
  • The worker guarantee for closures from 2030.3

What it costs

  • About $1.73 trillion over 2030-39 on our estimates, a little below what the plan first set aside for these purposes, and less after that, as the clean-energy credits step down under the law to nothing by 2047.5,6
  • On our estimates the dividend is about $405 an adult in 2035, in that year's dollars; it moves with what polluters pay, and shrinks as pollution falls.7

The honest limit

  • Electricity costs somewhat more while the clean grid is built: on our estimates the average price is about a tenth higher than under current law by 2040. On the carbon price and the dividend alone, on our estimates, the lowest-income two-fifths of households come out ahead on average, nationally, though some in states that burn the most coal may be behind.8
  • The clean electricity standard is a target. On our estimates the grid reaches about 73% clean power in 2035 and 87% in 2040, held back by connections to the grid, and suppliers that fall short pay a capped fee.9
  • It does not reach the 2035 climate goal, a 61-66% cut from 2005, on its own: on our estimates carbon emissions from energy fall about 41% below 2005 by 2035.10
  • The worker guarantee covers closures, not every oil and gas job.

Hard questions

Isn't this a carbon tax?

It is a price on the biggest polluters: power plants and large industrial facilities. Households pay no charge of their own, and most of the money goes back to every adult, with the first payment made before the price begins.

Didn't Canada try this and drop it?

Canada dropped its charge on consumers and kept the price on large emitters. This plan is the part Canada kept.

5:30 pm · The federal building · Immigration

Dani's oath

Jordan's roommate Dani was brought here as a child. When the law passed she got her green card on approval, after fingerprints and a criminal check. Today she takes the oath as a citizen, and Jordan came straight from work to take the picture.

Down the hall, a family that crossed the border in last spring's surge hears the decision on their asylum claim, a few months after they filed, not years.

What made the difference

What changed

  • Earned legal status: people who have lived here ten years or more get a green card after fingerprints and a criminal check, and so do Dreamers and people who have held temporary protection. Everyone else here on the day the law is signed can register, work legally and be protected from deportation while they reach ten years.1
  • Legal lines fixed: more family and employment green cards, and spouses and children of permanent residents no longer wait in line.
  • Trained asylum officers decide claims within 180 days of filing, and in about 90 days in a surge, with more officers instead of a ban on asking for protection.2
  • ICE is replaced, enforcement is focused by law on threats to security, serious crime and recent unlawful entry, and no more than 30,000 people are held on average, outside a declared surge.3

When it begins

  • Filing for legal status from late 2030.4
  • Workplace checks with E-Verify no earlier than October of the year after the law passes, and only once an independent inspector confirms legal status is up and running.5

What it costs

  • About $0.15 trillion over 2030-39 and about $22.5 billion in 2040, on our estimates.6

The honest limit

  • Surges will still happen. The plan meets them with more officers and a faster track, not a promise that they stop.
  • Not everyone who qualifies will apply; our estimates assume about 70% do.7

Hard questions

Isn't this amnesty?

It is earned status, by two routes. Dreamers, people who have held temporary protection, DACA or parole, and farm, care and construction workers with a work history get a green card on approval, after fingerprints and a criminal check.1 Everyone else here earns it after ten years' residence, under the registry already in immigration law, whose date has been stuck at 1972 since 1986.8

Will my information be used to deport my family?

No. The law bars using registration or program records for immigration enforcement, with penalties, except under a criminal warrant for a named person.

6:30 pm · A rented room in a shared apartment · Housing

The rent is still the hard part

Rent takes the biggest bite of Jordan's pay. The plan's rent help goes first to people with no home of their own, then to the poorest families with children and renters with disabilities. A single worker like him, who isn't homeless, isn't disabled and isn't raising children, has no funded route to rent help.

What reaches him is slower: more homes built that stay affordable for good, and the emergency fund if a crisis hits.

What made the difference

What changed

  • A million new rent awards from 2030 to 2033, paid as permanent spending so they never lapse in a budget fight, first for people with no home of their own: people who are homeless, families with children doubled up with others, and anyone leaving prison, a hospital, treatment or foster care with nowhere to go.1,2
  • A standing emergency fund for back rent, utilities, a deposit or moving costs, up to four months of the area's rent in a year, with legal help besides.3
  • Housing First: a home without first proving sobriety or entering treatment, with services offered.
  • A program for the poorest families with children, enrolling up to 300,000 families a year within a capped budget, with a right for the whole group named in law as a goal for the 2040s.4
  • Later stages in order: very low-income renters with long-term disabilities, of any age, from 2038; then a stage named in law for the 2040s, opening in 2042 at up to 400,000 households a year, for renters with the lowest incomes and the worst needs who no earlier stage reaches, single adults among them. The bill states that stage and its date, but its money is enacted only with its financing.5
  • Homes built and repaired that stay affordable for good, repairs for low-income owners, and property-tax help for older owners in states that join.

When it begins

  • Rent awards from 2030, 250,000 a year through 2033.1
  • The emergency fund from 2030, with $15 billion in its first two years.3
  • The families' program from 2034, and a review in 2041 of making it a right.4
  • Renters with disabilities from 2038; the stage for the 2040s opens in 2042 if its money is enacted.5

What it costs

  • About $0.81 trillion over 2030-39 and about $110 billion in 2040 as first priced, on our estimates. Capping the families' program in the financing settlement lowers the 2040 figure by about $18.6 billion.6

The honest limit

  • A single adult who pays most of his income in rent, but is not homeless, not disabled and not raising children, has no funded route to rent help beyond the emergency fund in a crisis.7
  • An adult without children who lives with family is not counted as without a home, so does not qualify for the first awards. The exception is a year after leaving prison, jail or foster care for a relative's or friend's home. Someone who comes home from treatment to their parents is not covered by it.2
  • The stage for the 2040s reaches only people who rent, and it is not paid for: the bill names it and its date, and a later Congress has to enact the money.5
  • Vouchers help only if families can use them. The plan assumes 90% of awards lead to a lease; the closest recent program reached 67% after about two years. That is the plan's weakest number.8
  • The program for the poorest families is capped: families compete by need, not by right, until a later Congress votes to make it a right.4

Hard questions

Does this end homelessness?

No one should promise that. For a family that gets a voucher, the effect is large and well tested. How far the national count falls is unknown, because people keep losing homes.

Will landlords take the vouchers?

Housing agencies are paid their full fees for the new awards so they can recruit landlords, search help comes with every award, and a national rule would make it illegal to turn someone away just for paying with a voucher. That rule needs a regular bill to pass.

What it costs, and who pays

After the children are asleep, Maya and Sam go over the month: no premiums, no deductibles, child care capped, the child benefit in. Their taxes are higher than their parents' were. On our estimates every fifth of households below the top comes out ahead on average, but an average isn't a promise for any one family.1

What made the difference

What changed

  • The top end and corporations pay first: the corporate rate rises to 28%, the top income tax rate returns to 39.6% for the highest earners, a new 45% bracket applies to incomes in the millions, and the 2025 law's tax cuts are reversed at the top.2
  • On our estimates the top 1% and corporations bear 65% of the plan's new general revenue over 2030-39, leaving out the health contribution that replaces premiums. That is the plan as booked, before the later steps below.3
  • Premiums give way to a health contribution on income: 3.80% from 2035, with nothing owed at or below 138% of poverty, rising to the full rate at 250%.4 In later years the rate can move a quarter point at a time under the health fund's own rule (pending).   • Who comes out ahead: on our estimates every fifth of households below the top comes out ahead on average, over 2030-39 and in 2040 and 2050 with the later steps on their first scoring.   • The financing doesn't touch Social Security, which has its own act and its own financing.15

  • On our estimates the margin is thin in the early 2040s, and the plan's first decade runs short, most in its later years as programs reach full scale.16

Hard questions

Is it paid for?

On our central estimates the plan as booked covers its new spending every year from 2040 on, with its own taxes, savings and state payments, and the top end and corporations paying first.8 But the margin is thin in the early 2040s, and the first decade runs short.16 The plan also chooses to bring the debt down, and that needs the later steps, a value-added tax among them.9

Who pays?

Corporations and the highest earners first. Then a health contribution on income, which replaces premiums and deductibles, exempts people at or below 138% of poverty, and pays only for health care.4,5 Then savings across the rest of the federal budget, defense included. From 2035, a value-added tax with a rebate for people below an income line (pending).11

Why a value-added tax, and why wait?

The other choice was a higher health contribution, which on our estimates does somewhat better for low-income workers. But the plan keeps that contribution for health care's own cost, with its rate set by the health fund's own rule, so the debt path is paid for by other steps, a value-added tax among them.5,18 The tax waits until 2035 so nothing new reaches the middle class in the plan's first years.12 Its rebate goes only to people below an income line, to keep the help for low and middle incomes; the line is still to be set (pending).11

2:00 pm · Eli's room · Housing

A room at his parents', for now

Eli would like a place of his own, and he can't afford one yet. He doesn't qualify for the plan's first rent awards. They go to people with no home of their own, and adults staying with family count only if they have children, or for a year after leaving prison, jail or foster care. The later stages reach the poorest families with children, then very low-income renters with disabilities. The plan names one more stage in law, for single renters with the lowest incomes and the worst rent burdens, but it isn't paid for yet, and it helps people who already rent. What made the difference

What changed

  • A million new rent awards from 2030 to 2033, paid as permanent spending so they never lapse in a budget fight, first for people with no home of their own: people who are homeless, families with children doubled up with others, and anyone leaving prison, a hospital, treatment or foster care with nowhere to go.1,2
  • A standing emergency fund for back rent, utilities, a deposit or moving costs, up to four months of the area's rent in a year, with legal help besides.3
  • Housing First: a home without first proving sobriety or entering treatment, with services offered.
  • A program for the poorest families with children, enrolling up to 300,000 families a year within a capped budget, with a right for the whole group named in law as a goal for the 2040s.4
  • Later stages in order: very low-income renters with long-term disabilities, of any age, from 2038; then a stage named in law for the 2040s, opening in 2042 at up to 400,000 households a year, for renters with the lowest incomes and the worst needs who no earlier stage reaches, single adults among them. The bill states that stage and its date, but its money is enacted only with its financing.5
  • Homes built and repaired that stay affordable for good, repairs for low-income owners, and property-tax help for older owners in states that join.

When it begins

  • Rent awards from 2030, 250,000 a year through 2033.1
  • The emergency fund from 2030, with $15 billion in its first two years.3
  • The families' program from 2034, and a review in 2041 of making it a right.4
  • Renters with disabilities from 2038; the stage for the 2040s opens in 2042 if its money is enacted.5

What it costs

  • About $0.81 trillion over 2030-39 and about $110 billion in 2040 as first priced, on our estimates. Capping the families' program in the financing settlement lowers the 2040 figure by about $18.6 billion.6

The honest limit

  • A single adult who pays most of his income in rent, but is not homeless, not disabled and not raising children, has no funded route to rent help beyond the emergency fund in a crisis.7
  • An adult without children who lives with family is not counted as without a home, so does not qualify for the first awards. Does this end homelessness?

No one should promise that. For a family that gets a voucher, the effect is large and well tested. How far the national count falls is unknown, because people keep losing homes.

Will landlords take the vouchers?

Housing agencies are paid their full fees for the new awards so they can recruit landlords, search help comes with every award, and a national rule would make it illegal to turn someone away just for paying with a voucher. That rule needs a regular bill to pass.

4:00 pm · The front porch, as a storm comes in · Clean power

Dividends with the check

Carol and Dev watch the storm roll in. Their Climate Dividends come four times a year, and when they claim Social Security, the dividends will come with their checks.

What made the difference

What changed

  • Power plants and large factories pay for the carbon they put in the air, and most of the money comes back to every adult as a Climate Dividend, paid four times a year, tax-free, with the first payment going out before the price begins. There is no carbon charge on your gas bill or at the pump.1
  • Clean power and the grid built faster: a clean electricity standard of 80% by 2035 and 100% by 2040, and a public builder for needed lines no one else will build.2
  • Help with energy bills that comes to you: a credit for households already getting SNAP, SSI or energy help, with no new application.
  • Money for every state and tribe to prepare for disasters, and a federal backstop for insurers in a catastrophic year.
  • A guarantee for workers when a mine, power plant or refinery closes: up to 90% of their old pay for two years, in a new job or in training, and lost local tax revenue replaced in full at first, then a little less each year for ten years.3

When it begins

  • The carbon price from 2030, at $25 a ton, reaching $150 by 2037.1
  • The bill credit from the winter of 2030-31.4
  • The worker guarantee for closures from 2030.3

What it costs

  • About $1.73 trillion over 2030-39 on our estimates, a little below what the plan first set aside for these purposes, and less after that, as the clean-energy credits step down under the law to nothing by 2047.5,6
  • On our estimates the dividend is about $405 an adult in 2035, in that year's dollars; it moves with what polluters pay, and shrinks as pollution falls.7

The honest limit

  • Electricity costs somewhat more while the clean grid is built: on our estimates the average price is about a tenth higher than under current law by 2040. On the carbon price and the dividend alone, on our estimates, the lowest-income two-fifths of households come out ahead on average, nationally, though some in states that burn the most coal may be behind.8
  • The clean electricity standard is a target. On our estimates the grid reaches about 73% clean power in 2035 and 87% in 2040, held back by connections to the grid, and suppliers that fall short pay a capped fee.9
  • It does not reach the 2035 climate goal, a 61-66% cut from 2005, on its own: on our estimates carbon emissions from energy fall about 41% below 2005 by 2035.10
  • The worker guarantee covers closures, not every oil and gas job.

Hard questions

Isn't this a carbon tax?

It is a price on the biggest polluters: power plants and large industrial facilities. Households pay no charge of their own, and most of the money goes back to every adult, with the first payment made before the price begins.

Didn't Canada try this and drop it?

Canada dropped its charge on consumers and kept the price on large emitters. This plan is the part Canada kept.

What it costs, and who pays

Carol and Dev go over their retirement plan: Social Security in full, no Medicare premiums, and the health contribution on their income in place of them. They want to enjoy the years ahead, and they set a little aside to help Eli with a deposit when the time comes.

What changed

  • On our estimates the top 1% and corporations bear 65% of the plan's new general revenue over 2030-39, leaving out the health contribution that replaces premiums. That is the plan as booked, before the later steps below.2
  • Premiums give way to a health contribution on income: 3.80% from 2035, with nothing owed at or below 138% of poverty, rising to the full rate at 250%.3 In later years the rate can move a quarter point at a time under the health fund's own rule (pending).   • On our estimates the margin is thin in the early 2040s, and the plan's first decade runs short, most in its later years as programs reach full scale.16

What it is for

  • Freedom you can use. A right matters more when you have the time, security and support to exercise it: to care for someone, change jobs, learn, make a home and take part in your community.
  • A society that cares for its people. Children should grow up without poverty. Illness, disability and aging should bring dependable support, and the people who give care deserve the pay and respect that make good care possible.
  • Power that answers to people. Voting should be easy and secure, money should not drown out citizens, and the officials who serve the public should not profit from what they know.
  • Responsibility across generations. Pay honestly for what we promise, say who pays, and leave the next generation a cleaner country and a government that works.

How it happens

  • Build a shared purpose. Start with the changes people want in their own lives, and organize around a program people can understand and help shape.
  • Elect a coalition for change. Turn that purpose into the power to govern, and press every candidate to say how they will deliver it.
  • Make the promises real. Pass the laws, staff the agencies and publish the counts, so people can see whether each promise is kept.
  • Keep showing up, and keep asking. Check the results, say what isn't working and change it. A plan that can't be questioned can't be trusted.
