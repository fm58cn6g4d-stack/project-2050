### SNAP under P.L. 119-21 in the 2029 baseline (A-8)

The 2029 baseline applies the 2025 law's SNAP changes (`snap2025.py`): the wider work rule, calibrated to CBO's participation estimates by group, and the energy-assistance and internet-cost changes. Not applied: the state cost share's state responses and the noncitizen rule.

- SNAP in 2029, as the CPS reports it: $48.2B at prior law, $43.4B under the law (10.1% less). About 2.05 million people (on the CPS's weights) lose benefits under the work rule. Probabilities that an at-risk adult loses benefits: 55-64 without children 0.811, parents whose youngest child is 14-17 0.484, 18-54 without children 0.508.
- SPM poverty in 2029: 13.20% at prior-law SNAP, 13.26% under the law (children 13.37% and 13.43%); 210 thousand more people poor. The CPS records under half of SNAP's benefits, so this understates the law's effect (`MODEL-CARD.md`, A-8).

### Dynamic effects

The ledger carries a dynamic sensitivity (not in any total): about −$0.26T over 2030-39 (−$1.24T to $0.66T) under CBO/JCT-style conventions, and about $1.14T under Talarico-style assumptions (estimates). ### Limits

- **Shares:** the measured keys are the household model's (`keys.csv`); the rest are typed estimates, anchored to published patterns, until tax data replace them.
- **Year profiles are shapes, not scored annual paths.**
- **No behavior:** coverage shifts, avoidance beyond the ledger's haircuts and macro effects are left out (the maternal-employment effect of child care, plausibly $25-45B a year of federal revenue at maturity, is not counted).

- **Corporate taxes:** 25% labor, 75% capital (JCT/CBO).
- **Not modeled:** the minimum wage (LAB-4), the H-1B fee, COM-5's bank-fee limits, veterans health (NSC-10) and immigration's legalization effects.

## 10. The CBO-convention check

CBO's distribution reports (*The Distribution of Household Income, 2022*, January 2026, SRC-4802) rank households by **income before transfers and taxes**: market income, including employers' payroll taxes and health premiums, plus Social Security, Medicare, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation. They divide it by the square root of household size, form quintiles of **equal numbers of people**, leave households with negative income out of the groups, and use the same income as the denominator of average tax rates. D3 ranks by expanded cash income (all cash income, means-tested cash included, plus the employer's payroll tax) over the square root of household size, in quintiles of **equal numbers of households**. The steps below move from D3 to CBO one convention at a time on the 2029 baseline households (`conventions.py`).

**What the check finds:**
- **The grouping is the convention that matters for the D3 answer.** Equal numbers of people move 8.2% of households to another quintile and put 32.3M households in the first quintile against 27.1M in the fifth, since low-income households are smaller. Scenario Q's fourth quintile goes from −$90 to −$290 a household. The sign is the same, so D3's verdict on Q does not depend on the convention, but the size of the shortfall moves by more than its sampling error (section 9).
- **The income definition matters little until the health values go in.** Taking out SSI and TANF and dropping negative incomes moves another 0.1% of households. Adding Medicare and employers' premiums moves 21.3% of households in all from their D3 quintile: retirees on Medicare and covered workers move up. The combined rates of the second to fifth quintiles fall by 1.2 to 2.3 points (a larger denominator), while the first quintile's rises by 2.0, since the retirees who leave it paid little tax. The fourth quintile stays at −$291.
- **The rates' shape matches CBO's; the levels differ for data reasons.** On CBO's conventions the first quintile's income tax rate is -3.7%, against CBO's −10.1% in 2022. The CPS under-reports low earnings and so the refundable credits (the model's earned income credit is $41B against SOI's $66B, section 2), and CBO also corrects under-reported transfers and counts the premium credit. The top 1%'s rate, 27.7%, is above CBO's 23.9% because the CPS misses most of the top's capital gains, which are taxed at lower rates.
- **What it means for D3:** keep equal households, the convention D3 states its per-household results on and compares with the legacy model's. Report CBO's grouping as a sensitivity where a quintile's result is near zero.
