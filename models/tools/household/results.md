### SNAP under P.L. 119-21 in the 2029 baseline (A-8)

The 2029 baseline applies the 2025 law's SNAP changes (`snap2025.py`): the wider work rule, calibrated to CBO's participation estimates by group, and the energy-assistance and internet-cost changes. Not applied: the state cost share's state responses and the noncitizen rule.

- SNAP in 2029, as the CPS reports it: $48.2B at prior law, $43.4B under the law (10.1% less). About 2.05 million people (on the CPS's weights) lose benefits under the work rule. - SPM poverty in 2029: 13.20% at prior-law SNAP, 13.26% under the law (children 13.37% and 13.43%); 210 thousand more people poor. The CPS records under half of SNAP's benefits, so this understates the law's effect (`MODEL-CARD.md`, A-8).

Each is a row of the D3 test (`incidence-rows.csv`, INC1 to INC5). Its amount is the booked ledger line; the household model gives its key (`keys.csv`, `rules.py`). Model amounts are 2029, at full take-up; booked amounts are the FY2030-39 yearly average.

| INC-3: SSI's limits, exclusions and the territories | `ssi` 75.5%, `ssi_exclusions` 24.5% (the booking's parts) | — | — |
| INC-4: the refundable dependent care credit | `cdctc` (rule) | $0.34B | $0.5B |
| INC-5: the exclusion rule's forgone offsets | `housing` | — | — |

- **INC-4, rescoped to care no other program pays for (Espen, November 13, option B).** On the CPS's child care spending, net of the child care guarantee (free below 85% of median income, a capped charge above; the CPS's national median for four-person units, $160,852, stands in for state median income), refundability pays about $0.34B a year to about 0.41 million tax units, 100% of it for school-age care. Most families who pay for care owe enough tax to use today's credit, and the guarantee takes over the care of the youngest children. *The school-age block grant (CCDF, FAM-2.04):* the survey asks what families paid, and the subsidies they receive are not in it, so the school-age spending it reports is already net of what CCDF pays; the plan keeps the block grant's school-age reach at today's level, so nothing more is netted. CCDF serves about 0.71 million children aged 5 to 12 a month (EV-CAR-700), against 2.99 million in the CPS's units below CCDF's income line that pay for care; netting that reach again, as if the survey reported care before CCDF's payments, would give $0.28B, a lower bound. *What the CPS cannot see:* care for a disabled adult (not in the survey), and spending it under-reports (it asks only about care while working, and misses much informal and part-year care): with spending a quarter, a half or twice as high, the gain is $0.39B, $0.43B or $0.49B, held down by the $3,000 and $6,000 limits and the earnings test. The full-take-up assumption pushes the other way. The booking (`dossiers/secure-floor/6-cost.md`, part 1) adds a judgment for adult care and under-reporting and takes off for take-up. *What would settle it:* IRS Statistics of Income's Form 2441 claims by income and tax owed, with the qualifying person's age.
- **INC-1.02 moves no money on the CPS** under the reading the rule takes: the Census already assigns each child to one tax unit by residence. If the earned income credit's age test were instead aligned to the child benefit's (under 18), about 1.71 million filing units with an 18-year-old or a student 19-23 would lose about $3.58B a year (about $2,094 each). The lead confirmed the no-loss reading on #320: no family loses the credit from the shared definition.
- **INC-3.03's territory residents are not in the CPS**, nor in the D3 test's households; their benefit is spread like today's SSI.

## 7. The 65-cent ceiling check (INC-5.02, shared rule 2)

For the three typical families at 50% to 300% of poverty: the combined cents lost per added dollar of earnings from every federal layer under the decided plan, taxes included, against the published ceiling of 65 cents. Housing's 30% rent rule is outside it, as INC-5.02 says. Layers (`families.py`, `plan_budget`): payroll with paid leave's worker share (FAM-3.04, 0.3%); income tax at 2029 law with FAM-1's child benefit in place of today's child credit and INC-1.01's childless credit; SNAP on the rules INC-2 restores, with its earned income deduction at 45% (INC-2.02, Espen's option D, `DEC-2026-11-13-03`), with and without the excess shelter deduction; the premium tax credit on the marketplace in 2030-34, which the single plan ends from 2035; child care's charge for the child under 5 (FAM-2.01), net of INC-4's refundable credit. The charge depends on the state's median income: the CPS's lowest state ($98,693 for four in 2029) and its national median ($160,852) stand in for HHS's figures. 2029 law and prices throughout. The 2030-34 marketplace cases at 100-138% of poverty apply in states that have not expanded Medicaid; in expansion states those adults have Medicaid instead.

**What INC-2.02 fixes:** before it, the single parent at 100% of poverty, 2035 on, with the rent, reached 75.0 cents: SNAP took 36 cents, not memo 7's 24, because each dollar also shrinks the shelter deduction, and FAM-1 replaces the child credit whose nonrefundable part today offsets the 10% bracket at these incomes (+10 cents). With the deduction at 45%, SNAP counts 55 cents of each earned dollar, so it takes about 17 cents, and up to about 25 where the shelter deduction still shrinks with income (the married couple at 125%): the same family is at payroll 8.0, income tax 31.1, SNAP 16.5, total **55.5**.

**The one case still above the ceiling:** the married couple with two children and one earner, on the marketplace in 2030-34 with the rent, at 125% of poverty: payroll 8.0, income tax 31.1, SNAP 24.8, the premium credit 2.1, total **65.9**. It applies only in states that have not expanded Medicaid, and only until the single plan ends the premium credit in 2035. INC-5.02's monitoring rule covers it (a family type above the ceiling for two years triggers a review by the owning dossiers); it is not fixed here.

**The options Espen weighed** (2029, static, full take-up):

- a nonrefundable family offset against income tax of $750 a child: about $42.6B a year ($28.9B at $500). Not chosen;
- SNAP's earned income deduction from 20% to 45% (option D, chosen): up to about $32.1B a year on the CPS's annual earnings of SNAP households, an upper bound (SNAP counts monthly income only in months on SNAP; the CPS's annual earnings include months off it). The booking uses SNAP's own records instead (`dossiers/secure-floor/6-cost.md`, part 1): about $12 billion in FY2030, range $8-16 billion.

## 8. The D3 report: the revenue-mix test, the years, and what moves the fourth quintile

This section carries the parts of the retired legacy report (`legacy/index/revenue-household-incidence-model.md`, last generated November 13, 2026) that section 5 does not: D3's revenue-mix test, the year-by-year paths, the rows that move the fourth quintile, the open choices, dynamic effects and the limits. D3's test: every income group below the top comes out ahead on net from the agenda's combined taxes, fees and benefits, and new general-fund revenue comes mostly from the top 1% and corporations. The engine is `d3.py`; "legacy" is the typed keys over 140 million households, which the budget model's readers still use, and "model" the measured keys.

- **Scenario Q, the decided package: the household test does not pass.** On the measured keys Q1 to Q4 are +$9,651, +$5,628, +$3,369, −$287 a household a year, FY2030-39 average (legacy keys: +$8,947, +$6,590, +$3,394, −$485). The fourth quintile is the group behind.
- **The revenue-mix test passes in every scenario:** the top 1% (by incidence) and corporations (statutory) bear 76% of new general-fund revenue on scenario Q (legacy keys 76%; the rule is more than half). The EDU-9B fund's contribution and SAF-R2 are not general-fund revenue and are left out of it.
- **The employer health-exclusion cap is the swing:** at the median premium (C19, the fallback) the fourth quintile reads −$450, against −$287 at the 75th percentile (C19b, decided). Once the employer groups move (2033-35) the exclusion ends (HX), so the cap stops applying; the settlement must take that overlap out.
- **Health is not in scenario Q on either side.** Scenario Q-H7 adds the single plan's household rows (H7h, H7w), long-term care (H9) and HX's tax, without the plan's financing (the settlement's): the fourth quintile reads +$18,386, a reading before financing, not a pass.
- **The education fund (EDU-9B, held)** as a closer in place of C19b and C20 gives the fourth quintile −$1,491. Its contribution (about 1.4 points of payroll) is counted entirely on workers.
- **Averages hide losers inside the fourth quintile**: a household without children, marketplace or Medicare coverage, and with employer coverage above the 75th-percentile premium, gets few of the benefits and most of the costs. Within-quintile spread is the household model's next addition.

### Open choices this test informs

These are options with their effects, not edits to any canonical file: (1) the early-year dip, by the sequencing levers above; (2) EDU-9B as a closer (a D3 and D4 decision: a middle-reaching contribution and a new dedicated fund); (3) SAF-R2, dropping the alcohol excise (scenario Q-SAF); (4) the Climate Dividend's share of the transition money (CLI-2C), which helps every quintile equally; (5) health's financing, which D3 can be read for only once the settlement's taxes are rowed against scenario Q-H7; (6) the 65-cent ceiling (section 7).

### Dynamic effects

The ledger carries a dynamic sensitivity (not in any total): about −$0.33T over 2030-39 (−$1.36T to $0.64T) under CBO/JCT-style conventions, and about $1.12T under Talarico-style assumptions (estimates). It does not change the quintile results: most of it is revenue (lower investment and more avoidance at the top), which lands on the top quintile and the gap; the household-facing pieces roughly offset (the care labor-supply gain against small hours effects from coverage and the child benefit); and the wage effect of corporate taxes is already in (25% on workers, the JCT/CBO convention). A dynamic gap closed with middle-reaching revenue (C19 or C20) would move the quintiles and should be run as its own scenario.

### Limits

- **Shares:** the measured keys are the household model's (`keys.csv`); the rest are typed estimates, anchored to published patterns, until tax data replace them.
- **Year profiles are shapes, not scored annual paths.**
- **No behavior:** coverage shifts, avoidance beyond the ledger's haircuts and macro effects are left out (the maternal-employment effect of child care, plausibly $25-45B a year of federal revenue at maturity, is not counted).
- **Public goods are left out** on both sides: the agency rebuild, transit service, climate credits to firms, defense, education research (EDU-8) and the civic and information rows.
- **Full pass-through** of carbon, border, transport and alcohol charges to households, which is conservative for the middle.
- **Corporate taxes:** 25% labor, 75% capital (JCT/CBO).
- **Not modeled:** the minimum wage (LAB-4), the H-1B fee, COM-5's bank-fee limits, veterans health (NSC-10) and immigration's legalization effects.
- **Typed inputs kept:** 2034 group incomes of $23K, $60K, $105K, $180K and $520K and $4.3M for the top 1% (legacy version); 30% of Medicare Advantage capture passed to enrollees; 30% of clean-energy credits to households; Part B premiums at 28% of HLT-2; EDU-9B at about 1.4 points of uncapped payroll ($2.27T over 2030-39) with $0.63T of benefits beyond the chosen rows (`legacy/domains/domain-education.md`). Every row and its basis is in `models/data/inputs/incidence-rows.csv`; every typed key in `incidence-keys.csv`.
