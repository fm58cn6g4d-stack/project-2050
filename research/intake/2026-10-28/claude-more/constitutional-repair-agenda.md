# A Constitutional Repair Agenda for the 21st Century
### Anti-capture amendments, draft texts, and a sequencing strategy anchored to a post-2028 federal trifecta

*Working synthesis — illustrative drafting, not settled language. Every text below is a starting point meant to be marked up.*

---

## Framing: the three filters

Across this project, an amendment earns a place in the family only if it clears three tests at once. Most "good government" wishes fail at least one.

1. **Constitutional necessity.** Ordinary legislation can't durably do the job — either because the Court has constitutionalized the obstacle (money, after *Citizens United*; partisan gerrymandering's non-justiciability, after *Rucho*), or because the reform must bind the legislature against its own future self, which no statute can survive. *This is why the congressional stock-trading ban is out: a statute solves it completely, so reaching for Article V is a pile driver on a finish nail.*
2. **Fox/henhouse fit.** The reform is blocked at the legislative veto-point by an entrenched minority whose power is legislative rather than popular — ideally, where the body that must ratify is the body the reform constrains. This is what makes **convention ratification** (elected single-purpose delegates, bypassing captured legislatures — the mechanism Congress used once, for the 21st Amendment) the correct instrument rather than an incidental one.
3. **Catalytic effect.** Repair at this node lowers the activation energy for repair at the next — a positive feedback loop running the "double unraveling" backward, rather than a one-off fix.

A fourth, quieter principle governs the drafting itself: **constitutionalize procedure and capacity, never substantive policy.** The 21st Amendment exists to undo the 18th — the canonical proof that hard-coding a policy answer (Prohibition) into the Constitution is a category error. We empower the polity to reach its own answers; we do not freeze the answers.

## Part I — The amendment family, by circuit node

Think of democracy as a circuit: **inputs → integrity → checks → self-repair.** Capture at any node degrades the whole; repair at each lowers resistance at the next.

### Inputs — who votes, who wins, whom they answer to
The highest-catalytic layer, because it changes the *incentives* politicians face, and incentive change cascades.

- **A. Democratic Integrity (campaign finance / anti-capture).** Restores the authority to limit money in elections and to distinguish natural persons from artificial entities. *Necessity:* high — *Citizens United* constitutionalized the obstacle. *Catalysis:* removes the transmission belt converting concentrated wealth into a legislative veto over popular majorities — plausibly the single highest-leverage climate intervention inside the democratic system, precisely because it says nothing about carbon.
- **B. Fair Representation (independent redistricting).** Bans partisan gerrymandering and — critically — restores its *justiciability*. *Necessity:* high post-*Rucho*, which slammed the federal courthouse door. *Fit:* near-perfect fox/henhouse; the beneficiaries of gerrymandering are exactly the ratifiers you'd otherwise beg.
- **C. Right to Vote.** Establishes an affirmative individual right to vote — which the Constitution conspicuously lacks. *Necessity:* high and rising, as the Court narrows the statutory franchise (*Shelby County*, *Brnovich*); a statutory right is a right held at the Court's sufferance.

### Integrity — trust in the count
- **D. Election Integrity / Anti-Subversion.** Makes certification a ministerial act insulated from partisan discretion. *Necessity:* the statutory patch (Electoral Count Reform Act) sits one hostile majority from repeal. *Catalysis:* defensive but enormous — it starves the "stolen election" delegitimization engine of its fuel by taking a corrupted count off the table.

### Checks — whether power stays constrained
- **E. Supreme Court Regularization (18-year staggered terms).** Two appointments per presidential term; lowers the stakes of any single vacancy. *Fit:* looser fox/henhouse (the conflicted party is the Court, not the ratifiers), but uniquely catalytic as the *durability insurance* on every other item — a regularized Court is the answer to the Strauss hollowing-out risk. *(Presidential-accountability reforms — reversing* Trump v. United States *immunity — live here too but are too partisan-coded now; hold for later in the sequence.)*

### Self-repair — the capacity to fix itself
- **F. Future Generations (the procedural climate fit).** Not an environmental right or emissions mandate — a *representational* fix for the structural under-representation of future generations in institutions optimized for two-to-six-year horizons. Fixes *who counts*, extended along the time axis, exactly as B and C fix it along the spatial one.
- **G. Constitutional Self-Repair (rolling convention + convention-ratification revival).** The meta-reform. Re-opens the amendment channel itself so pressure finds a legitimate outlet instead of routing through the Court's back door or raw norm-breaking. *The deepest anti-unraveling move: it repairs the healing mechanism, not just an organ.*

## Part II — Draft texts

### Amendment A — Democratic Integrity

> **Section 1.** The people have compelling interests in free and fair elections, in representative self-government, and in the political equality of natural persons.
>
> **Section 2.** Congress and the several States, within their respective jurisdictions, may regulate and set reasonable limits on the raising and spending of money to influence elections, including independent expenditures and spending on ballot measures.
>
> **Section 3.** Congress and the States may distinguish between natural persons and artificial entities. Artificial entities possess only such rights and powers as are conferred upon them by law and may be denied the use of their funds to influence elections.
>
> **Section 4.** Nothing in this article shall be construed to grant any power to abridge the freedom of the press.
>
> **Section 5.** Congress and the States shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

*Design note:* deliberately a **hybrid** — permissive enough to build the ratification coalition (§2's "reasonable limits" lets a federalist and a progressive both vote yes), but specified enough (§2's explicit reach to *independent expenditures*; §3's charter-power language, borrowed from the Montana logic) to deny a hostile Court the room to hollow it out by reading "reasonable" down to nothing. §4 forecloses the strongest *Citizens United* objection (media suppression).

### Amendment B — Fair Representation

> **Section 1.** Districts for the election of members of the House of Representatives and of any State legislature shall afford the voters an equal and effective opportunity to elect candidates of their choice, and shall not be drawn with the intent or effect of unduly favoring or disfavoring any political party.
>
> **Section 2.** Each State shall provide for districting through an independent and impartial process, established by law and insulated from control by officials whose election the districts govern.
>
> **Section 3.** Compliance with this article presents a justiciable question. District plans shall be subject to review in the courts of the United States and of the States.
>
> **Section 4.** Congress may establish uniform standards to implement this article.

*Design note:* §3 is the load-bearing clause — it directly overrides *Rucho*'s "political question" holding and restores judicial enforceability, without which the standard is hortatory.

### Amendment C — Right to Vote

> **Section 1.** Every citizen of the United States who is eighteen years of age or older shall have the fundamental right to vote in public elections held in the jurisdiction in which the citizen resides.
>
> **Section 2.** This right shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or any State. Any law burdening this right shall be permitted only where narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest.
>
> **Section 3.** Congress and the States shall have power to enforce and to expand access to this right by appropriate legislation; where their provisions differ, the more protective shall govern.

*Design note:* §2 writes strict scrutiny into the text so the right can't be diluted to a "reasonable regulation" balancing test. §3's ratchet lets states lead upward (as several already do) without inviting a race to the bottom.

### Amendment D — Election Integrity

> **Section 1.** The tabulation and certification of lawful votes, and the ascertainment and transmission of election results, are ministerial duties to be performed as prescribed by law and free from partisan discretion.
>
> **Section 2.** No officer charged with any such duty shall refuse, delay, or alter a certification or ascertainment that is lawfully due; disputes as to the lawful count shall be resolved exclusively through process established by law.
>
> **Section 3.** Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

### Amendment E — Supreme Court Regularization

> **Section 1.** The Supreme Court of the United States shall be composed of nine Justices.
>
> **Section 2.** The President shall nominate, and by and with the advice and consent of the Senate shall appoint, one Justice during the first and one during the third year of each four-year presidential term. Each shall serve as an active Justice for a term of eighteen years.
>
> **Section 3.** Upon completion of an active term, a Justice shall assume senior status and may perform such duties as law provides, retaining the office and its protections of tenure and compensation.
>
> **Section 4.** Should the Senate fail to confirm a nominee within a period fixed by law, the President shall appoint an interim Justice to serve until confirmation, in the manner Congress shall provide.
>
> **Section 5.** Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

*Design note:* §2 produces exactly two appointments per term and the 18-year stagger. §3 (senior status) preserves life tenure in substance, defusing the strongest legal objection. §4 disarms confirmation-blockade sabotage.

### Amendment F — Future Generations

> **Section 1.** In the exercise of its powers, the United States shall give due consideration to the interests of future generations.
>
> **Section 2.** There shall be an independent office, established by Congress, charged with assessing and publicly reporting the long-term and intergenerational effects of major actions of the United States, and empowered to be heard in the making of such decisions.
>
> **Section 3.** This article creates duties of consideration and disclosure and shall not be construed to mandate any particular policy or to create a private right of action for damages.

*Design note:* §3 is what keeps this *procedural* — it fixes representation of futurity (the actual structural deficit: short-termism) without constitutionalizing any substantive answer that would age like the 18th Amendment. This is the correct — and only defensible — way climate touches the text.

### Amendment G — Constitutional Self-Repair

> **Section 1.** In addition to the methods provided in Article V, an amendment to this Constitution may be proposed by the legislatures, or by conventions, of three-fourths of the several States, each acting upon identical text; such proposal shall become valid as part of this Constitution when agreed to by two-thirds of each House of Congress.
>
> **Section 2.** Whenever Congress proposes an amendment, it may direct that ratification be by conventions in the several States, the delegates to which shall be elected by the people thereof for that sole purpose. Congress and the States shall facilitate such conventions by appropriate legislation.
>
> **Section 3.** Nothing in this article alters the thresholds of two-thirds and three-fourths required for the proposal and adoption of amendments.

*Design note:* §1 is the Sachs "rolling convention" flip — states propose, Congress ratifies — preserving both thresholds (§3) while letting broadly popular reforms originate outside a gridlocked Congress. §2 revives and standardizes the ratifying-convention route. This is the hardest to pass (the bootstrapping paradox: you need the broken process to fix the process) and the most catalytic, so it is pursued *last* or via convention-threat leverage — never first.

## Part III — Sequencing strategy

**Within the family, lead with the least culture-coded, most fox/henhouse-legible reforms and bootstrap outward.** The virtuous cycle must be started from its most *consensual* node, because early wins build the convention-ratification infrastructure and the public habit of direct constitutional participation that the harder, polarized reforms later require.

Rough order of assault:

1. **The inputs trio first — B (redistricting), A (money), C (franchise).** These clear cross-partisan supermajorities (redistricting and anti-corruption poll in the 70s across parties), share the "un-distort who holds power" logic, and are the cleanest single-question convention-ratification vehicles.
2. **D (integrity) alongside or just after.** Rides the same democratic-process framing; low substantive controversy once decoupled from any specific election grievance.
3. **F (future generations) can move relatively early** — procedural, low-salience-controversial, and it quietly seeds the intergenerational frame.
4. **E (Court) after the input wins** — more contested, but it is the durability insurance that protects everything already banked, so it must precede any attempt at G.
5. **G (self-repair) last, or as a running convention-threat throughout.** The keystone you build *toward*.

**The mode choice is itself part of the sequence.** For every amendment that reaches proposal, press Congress to specify **convention-mode ratification** (Amendment G formalizes this; until then it's a lobbying ask each time), so ratification bypasses captured legislatures — the 21st Amendment logic, applied to the whole anti-capture family.

## Part IV — Situating it in a post-2028 realignment

### The binding constraint is Republican votes, not Democratic seats
Because proposal takes two-thirds of each chamber, **there is no such thing as a party-line amendment** — any amendment that ever clears Congress is bipartisan by definition. This reframes everything. A Democratic majority, however large, is a *launch pad*, not a landing: on its own it can pass statutes, build the record, and fund the state machinery, but the amendment's fate hinges entirely on Republican crossovers. The gap to close is *not* public opinion — roughly 74% of Republican **voters** already support reform. It is an elite-and-donor problem at the level of sitting officeholders, and it is the reason the "split the right" operation isn't optional flavor but the load-bearing wall.

*Technical note:* the two-thirds bar is of members **present and voting** given a quorum (*National Prohibition Cases*, 1920), not two-thirds of the full body. Turnout on a vote this momentous will be near-total, so 67 is the right working number — but a disciplined majority that whips its own side to full attendance while a few opponents are absent can shave the real number a seat or two. Marginal, but a lever a good floor operation uses.

### The whip math under a strong-wave scenario
Stipulate an exceptional 2026–2028 cycle: by January 2029, **54–57 Senate seats in aligned hands** (Democrats plus flipped independents willing to vote for reform). That leaves **10–13 Republican crossovers** needed. Perhaps ~5 come once popular will is unmistakable, leaving **4–8 hard votes to whip** — much harder, not impossible. In the House, a 240+ aligned majority means finding **~50 of a ~195-member Republican conference** — roughly a quarter.

### The wave/moderate paradox — the trap in the arithmetic
Here is the counterintuitive catch: **the wave that delivers 54–57 aligned seats is the same wave that kills the moderates you were counting on to cross.** A surge of that magnitude wins by flipping *purple-state* Republican seats — so the Republicans who **survive** it are disproportionately from deep-red states, and the surviving conference is more conservative and more donor-aligned on average, not less. The five "gettable once the will is visible" moderates are exactly the profile a wave sweeps out. There is an inverse relationship hiding in the model: **the bigger the Democratic denominator, the thinner the pool of easy Republican crossovers.** You cannot simultaneously maximize the wave and the moderate remnant; they trade against each other.

### The resolution: populist crossovers, asymmetric frame, symmetric text
The crossover votes therefore come **not from a shrinking center but from the populist right**, and through the frame that makes it possible: not "join a good-government reform" but **"ban the corrupt donor-class money"** — theirs and, pointedly, the other side's. That reframing lets a MAGA-aligned member vote yes as an *extension* of an anti-elite brand (anti-oligarchy, anti-foreign-money, drain-the-swamp) rather than a betrayal of it. And the wave *helps* here rather than hurting: it terrifies the Republican survivors, and a red-state populist who just watched colleagues lose, facing a primary electorate that is 74% pro-reform, may conclude that *obstructing* this is the dangerous vote. The whip strategy accordingly flips: **from *persuade the moderates* (mostly dead in a wave) to *frighten the populists with their own voters* (more promising precisely in a wave).**

Two disciplines keep this honest:
- **Rhetoric ≠ record.** Populist anti-corporate talk is loud, but many of these members are funded by the very networks they run against; converting affect into a hard vote against their own funders is the real test. The genuinely heterodox may do it; the packaging-only populists won't.
- **Asymmetric persuasion, symmetric text.** The winning *frame* is asymmetric ("ban *their* corruption"), but the *language* must stay neutral, or you get a partisan-weaponized amendment that collapses when the coalition shifts — the 21st-Amendment unwind problem. The permissive **For Our Freedom** design squares this: populists vote yes for their reasons, Democrats and moderates for theirs, while the text empowers regulation symmetrically. Wrap asymmetric persuasion around symmetric text.

### The enforcement mechanism: a bilateral primary pledge
The whip theory above rests on "frighten the holdouts with their own voters" — but that sentence has no verb until you name the instrument that does the frightening. **The primary pledge is that instrument.** It is the transmission belt that converts the inert fact *74% of Republican voters support reform* into the operative fact *fifteen Republican senators are afraid to vote no*. It is the missing half of the whip story — the specific device that ties together the latent majority, the populist-crossover theory, and the safe-seat/primary-electorate targeting that otherwise float separately.

**Why the instrument works — the counterintuitive mechanism.** The model is Norquist's Taxpayer Protection Pledge, and its power was never money (Americans for Tax Reform is a small shop). Its power was making a *single bright-line vote* career-ending and then **outsourcing enforcement to primary voters**, so the deterrent became self-executing — he rarely had to spend against anyone, yet it disciplined a whole party on taxes for thirty years. The lesson: a pledge is **asymmetric warfare against asymmetric money.** You will never outspend the donor class dollar-for-dollar; you don't have to. You have to *re-price the incumbent's calculation* so the expected cost of crossing their own primary electorate exceeds the cost of crossing their donors. A pledge is a cheap, self-executing device for changing that one number — the correct weapon for a movement permanently outgunned on cash.

**Design: a bright line, not a manifesto.** Norquist's pledge is two sentences. The discipline is that "kept or broken" must be a *binary fact* verifiable from public data, never a judgment call — the moment enforcement requires interpretation, the evader wins. So: a very small number of commitments, of two kinds. *Positional* planks ("I will vote for the amendment; I will back the charter measure") are cheap to sign and keep, so they mostly signal. *Behavioral* planks ("no corporate-PAC money; no coordinated super-PAC spending; small-dollar funding above threshold X") are costly — and cost is what makes a signal credible, because they bind the signer against something they'd otherwise want and are checkable from FEC filings without anyone's cooperation. **The behavioral planks are load-bearing; a pledge that costs nothing deters nothing.**

**The enforcement engine — three parts, only one expensive.** The two cheap parts are a public *scorecard* (signed / kept / broken, in a form a local reporter or primary opponent can weaponize) and a credible standing *threat* to back a primary challenge against a violator. The expensive part that buys the threat's credibility is the **demonstration case**: one early, unambiguous instance where a breaker or conspicuous non-signer visibly loses — or is scared badly enough that everyone watching updates. Deterrence is *bought once* by making an example and then compounds for free. This inverts the metric: **success is compliance, not scalps.** You measure the pledge's power by the silence around it — the votes you never had to fight because incumbents pre-emptively signed — not by heads on pikes. But you must take the first head, or the whole thing is a press release.

**It must be bilateral — the hard part Norquist never faced.** Two-thirds means disciplining *both* primaries, which means two operations sharing commitments but nothing else. The **Democratic track** is defensive and easy: lock in every Democratic senator so the denominator never wobbles; make the pledge table-stakes in Democratic primaries (partly true already of the no-corporate-PAC pledge). The **Republican track** is the whole ballgame and *cannot be run by the same people* — it needs its own conservative-fronted vehicle, conservative messengers, conservative brand, and the "ban the corrupt money, theirs and ours" frame, because a pledge that reads as progressive is radioactive in a GOP primary regardless of its substance. The architecture is therefore **symmetric commitments, asymmetric messengers** — identical language, two entirely separate delivery systems. This is the amendment-drafting principle (asymmetric frame / symmetric text) applied one layer down. Get it wrong — let the right-track look like a Democratic front — and you've built a device that locks in your own votes while freeing theirs, which is worse than useless.

**Metabolize the hypocrisy.** Funding the primary challenges that give the pledge teeth means operating a PAC and raising money — the very machinery you're abolishing, and the "you're doing the thing you decry" attack writes itself. The only answer is to make the enforcement vehicle a *living model of the reform*: small-dollar funded, radically transparent, sworn off exactly the categories the pledge bans. That's not just defense — it's proof of concept that a competitive operation *can* run on the clean rules, which is itself part of what you're demonstrating to a skeptical public.

**Targeting — where it reaches, and where it doesn't.** The pledge is precisely the instrument for the safe-seat deep-red survivors who are immune to general-election waves and exposed *only* to primaries, and the GOP primary electorate's genuine anti-donor-class streak (the 74%) means it's not hostile terrain. But the paradox nests: the very safest R+25 incumbents may be so safe that losing a primary just swaps in someone equally opposed, so the pledge does its best work in the *moderately* safe tier and thins at the extreme. **The pledge and the scandal are complements:** the pledge builds and pre-commits the standing crossover cohort (token → substantial) during the first-term phase; the galvanizing scandal converts the residual holdouts; and the pledge's pre-built credibility is what lets the scandal convert them *fast*, before the window shuts.

**Timing and failure modes.** The pledge's entire value is being an established, feared institution *when the amendment vote comes* — credibility you cannot manufacture in the heat of the vote — so it belongs in the **2029–2033 build phase** beside the enabling statutes and resolution drive, another thing cheap to have ready and impossible to improvise. Two dangers: **proliferation kills bright lines** (a dozen competing reform pledges each have none of Norquist's power — this must be *the* pledge, which loops back to consolidating the ask), and **the tool outlives your intentions** (the Norquist pledge is now widely blamed for ossifying its party into anti-tax rigidity; a discipline device this effective can later constrain your own coalition on questions you'd want kept flexible). You are building a machine for making one vote unforgivable — be sure it is the right vote, because the machine won't care once it runs.

### The House unlock is floor control, not arithmetic
In an aligned majority the *floor-control* problem is solved — the Speaker who would otherwise bury the resolution is yours — and that, not the vote count, is usually the binding constraint on House reform. Finding ~50 of ~195 Republicans is a genuine lift given House discipline and primary exposure, harder per-capita than the Senate populists. But with floor access guaranteed, a clear mandate, and the populist frame, "hard but not impossible" is the honest read.

### What the aligned majority does on its own (2029–2030): building the launch pad
Even without a single Republican amendment vote, the majority can:
- **Pass the statutory layer** (filibuster reform being the real gate — none of this is reconciliation-eligible): *Freedom to Vote + John Lewis VRA* (statutory shadows of B, C, D); *DISCLOSE Act* (the disclosure regime *Citizens United* upheld 8–1 — the reachable fragment of A); *ECRA-plus* (statutory D); a *Supreme Court term-limits statute* (a deliberate vehicle whose test case builds the record for E).
- **Force record-building floor votes** on the amendments themselves — losing votes put every member on record and manufacture the accountability primary challengers later wield. (The 2014 Democracy for All Amendment drew 54 votes, essentially all Democratic — the baseline you're building up from.)
- **Fund and catalyze the state machinery**: convention-ratification **enabling statutes** (mothballed since 1933 — rebuilt *with spending limits on delegate races baked in*), the resolution drive toward the 34-application threat line and 38-state ratification base, and the next tranche of Montana-model charter measures.

### The theory of change

> **Aligned majority passes statutes → hostile Court strikes or narrows them (Strauss, on schedule) → "we enacted what 70% of Americans wanted and five Justices erased it" becomes the mandate → a galvanizing corruption scandal converts the populist holdouts → the pre-built cohort and infrastructure turn the moment into 67 votes before it dissipates → ratification via convention mode bypasses captured legislatures.**

The crucial realism: **the wave supplies the *denominator and the cohort*, never the crossovers.** The final 4–8 Senate and ~50 House votes flip when the cost of voting *no* exceeds the cost of crossing their donors — and that inflection is **scandal-driven, not persuasion-driven**. Campaign finance is the family member most likely to get there first precisely because it is the most scandal-responsive reform in American history: Watergate produced FECA (1974); the soft-money era produced McCain–Feingold (2002), *Republican-led*. You cannot schedule the scandal. You can be ready when it comes.

### First term vs. second term
- **2029–2033 (first term of a reform president):** almost certainly too early for the harvest, exactly right for the intermediate objective — converting the Republican cohort from **token to substantial** (the lone-sponsor Barrett/Katko pattern → 10–15 committed), running the statute-pass-then-Court-strikes cycle that manufactures the mandate, and pushing state applications toward the 34 that make Congress prefer to control the outcome itself.
- **2033–2037 (a second term):** the sharper scenario for the proposal actually clearing — two more cycles to build the cohort and mature the mandate, a president freed from reelection able to spend capital, and the convention-threat leverage closer to biting. If penciling a realistic congressional-proposal moment, it belongs here, with the first term as the cohort-building and scandal-readiness phase that makes this window live.

### Realism about fragility
A 54–55 Senate still puts filibuster reform at the mercy of one or two holdouts; if it survives, the statutory layer is one Court term from evisceration. **Plan for the statutes to be struck, not preserved** — their political function is to fail instructively. Build the record and the state infrastructure regardless of the statutes' fate, because those, not the statutes, survive to the harvest. And the harvest window itself opens suddenly — plausibly forced by the same climate shock that could as easily power authoritarian consolidation. The entire wager is that the repair machinery matures before the shock arrives, so the pressure routes through the legitimate channel you've reopened rather than the illegitimate ones standing wide open today.

## Part V — The 2029–2033 build-phase operating plan

The pieces of the build phase are, at this point, described in four scattered places — the pledge vehicle in Part IV, the enabling-statute drive and resolution campaign in the state machinery bullet, the charter measures in Part I, the statutory layer and record votes elsewhere. They are not four capabilities mentioned where each happens to arise; they are **one coordinated program with a single job: have every instrument mature and pre-positioned before the harvest window opens.** This part names them as that program. The bridge from strategy to operations is this: an owner, a dependency, and a metric for each workstream — and, above all, a coordination layer that makes them one effort rather than six.

### Workstream 0 — The coordination layer (build this first; it is the actual product)
Everything else fragments without it. Three deliverables, in order:
- **Consolidate the ask.** Designate **For Our Freedom** as *the* legislative vehicle; broker a radical-flank non-aggression pact with the maximalists (Move to Amend holds the Overton flank and the base energy, but stops competing in the same statehouses); settle on **one** pledge text, **one** amendment text, **one** model convention-ratification statute. A fractured ask is the first thing opponents exploit.
- **Stand up a single coordinating table** — a campaign committee that owns cross-workstream sequencing and the shared dashboard, so the six streams below reinforce rather than duplicate.
- **Adopt the Clock B dashboard** (below) as the only scoreboard that counts.

*Owner:* the coordinating table. *Metric:* one ask, one table, one dashboard in place by end of Year 1.

### Workstream 1 — Federal legislative (the majority's own power)
- *Actions:* win filibuster reform (the gate); pass the statutory layer — Freedom to Vote + John Lewis VRA, DISCLOSE, ECRA-plus, a Supreme Court term-limits statute; force record-building amendment floor votes.
- *Owner:* congressional champions + leadership. *Dependency:* filibuster reform first; nothing statutory moves without it.
- *Produces:* statutes engineered to be struck (mandate fuel) + on-record roll calls.
- *Metric:* statutes enacted; the trend line of **Republican yes-votes** on the amendment roll calls (this is the cohort forming in public).

### Workstream 2 — The bilateral pledge & primary enforcement
- *Actions:* finalize the bright-line pledge; recruit signers; publish the scorecard; land the demonstration case.
- *Owner:* **two separate vehicles** — a Democrat-facing defensive operation and a conservative-fronted offensive one — sharing pledge language and nothing else. *Dependency:* the consolidated ask (one pledge).
- *Produces:* the pre-committed crossover cohort and the self-executing deterrent.
- *Metric:* signers **among competitive candidates** (not safe incumbents padding the count); the first demonstration win.

### Workstream 3 — State charter ballot measures
- *Actions:* run the next tranche on a deliberate calendar (a purple, a red, and a blue state each cycle); harden the model text against each round of litigation; fuse every campaign to a concrete local corruption grievance.
- *Owner:* a ballot-measure campaign arm + state coalitions. *Dependency:* Montana's litigation outcome feeds the model text.
- *Produces:* policy wins, precedent, trained volunteer bases, donor files, and the "reform doesn't cause the sky to fall" factual record.
- *Metric:* measures passed **over funded opposition**; **opposition dollars drawn** (the graduation metric — when they finally spend eight figures against you, you've arrived).

### Workstream 4 — State resolutions + convention machinery
- *Actions:* climb the resolution count toward **34** (convention-threat leverage) and **38** (ratification base); and — the underrated deliverable — pass state **ratifying-convention enabling statutes**, mothballed since 1933, *with spending limits on delegate races baked in* so the money disease can't infect the cure.
- *Owner:* a state-legislative campaign arm (American Promise–style), shifting from non-binding memorials to whipped, funded coalitions and pre-positioned model resolutions.
- *Produces:* the scoreboard, the convention-threat cudgel, and the rebuilt ratification apparatus that outlives this fight and serves every anti-self-dealing amendment behind it.
- *Metric:* states on record; **states with enabling statutes on the books**; cross-partisan legislative champions recruited.

### Workstream 5 — The right-flank framing operation
- *Actions:* fund audience-segmented message testing (foreign money, anti-corruption/self-dealing, federalism — these beat "money isn't speech" with R/I voters); build out the GOP electeds and small-business/veterans/faith messenger roster; win the framing contest for anti-elite anger **before** a rival populism captures it for immigration or nihilism.
- *Owner:* the conservative-fronted vehicle (shared with WS2's Republican track). *Dependency:* must be genuinely right-fronted — a Democratic front here poisons the whole crossover strategy.
- *Produces:* the cross-partisan cover without which two-thirds is arithmetically impossible.
- *Metric:* GOP electeds publicly committed; message-test lift among Republican and independent voters.

### Workstream 6 — Litigation & mandate manufacture
- *Actions:* defend the statutes; when they're struck (they will be), convert each loss into the "five Justices erased what 70% of Americans wanted" narrative; steward the factual record the charter wins generate.
- *Owner:* a legal arm. *Dependency:* WS1's statutes and WS3's measures supply the cases.
- *Produces:* the mandate fuel that turns a courtroom defeat into political momentum.
- *Metric:* post-strike polling shift and earned media; narrative conversion rate.

### Sequencing across the window
- **Year 1 (2029):** Workstream 0 first, non-negotiable — consolidate the ask, stand up the table. In parallel: the filibuster fight, pledge and scorecard stand-up, message testing.
- **Year 2 (2030):** statutory layer passes; first charter tranche on the ballot; the demonstration primary; enabling-statute bills introduced in friendly states.
- **Years 3–4 (2031–2032):** statutes reach the Court and the mandate cycle begins; resolution count and enabling-statute count climb; the GOP cohort moves token → substantial; the convention-threat approaches leverage.
- **Handoff (2033):** every instrument mature and pre-positioned for the second-term harvest window — and, more importantly, for the unscheduled scandal trigger, which the ready machinery converts instead of squandering.

### The dashboard — Clock B, never Clock A
Stop optimizing the flattering number. **Track:** pledge signers among competitive candidates; binding measures passed over funded opposition; GOP electeds committed; states with enabling statutes; **opposition dollars drawn**; the GOP-yes trend on amendment roll calls. **Do not track as success:** non-binding resolutions passed — that is the cheap Clock A signal that lets a movement feel two-thirds done while being a fifth of the way to actual power.

### The single failure mode
If these run as six organizations with six budgets and six theories of change, you get abundant Clock A motion and no Clock B power. **The coordination layer is the product.** One ask, one table, one dashboard — or none of the rest compounds.

## Closing: the honest limits

- **The deepest malapportionment is off the table.** Article V's equal-suffrage entrenchment makes the Senate's small-state bias effectively unamendable — and it is also the single largest structural tilt against both climate action and majority rule. De-capturing the amendable channels is therefore *partial* by design; the residual bias survives even total victory.
- **The instrument is ideologically neutral.** Reviving convention ratification hands the same tool to balanced-budget and federal-power-limiting movements you'd oppose. You are sharpening a blade that cuts every direction; proceed clear-eyed.
- **Hollowing-out is the standing threat.** A hostile Court can read any empowering amendment down to minimum content — which is why the drafts above *specify* powers rather than merely gesture, and why Amendment E (a regularized Court) is the insurance on the whole family.
- **Climate sets the clock.** These reforms are how democracy earns back the capacity to face climate; climate is why democracy no longer has the luxury of not repairing itself. Each is the other's reason — and the race between the repair infrastructure maturing and the shocks arriving is the real timeline the whole strategy runs against.

*The through-line: not a grab-bag of reforms but a single interlocked argument about capacity and time — inputs, integrity, checks, and self-repair — ratified by the People directly, because the act of a citizenry successfully amending its own constitution repairs the very thing the unraveling corrodes: the felt reality that self-government is responsive rather than captured. The medium is the message.*
