## PREFATORY NOTE: FOREIGN POLICY AS DOMESTIC POLICY

Foreign policy is often treated as a separate domain from the domestic agenda — something for diplomats and generals while voters care about healthcare and groceries. This framework rejects that separation for three reasons:

**First, the cost of chaos is borne at home.** The tariff war cost American households $1,500 per year. The gutting of USAID eliminated foreign markets for American agricultural exports. The deterioration of NATO creates pressure for higher U.S. defense spending to compensate for lost alliance burden-sharing. The abandonment of multilateral trade created supply chain vulnerabilities that raised prices on American consumer goods. Foreign policy failures land in American kitchens.

**Second, peace is itself a policy.** The user's mandate for this framework explicitly includes "promote peace." That commitment runs through every chapter — immigration root causes, climate displacement and conflict, USAID as conflict prevention — but it requires explicit treatment as a foreign policy principle. American strength deployed in service of international order, democratic values, and human security is the most cost-effective peace strategy in history. The alternative — strategic withdrawal, transactional relationships, and indifference to authoritarian aggression — does not reduce American entanglement; it raises the price of eventual engagement.

**Third, democratic foreign policy requires a democratic mandate.** The Trump administration's conduct of foreign policy — ceding diplomatic leadership to Russia, withdrawing from multilateral institutions, dismantling the development infrastructure built over 80 years — was executed without a democratic mandate, without congressional authorization, and in defiance of bipartisan congressional will in many instances. Restoring accountable, lawful, and democratically anchored foreign policy is part of the democracy restoration agenda in Chapter 6.

## PART I: THE DAMAGE INVENTORY

### I.1 The Alliance System: Near-Fatal Damage

2025 saw the greatest and fastest change in United States-Russia relations since the 1917 Russian Revolution — driven by the pro-Russian and anti-Europe realignment of the Trump administration, which has badly damaged America's security and global standing while simultaneously posing an acute threat to Europe and most obviously to Ukraine.

NATO's credibility as a unified force under U.S. leadership has taken a huge hit over the past year as trust within the 32-nation military organization dissolved. Specific damage:

- Trump threatened NATO withdrawal; questioned whether the U.S. should defend Article 5 commitments; publicly expressed disappointment with the alliance
- In negotiations over Ukraine, European officials say Trump's team has positioned the U.S. as an independent arbiter rather than the leading voice of NATO — going so far as to sideline NATO allies entirely from the U.S.-Russia negotiation process
- Trump threatened to seize Greenland, a semiautonomous territory of NATO ally Denmark; threatened to expel Spain from NATO for insufficient defense spending; made false claims about NATO allies' conduct in Afghanistan
- In October 2025, NATO learned that up to 1,500 American troops would be withdrawn from an area bordering Ukraine, angering Romania
- The "kill switch" panic: European partners worried that U.S. software dependencies in F-35s and other jointly-operated weapons systems could be used as leverage — the Pentagon was forced to publicly deny such a mechanism existed
- The whispered but increasingly believed calculus among European security analysts: being an American ally is becoming more dangerous than being America's enemy

**The European response:** Facing U.S. unreliability, European allies made unprecedented defense commitments. Trump's pressure ultimately convinced NATO members (excluding Spain) to pledge 5% of GDP for defense, split between 3.5% on core defense and 1.5% on security-related infrastructure — and the EU launched its own $843 billion rearmament package. Europe is building strategic autonomy not because it wants to, but because it can no longer rely on the United States. This decoupling, once established, will take a decade to reverse even under a cooperative administration.

**The emerging calculus that must be reversed:** Trump doesn't consider America's alliance system an asset; he perceives it as a liability. While some envy Washington for its alliances, Trump envies China for being unconstrained by allies. This inversion — alliances as burden rather than force multiplier — is precisely backward. America's alliance network is its single greatest strategic asymmetric advantage over China and Russia, both of which have few genuine allies. Rebuilding it is not charity toward Europe. It is the highest-ROI national security investment available.

### I.2 Ukraine: A Peace Process Built on Russian Terms

The Trump administration chose to recognize Putin as a friend and equal, giving Russia a huge diplomatic boost via a hurried summit in Alaska, contrasting strikingly with the attempt to humiliate Zelensky in the White House. The White House focused on two goals: a close economic relationship with Russia and pushing allies to normalize relations with Moscow, including the readmission of Russia to the G8 and what its 28-point peace plan described as the "reintegration" of Russia into the global economy — in other words, the lifting of sanctions.

The November 2025 28-point peace plan: The Trump administration's 28-point plan for peace in Ukraine does not create a path out of the war; it repackages the Kremlin's long-standing demands and presents them as a diplomatic breakthrough. It demands that Ukraine surrender territory, accept limits on its armed forces, and curb Western military assistance — punishing the victim and shielding the aggressor from any cost.

The state of play as of March 2026:
- August 2025 talks centered on whether negotiations should seek an interim ceasefire prior to a comprehensive settlement; Putin proposed a ceasefire along the line of contact if Ukraine transferred additional Donetsk territory — a proposal Ukraine rejected
- U.S. military assistance has shifted from direct grants to commercial arms sales funneled through NATO allies — a significant reduction in support
- Ukrainian public opinion remains opposed to major territorial concessions; Ukrainian resilience and opposition to wholesale territorial concessions demanded by the Kremlin will persist barring rapid and sustained Russian advances threatening major cities
- Ceasefire negotiations are ongoing but no settlement has been reached as of March 2026

**What has NOT happened (important constraints on the 2029 situation):**
- U.S. pre-2025 sanctions on Russia have not been lifted as of March 2026
- Ukraine has not been forced to accept a peace agreement against its will
- NATO has not formally changed its open-door policy, though the 28-point plan's demand that it do so remains on the table
- Ukraine's military capacity, while strained, has not collapsed

### I.3 USAID: A Humanitarian Catastrophe in Real Time

The dismantling of USAID is the most lethal single policy action of the Trump administration — more lethal than any military operation, yet conducted with almost no public debate.

The numbers, carefully sourced and properly characterized:

The Center for Global Development calculated that USAID program cancellations announced by Secretary Rubio could contribute to as many as 500,000 to 700,000 additional deaths annually. An updated analysis using financial data to end of FY2025 suggests lives lost based on the decline in outlays may be in the range of 500,000 to 1,000,000.

A peer-reviewed Lancet study projects that if USAID cuts continue, more than 14 million additional deaths could occur by 2030, including over 4.5 million among children under five — or about 700,000 extra child deaths per year.

At least 23 million children stand to lose access to education, and as many as 95 million people would lose access to basic healthcare.

More than 176,000 additional adults and children could die from HIV, and at least 62,000 additional people from tuberculosis, if foreign aid is not restored.

**Congress has partially responded:** On February 3, 2026, Congress passed a bill allocating $50 billion for foreign aid — a vital first step reasserting Congressional authority, but only a fraction of what was removed. The U.S. spent $32 billion on foreign aid in 2025 versus $68 billion in 2024. The $50 billion allocation begins to address the gap, but the institutional infrastructure — the staff, the contracts, the NGO partners, the in-country relationships — takes years to rebuild once dismantled.

As of April 2025, 81 NGOs had closed at least one office. World Vision, the world's largest evangelical humanitarian organization, lost 10% of its budget, laid off as many as 3,000 employees, and cut programs in Bangladesh, Kenya, and Rwanda. These organizations cannot be reconstituted quickly. The human capital, the field presence, the institutional knowledge — all have been dispersed.

**The cascading effect:** The U.S. previously contributed over 40% of global humanitarian funding; other donors may also reduce their commitments, further weakening service delivery in countries already dependent on external support — and the OECD estimates an overall decline in overseas development assistance in 2025 of 9-17%.

The result: China has adapted by deepening relationships with Europe, the Global South, and Russia; the U.S. has weakened itself by alienating the allies it needs to manage China effectively.

## PART II: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

### II.1 The Core Principles

**American strength is alliance strength.** The U.S. alone, regardless of military spending, cannot manage China, deter Russia, stop pandemics, address climate displacement, or govern a rules-based global economy. These challenges require the coordinated action of democracies. America's 80-year investment in building an alliance of democracies is its greatest strategic asset. Every dollar spent maintaining it is cheaper than the military spending required to compensate for its loss.

**Rules-based order serves American interests.** The international system of institutions, treaties, and norms built after World War II — the UN, WHO, WTO, NATO, the Bretton Woods financial institutions — was designed by American statesmen and reflects American values and interests. It is not a constraint on American power; it is an amplifier of it. Withdrawing from it does not make America freer; it makes the world more dangerous and American engagement more expensive.

**Development is security.** USAID-funded programs helped save 91 million lives over the past 20 years — that is also 91 million people who did not become refugees, did not join extremist movements born of desperation, and did not require expensive military interventions. Foreign aid is not charity. It is the cheapest form of conflict prevention available. Rebuilding it is a national security imperative.

**Peace requires strength, fairness, and patience.** Democrats do not believe in weakness, appeasement, or naïve multilateralism. They believe in a strong military, a credible deterrent, and a diplomatic framework that manages competition while avoiding catastrophic conflict. The choice is not between strength and diplomacy — it is between strength used wisely through alliances and diplomacy, and strength wasted through unilateral action and strategic incoherence.

**Ukraine matters beyond Ukraine.** If Russia's invasion of a sovereign democracy is rewarded with territorial gains and security concessions, every authoritarian government in the world draws the lesson that borders can be changed by force and that Western resolve is for sale. The cost of supporting Ukrainian sovereignty is far lower than the cost of the precedent set by abandoning it.

### II.2 The "Rebuild and Reform" Principle

The Project 2029 foreign policy agenda is not simply to reverse Trump — it is to rebuild better. Several elements of the pre-Trump international architecture had genuine problems: USAID had inefficiency and insufficient coordination with recipient countries; the WTO's dispute resolution mechanism was slow and inadequate for addressing China's state capitalism; NATO burden-sharing was genuinely imbalanced; the aid system had dependencies that undermined local ownership. The Democratic restoration agenda acknowledges these critiques and builds reforms into the rebuilding.

### IV.2 Ukraine Policy

#### The Guiding Principles

1. **Ukrainian self-determination is non-negotiable.** Ukraine decides what peace terms it can accept — not Washington, not Moscow, and not a bilateral U.S.-Russia deal constructed over Ukraine's head
2. **Russia must not be rewarded for aggression.** Any settlement that formalizes territorial gains achieved through force sets a precedent that makes the next war more likely
3. **Support Ukraine's capacity to defend itself.** The strongest negotiating position for Ukraine at any peace table is military capacity sufficient to make continued Russian offense costly
4. **Europe must lead the security guarantee.** European-led security guarantees for Ukraine, backed by U.S. support, are more durable than U.S.-only commitments subject to electoral reversal

#### Day 1 Executive Actions

- Restore full U.S. military assistance to Ukraine: end the commercial-only arms sales model; restore direct FMF (Foreign Military Financing) grants; release all congressionally-appropriated Ukraine aid frozen by Trump executive action
- Restore Ukraine's access to U.S. intelligence sharing at pre-2025 levels
- Reject any peace plan that: (a) requires Ukraine to cede additional territory beyond what was already occupied on Feb 24, 2022; (b) prohibits Ukraine from NATO membership permanently by statute; (c) lifts Russia sanctions without verifiable compliance with agreed terms

#### Legislative / Diplomatic Agenda

- Ukraine Security Commitment Act: Provide a long-term U.S. security commitment to Ukraine — not Article 5 (which requires NATO membership), but a bilateral defense commitment similar to Israel's that includes military assistance, intelligence sharing, and a statement that the U.S. will impose severe consequences for any resumed large-scale Russian military action
- Sanction enforcement: Maintain and strengthen existing Russia sanctions; close the enforcement gaps that have allowed Russian oligarchs and institutions to circumvent them through third-country financial systems
- Reconstruction support: U.S. participation in the Ukraine reconstruction fund, with conditions requiring democratic governance, anti-corruption measures, and European co-leadership — reject the Trump administration's demand for 50% of reconstruction profits to flow to the U.S. Treasury as mercenary and counterproductive
- Negotiate with European allies on long-term security architecture for Ukraine that provides meaningful deterrence without committing to permanent war

#### The Honest Assessment of What 2029 Inherits

The Ukraine situation in January 2029 will be shaped by what happens in 2026-2028 — which is genuinely uncertain. The governance plan must be flexible:

- **If a ceasefire has been reached:** The 2029 agenda focuses on reconstruction support, security guarantee negotiations, sanction enforcement, and preventing Russian normalization without compliance verification
- **If fighting continues:** The agenda focuses on restoring and sustaining military assistance, maintaining European alliance cohesion, and preventing a forced settlement
- **If a settlement has been imposed against Ukraine's will:** The agenda focuses on making clear the settlement lacks U.S. legitimacy, maintaining sanctions until Russia's conduct justifies relief, and supporting Ukrainian resilience

In all scenarios, the core principle is the same: the United States does not validate territorial conquest by force, and does not participate in the rehabilitation of an aggressor that has not paid a meaningful price for its aggression.

### IV.3 USAID Reconstruction — The Global Health and Development Restoration Act

USAID cannot simply be turned back on. The institutional infrastructure has been dismantled: staff dispersed, contracts cancelled, NGO partners forced to lay off staff, field offices closed, country-level programs terminated. The $50 billion Congressional appropriation in February 2026 begins to address funding; the 2029 administration inherits the implementation challenge of spending it effectively without the institution that used to do so.

**The Restoration Architecture:**

**Year 1 (Immediate):**
- Reconstitute USAID as a Cabinet-level agency with a Senate-confirmed Administrator; restore its independent budget and personnel authority
- Emergency hiring: USAID lost more than two-thirds of its personnel. Restore 5,000 positions in Year 1; target full staffing of 10,000+ within 3 years
- Re-establish field presence in the 50+ countries where USAID programs operated; re-contract with implementing partners where they still exist
- Restore PEPFAR to full funding and scope: the President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief is the most cost-effective global health program in U.S. history — more than 176,000 additional people could die from HIV if PEPFAR is not restored; restore it completely and with a 5-year funding commitment to restore partner confidence
- Restore food aid programs: Food for Peace, WFP contributions, regional food security programs
- Restore tuberculosis, malaria, and maternal/child health programs through PEPFAR, PMI, and bilateral health programs

**Funding Target:**
- Restore U.S. foreign aid to $65-70B annually by Year 3 (approximately 1% of GDP, consistent with historical U.S. commitment and the UN's longstanding 0.7% GNI target for developed nations)
- Maintain and expand the $50B Congressional appropriation; seek bipartisan support by framing aid as national security investment, not charity

**Reform Architecture (Rebuilding Better):**
- Shift from purely project-based aid toward budget support and systems strengthening for countries with strong governance; reduce overhead burden on recipient partners
- Increase local procurement and local partner engagement: less Washington-based NGO contracting, more direct support for civil society organizations in recipient countries
- Strengthen anti-corruption conditionality: U.S. aid should reinforce good governance, not enable corruption; build Millennium Challenge Corporation model into more programs
- Restore and strengthen the Millennium Challenge Corporation as a model of transparent, results-oriented, bilateral development assistance
- Coordinate with multilateral development banks (World Bank, IDB, African Development Bank) to leverage U.S. bilateral aid with multilateral resources

**USAID and Peace:**
- The single most important long-term impact of USAID is conflict prevention — development assistance reduces the poverty, state fragility, and social tension that breed violence and eventually require military intervention
- Program priorities for conflict prevention: food security (starvation is a conflict driver); democracy and governance support (weak institutions breed conflict); countering violent extremism (address root causes, not just symptoms); women's empowerment (societies with greater gender equality have dramatically lower rates of political violence)

### IV.4 China Strategy

China policy requires the most careful calibration in this framework — because Democrats must simultaneously: (1) be credibly tough on Chinese economic and security threats; (2) avoid the economic self-harm of the Trump tariff approach; (3) maintain the multilateral coordination that actually constrains China; and (4) prevent military escalation that would be catastrophic for both countries and the world.

**The Strategic Framework: Competitive Coexistence with Allied Coordination**

The U.S.-China relationship is not a cold war that requires total decoupling. China is simultaneously the U.S.'s largest trading partner (in many categories), the primary source of climate solution components, and the primary strategic competitor. A policy that treats every dimension of the relationship as adversarial will fail — and will harm American consumers, workers, and military readiness more than it harms China.

The correct framework is: **compete fiercely in technology, clean energy, and strategic industries; cooperate where interests converge (climate, pandemic preparedness, nuclear stability); confront clearly on security threats, human rights abuses, and unfair economic practices; and do all of this through allied coordination rather than unilateral action.**

**Economic Dimensions:**
- Eliminate blanket consumer goods tariffs that harm American households; maintain targeted strategic tariffs on specific sectors where Chinese state subsidies and intellectual property theft are documented
- Strengthen and coordinate technology export controls with allied nations — the Biden-era chip restrictions are the model; expand to other strategic technologies (quantum computing, AI chips, advanced biotech)
- Use the Democratic Trade Alliance (Chapter 7) to build common standards that effectively exclude Chinese products that don't meet labor, environmental, and IP protection requirements — this is more effective than unilateral tariffs and creates no domestic consumer cost
- Supply chain diversification: fund allied-country investment in critical mineral processing, battery manufacturing, and solar component production to reduce dependence on Chinese supply chains — the IRA's domestic content requirements are the domestic component; allied manufacturing investment is the international component

**Security Dimensions:**
- Taiwan: Maintain strategic ambiguity; strengthen deterrence through arms sales, military presence in the Indo-Pacific, and clear communication that unilateral military action would face severe economic and diplomatic consequences; pursue confidence-building measures to reduce risk of miscalculation
- South China Sea: Assert freedom of navigation; work with regional allies (Philippines, Vietnam, Japan) to resist Chinese territorial claims; pursue arbitration through UNCLOS frameworks
- Military posture: Maintain a strong Indo-Pacific military presence; prioritize naval, air, space, and cyber capabilities relevant to the China challenge; invest in resilient logistics and distributed basing that reduces vulnerability to Chinese anti-access capabilities
- Avoid a new arms race that China cannot afford and will not be deterred by: the U.S. defense advantage is best maintained through technological superiority and allied burden-sharing, not through spending that hollows out domestic investment

**Cooperation Dimensions:**
- Climate: China and the U.S. together represent approximately 40% of global greenhouse gas emissions; climate action is impossible without U.S.-China cooperation at some level. Restore the diplomatic climate dialogue; pursue joint methane reduction commitments; cooperate on clean energy technology standards even while competing in clean energy markets
- Pandemic preparedness: Restore U.S.-China scientific exchanges and WHO cooperation on disease surveillance; the COVID pandemic demonstrated that pathogen surveillance requires global cooperation regardless of political tensions
- Nuclear stability: Open strategic stability talks; establish communications channels to prevent nuclear miscalculation; pursue arms control framework that includes China along with U.S. and Russia for the first time

### IV.5 Iran and Middle East

The Iran situation as of March 2026 involves the aftermath of U.S.-Israel military strikes on Iranian nuclear and military facilities in late 2025. Iranian representatives have reached out to reopen diplomatic channels. The governance agenda:

**Iran:**
- Pursue a diplomatic framework to prevent Iranian nuclear weapons development — the JCPOA or its successor remains the best available mechanism; military strikes can delay but not permanently prevent Iranian nuclear capability
- Open diplomatic back-channel through Switzerland or Oman (the traditional intermediary); conditional on Iran returning to IAEA monitoring and capping enrichment below weapons-grade
- Coordinate with allies on sanctions framework: maintain pressure where Iran is non-compliant; provide sanctions relief where compliance is verified
- Support Iranian civil society and democratic movements without government-to-government engagement that delegitimizes opposition

**Israel-Palestine:**
- The U.S. has historically been the indispensable party in any Israeli-Palestinian peace framework; the Trump administration's approach — unconditional support for Israeli government decisions regardless of international law compliance — has removed U.S. credibility as a mediator

- Resume humanitarian aid to Gaza: restore UNRWA funding; support humanitarian access; press for release of hostages; press for end to civilian casualties
- Resume engagement with regional partners (Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Egypt, UAE) on normalization architecture that requires Palestinian political progress as a component

**Broader Middle East:**
- Restore relationships with Gulf partners (Saudi Arabia, UAE) damaged by Trump's erratic engagement and transactional framing
- Restore engagement with Turkey on shared NATO interests while pressing on democratic backsliding
- Support the Abraham Accords framework while insisting that normalization cannot substitute for a Palestinian political pathway

### IV.7 Defense Policy and Pentagon Reform

**The Core Principle:** The U.S. must maintain sufficient military strength to deter great-power conflict, protect allies, and respond to terrorist threats — while reforming the defense acquisition and management system that wastes hundreds of billions of dollars annually and produces results that often fail to match the investment.

**Defense Budget Framework:**
- Maintain defense spending at approximately 3-3.5% of GDP — sufficient for the global mission; resist pressure to either slash it (which would hollow out deterrence) or dramatically increase it (which would crowd out domestic investment without proportional security gain)
- The defense budget is not a jobs program; procurement decisions should be made on strategic grounds, not based on which congressional districts have factories
- Support the troops, not just the contractors: pay, housing, healthcare, and family support for service members have chronically lagged weapons procurement; rebalance

**Pentagon Audit and Reform:**
- The Pentagon has failed its audit for seven consecutive years — the only major federal agency that cannot account for its own spending. This is not a Democratic talking point; it is an Inspector General finding. Pass the Department of Defense Audit Reform Act: require a clean audit opinion within 4 years; link senior civilian and military leader performance reviews to audit progress
- Reform the acquisition system: major weapons programs routinely run 50-100% over budget and years late. Adopt the rapid acquisition and prototype-first approach used successfully in Space Force and special operations procurement
- Address the revolving door: the defense contractor-to-Pentagon revolving door is the military-industrial complex in operation. Strengthen post-employment restrictions; require senior officials to recuse from decisions affecting their former employers for the duration of their government service

**Nuclear Posture:**
- Maintain a credible nuclear deterrent; modernize aging delivery systems (the triad of land-based missiles, submarine missiles, and bomber aircraft) on a realistic timeline
- Resist pressure to add new tactical nuclear weapons or lower the threshold for nuclear use — the deterrence value of nuclear weapons comes from the clarity that their use would be catastrophic, not from threatening to use them in regional conflicts
- Lead a global nuclear risk reduction dialogue: the risk of nuclear war by miscalculation or technical failure is higher than at any point since the Cuban Missile Crisis; this requires diplomatic engagement regardless of political tensions

**Cybersecurity and Emerging Technology:**
- Establish a National Cybersecurity Authority with cross-agency authority to defend critical infrastructure, government systems, and election infrastructure
- Maintain U.S. leadership in AI military applications while pursuing international governance frameworks to prevent an AI arms race
- Invest in space domain awareness and resilience: U.S. military dependence on satellite infrastructure is a strategic vulnerability; harden and diversify

## PART VI: THE POLITICAL STRATEGY

### VI.1 Foreign Policy as Domestic Competence

Foreign policy is a political winner for Democrats when framed as competence, strength, and fiscal prudence — not as idealism. The frames that work:

- "Strong alliances mean other countries share the cost of defense" (burden-sharing argument)
- "USAID is cheaper than war" (fiscal argument)
- "Chaos abroad raises prices at home" (economic argument)
- "A strong America leads through respect, not threats" (strength argument)

The frames that lose: any framing that sounds like America sacrificing for the world, or that prioritizes foreign interests over domestic ones.

### VI.2 The Veterans and Military Family Coalition

Military families are a natural audience for the alliance restoration and Ukraine support agenda:
- Service members understand that alliances reduce their individual operational burden
- Military families understand that unnecessary wars have costs they pay directly
- Veterans understand that a strong, well-funded military with good diplomatic backing is better than an overstretched military with no allies
- The USAID/global health framing resonates with military chaplains, international health-focused veterans, and the large number of veterans who have served in humanitarian operations

### VI.3 The Bipartisan Opening

Foreign policy has the most genuine bipartisan potential of any domain in this framework:
- NATO support is bipartisan among the vast majority of senators and House members who are not in the Trumpist faction
- Ukraine support had bipartisan congressional backing; the Senate passed Ukraine aid twice with Republican votes before Trump blocked it
- PEPFAR is a Republican program (created by George W. Bush); restoration has evangelical Christian and conservative humanitarian support
- China competition framing is broadly bipartisan
- Defense reform (audit, acquisition) has conservative fiscal hawk support

The strategy: lead with the areas of bipartisan agreement to demonstrate the new administration's commitment to working across the aisle; use these wins to build relationships that make the harder votes more achievable.

This $450B over 10 years is not in the Chapter 2 fiscal framework, which allocated only $100B for "USAID/foreign aid restoration." The revised total is approximately 4.5x that allocation. **This is a meaningful gap that the audit process should flag.**

**How to close it:**
1. The Pentagon audit savings ($50B+) offset a significant portion
2. Foreign aid is approximately 1% of the federal budget; at $65B/year it is still below the UN target of 0.7% of GNI; this is defensible fiscal prioritization, not extravagance
3. The defense budget trade: shift from the most wasteful defense acquisition programs to foreign aid and alliance maintenance — the ROI is far higher
4. Frame honestly: restoring U.S. global leadership requires real investment; it is cheaper than the military spending required to compensate for lost alliances and the interventions required to address crises that development assistance would have prevented
