## PART I: THE BRUTAL POLITICAL DIAGNOSIS

### I.1 Where Democrats Actually Stand

No honest political analysis can begin the immigration chapter anywhere but here: **immigration is where Democratic credibility with persuadable voters is most damaged, and where the gap between the party's instincts and effective politics has been most costly.**

Third Way's battleground polling from May 2025 found that Democrats started with a **30-point deficit** on immigration trust compared to Republicans — rising to a **41-point deficit specifically on border security**, making it the single largest trust gap of any tested issue, three times bigger than the gap on inflation, four times bigger than on the economy. A Way to Win/Valiente Action Fund poll of key 2026 Congressional district voters found Congressional Democrats are a **net-58 point negative** on how they're handling immigration.

These numbers are not a messaging failure. They are a consequence of years in which Democrats:
- Reflexively defended any immigration enforcement as cruel or racist
- Failed to articulate a coherent enforcement philosophy that voters could understand
- Allowed a genuine border management crisis (2021-2023 record crossings) to fester without a clear legislative response
- Were seen as more comfortable advocating for undocumented immigrants than for the law-abiding citizens in border communities
- Let Republicans define "border security" as entirely synonymous with cruelty and exclusion — without offering a competing definition

**The good news, as of March 2026**: The political terrain has shifted materially in Democrats' favor. Trump's mass deportation agenda — highly visible, often cruel, economically destructive, and targeting people with deep community roots — has moved public opinion faster than almost any issue in recent polling history. Democrats now hold the winning position on immigration if they can articulate it with clarity and conviction. The question is whether the party is disciplined enough to do so.

### I.2 The Opportunity: What the Polls Actually Say Now

The following findings, from reputable pollsters between mid-2025 and early 2026, define the terrain Democrats must occupy:

**On pathways to citizenship vs. mass deportation:**
- By a 59-29% margin, Fox News found Americans prefer a path to citizenship over mass deportation (July 2025)
- Gallup (July 2025): 78% of Americans support citizenship for undocumented immigrants, including majorities of all parties; 85% support Dreamers' citizenship, including 71% of Republicans
- 65% of Americans support providing undocumented immigrants currently working in the U.S. with a pathway to citizenship (Chicago Council, July 2025)
- Support for a pathway to citizenship increased from 56% to 60% between 2024 and 2025, with the sharpest rise among independents (PRRI, December 2025)

**On current enforcement tactics:**
- 62% of Americans say current ICE efforts go "too far," up from 58% the week prior; 65% of independents and 92% of Democrats agree (Ipsos, February 2026)
- Voters 56-39% disapprove of the way ICE is doing its job; independents disapprove 64-32% (Quinnipiac, June 2025)

**On what voters want (across party lines):**
- Majorities in every swing state prefer offering undocumented immigrants a path to citizenship over mass deportation; majorities also favor strengthening the border and E-Verify to deter illegal crossings; majorities favor increasing work visas to meet labor demand through legal channels
- 85% of voters agree on restoring order at the border through more agents, immigration judges, and technology; 75% agree on a pathway to citizenship for hardworking long-term residents

**On desire for immigration overall:**
- The percentage of Americans who say immigration should be reduced dropped from 55% in 2024 to 30% by mid-2025 — back to 2021 levels — as illegal crossings dropped sharply under Trump enforcement

**The core insight from this data**: The supermajority position in American politics is *not* mass deportation and *not* open borders. It is: **secure and orderly legal processes; a pathway to legal status for established community members; an end to performative cruelty; and a functioning immigration system that works for families, employers, and law enforcement alike.** That is precisely the position this framework advocates — and it is where Democrats can and must plant their flag.

### I.3 The Trump Record: A Damage Inventory

Before building the Democratic alternative, the scope of what has been done must be documented clearly. This is the contrast material for campaigns and the governing agenda for what must be reversed.

**Scale and Speed of Enforcement**
- The OBBBA provided $170.1 billion in new spending for immigration enforcement, making ICE the highest-funded federal law enforcement agency in history — funded at a level higher than some foreign militaries
- More than 290,000 people were detained from January through mid-October 2025, a 19% increase over the same period the year prior; 2025 holds the distinction of having the most people die in ICE custody ever
- ICE detention rose nearly 75% in 2025, from roughly 40,000 at the start of the year to 66,000 by December — the highest level ever recorded; over 100 additional facilities were opened including tent camps with brutal conditions
- By November 2025, for every person released from ICE detention, more than fourteen were deported directly from custody, compared to approximately a one-to-two ratio a year earlier

**Due Process Dismantled**
- People fulfilling their legal obligations by attending immigration hearings and mandated check-ins with ICE are being targeted for arrest — punishing people for complying with the law and sending a dangerous message that participation in the legal system carries the risk of detention
- The administration stripped visa holders of legal status for political speech, revoked Temporary Protected Status from migrants from certain countries, reduced refugee admissions to historic lows, halted all asylum decisions in November 2025, and has planned to denaturalize certain U.S. citizens at a quota of up to 200 per month in 2026
- The administration sought to eliminate constitutionally protected birthright citizenship and sent people to a notorious Salvadoran prison and other third countries where immigrants have no ties

**Economic Damage**
- Mass deportations are forecast to reduce the nation's GDP by more than 7% in the next three years
- Peterson Institute estimates: deporting 8.3 million undocumented immigrants would reduce GDP 7.4% and employment 7.0% by 2028 — comparable to the Great Recession
- For every half-million immigrants removed from the labor force, 44,000 U.S.-born workers would also lose their jobs
- Between May 2025 and January 2026, states with heavy DHS enforcement presence experienced a 1.8% decline in private-sector employment while the rest of the U.S. saw a 1.1% increase — a decline researchers called "highly unusual or unprecedented"
- In Chicago's Latino business corridors, small business owners reported year-over-year revenue drops ranging from 20% to 50%; in Portland-area Latino businesses, some reported drops as much as half, with owners saying it was "worse than during the pandemic"

**Community Terror**
- Mass deportations cause dramatic mental and physical health impacts on families and communities; U.S. citizens, particularly children, are experiencing greater anxiety, depression, and psychological distress from having loved ones detained or deported
- Immigrants avoid medical care out of fear of enforcement, resulting in worse health outcomes and higher costs for everyone — including increased burden on already-strained rural hospitals
- Even immigrants with lawful status and U.S. citizens live under the shadow of weaponized enforcement, and social scientists document increases in vigilantism and hate crimes as a result of a massive, visible deportation operation

**Legal Immigration Gutted Too**
- The administration paused all final asylum adjudications in November 2025
- Expanded travel bans to nearly 40 countries; paused green card and citizenship processing from 19 "countries of concern"
- Halted all Diversity Visa issuance
- Terminated automatic employment authorization extensions, creating work gaps for legal workers
- Effectively eliminated refugee admissions except for a small group of white South Africans

## PART II: THE GOVERNING PHILOSOPHY

### II.1 What Democrats Believe — Stated Plainly

The Democratic position on immigration should be stated without evasion, hedging, or apology:

**We believe the current immigration system is broken in two directions simultaneously**: it is too hard to enter legally, and it has been managed in ways that create unauthorized populations and then punish them for it. The answer is to fix the legal pathways so that people who want to come here and contribute can do so through legitimate channels — and to treat those already here with dignity and due process.

**We believe in immigration enforcement** — targeted, lawful, prioritized around public safety, and conducted by trained professionals operating under constitutional constraints. "We enforce immigration law" is not a Republican position. It is the law of the land. Democrats support enforcing it fairly.

**We do not believe in performative cruelty.** Terrorizing families, arresting people at their own mandatory check-ins, sending people to foreign prisons without due process, detaining nursing mothers and university students — these are not immigration enforcement. They are authoritarian theater that makes us less safe, destroys communities, costs hundreds of billions of dollars, and damages the economy.

**We believe in the economic truth about immigration.** Immigrants built this country. They continue to build it. In an era of an aging workforce and declining birth rates, immigration is not a threat to American prosperity — it is a necessity for sustaining it. The labor force in agriculture, construction, healthcare, and hospitality depends on immigrant workers. Any honest economic policy must reckon with that.

**We believe in the rule of law** — including constitutional protections for due process, equal protection, and birthright citizenship. These are not negotiable.

### II.2 The Three-Part Framework

The Democratic immigration vision rests on three mutually reinforcing pillars, each of which is popular with supermajorities of Americans:

**Pillar One: SECURE** — A real, smart, well-funded border management system that processes people efficiently, deters illegal crossings, and stops criminal trafficking

**Pillar Two: FAIR** — A legal immigration system that works — for families, workers, employers, and asylum seekers; with backlogs cleared and pathways that match modern economic reality

**Pillar THREE: HUMANE** — Treatment of immigrants that reflects American values: due process, constitutional rights, no family separation, no private profit detention camps, no deportation to death or torture

These three pillars cannot be separated. Republicans want to talk only about Pillar One, and only on their terms (maximum cruelty as deterrent). Democrats have historically been perceived as focused only on Pillar Three, and only defensively. The winning position — empirically validated by the polling — pairs all three as a unified vision. **An orderly, lawful, fair immigration system is simultaneously more secure AND more humane than the current chaos.**

## PART IV: THE LEGISLATIVE AGENDA

### IV.1 The Comprehensive Immigration Reform Act of 2029

The centerpiece of the governance agenda is a single, comprehensive immigration reform bill passed in the first legislative year. It combines border management, legal system reform, and a pathway to legal status for established residents. It is modeled on the bipartisan framework of the Dignity Act of 2025 and the Senate's 2013 Gang of Eight bill — but strengthened and updated to reflect the 2025-2026 damage.

**Why comprehensive over piecemeal?**

The history of piecemeal immigration legislation is a history of failure. Passing DACA protection alone — without fixing the underlying system — leaves the core dysfunction intact. Passing border security alone — without legal pathway reform — makes illegal crossings more likely, not less, because people cannot wait 100 years for a legal visa. A comprehensive package forces a genuine exchange: real enforcement investment paired with real legal reform. This is also the only path to sustainable political durability: a deal that each coalition member finds acceptable is harder to reverse than a series of one-sided wins.

#### Title I: Border and Enforcement Reform

**Sec. 1.1 — Smart Border Investment**
- $30 billion over 5 years for border technology: advanced sensors, aerial surveillance, inspection equipment for ports of entry, fentanyl detection technology
- Hire 8,500 additional U.S. Customs and Border Protection officers (focus on ports of entry where 90%+ of fentanyl enters)
- Hire 5,000 additional immigration judges to eliminate the court backlog (currently 3+ million cases pending)
- Hire 4,500 additional immigration attorneys for immigration courts
- Restore and modernize CBP One app functionality for port-of-entry scheduling
- Invest in biometric and digital processing infrastructure to dramatically speed case adjudication

**Sec. 1.2 — Enforcement Priorities and Accountability**
- Codify in statute that immigration enforcement priority must be given to: (1) national security threats; (2) people with violent criminal convictions; (3) recent entrants without established community ties
- Prohibit enforcement at sensitive locations: schools, hospitals, churches, courthouses, immigration hearings
- Prohibit arrests at immigration check-ins for people with pending cases in compliance with their reporting requirements
- Require body cameras for all ICE interior enforcement operations; mandatory reporting and public disclosure
- Restore independent Inspector General oversight of DHS/ICE; Congressional oversight access to detention facilities
- Prohibit use of U.S. military in interior immigration enforcement (reaffirm Posse Comitatus in immigration context)
- Establish independent accountability board for use-of-force complaints against CBP/ICE

**Sec. 1.3 — Asylum Reform**
- Establish in-country pre-screening facilities in Colombia, Guatemala, Ecuador, and other transit countries, so asylum seekers can receive initial screening before reaching the U.S. border (reduces dangerous smuggling routes; models Canada's approach)
- Fund 200-day case adjudication standard at border: adequate judges, attorneys, and facilities to resolve cases within 200 days for most applicants
- Restore credible fear standard to its legally correct interpretation; reverse Trump-era guidance gutting asylum protections for domestic violence and gang violence survivors
- Create dedicated fast-track court for meritless cases (speed deportations of people with no valid claim)
- Restore and fund legal representation for asylum seekers in immigration court (represented cases resolve 5x faster)
- Restore refugee admissions to 125,000/year; establish dedicated refugee processing partnerships with UNHCR

**Sec. 1.4 — Employer Accountability**
- Mandatory E-Verify for all employers with 20+ employees (phased in 24 months)
- Significantly increased civil and criminal penalties for employers knowingly hiring unauthorized workers
- End the exploitation dynamic: employers who hire undocumented workers at below-market wages while using their vulnerability as leverage are a root cause of the unauthorized population; accountability ends that dynamic
- Note: E-Verify is politically viable *only* when paired with legal pathways that make it possible to hire workers legally — the comprehensive nature of this legislation is what makes this provision workable

#### Title II: Legal Immigration System Reform

**Sec. 2.1 — Backlog Elimination**
- $3.6 billion for USCIS, State Department, and Department of Labor to hire staff and upgrade technology specifically for backlog reduction
- Raise per-country caps from 7% to 15% for both employment-based and family-based categories
- Exempt spouses and minor children from employment-based visa numerical caps (they should not count against the limits meant for workers)
- Establish a 10-year maximum wait standard: any pending approved petition waiting 10+ years will be processed through expedited track by 2035
- Goal: eliminate the decades-long backlogs (current backlog includes people waiting over 100 years for a visa from Mexico through family preference categories)

**Sec. 2.2 — Work Visa Modernization**
- Create a new flexible non-immigrant work visa category for industries with documented shortages (agriculture year-round, construction, healthcare, hospitality, education) — tied to actual labor market data, updated annually
- Reform H-2A agricultural worker program: allow year-round status (current program is seasonal only, creating structural incentives for illegal employment); include robust worker protections and portability between employers
- Reform H-1B high-skilled worker program: end employer lock-in that creates exploitation vulnerability; improve portability; address documented abuse by body shops
- Expand EB-2/EB-3 caps for healthcare workers (nurses, therapists, care workers) given projected shortfalls
- Create a startup visa for immigrant entrepreneurs who meet funding/job creation thresholds
- Permanently authorize Conrad 30 waiver (physicians trained in U.S. who serve underserved communities)

**Sec. 2.3 — Family Reunification Modernization**
- Reduce family reunification backlog by expanding numerical limits for immediate family categories
- Add same-sex spouses and families to all family visa provisions explicitly
- Create a humanitarian "family hardship" parole mechanism for cases where U.S. citizen children would face severe hardship from a parent's removal
- Address "Documented Dreamers" — children of long-term work visa holders who have grown up in the U.S. and face aging out of status at 21: grant them path to permanent residency

**Sec. 2.4 — Student and Academic Visas**
- Allow F-1 students graduating from U.S. universities with STEM degrees to apply for permanent residency upon graduation (fix "brain drain" that sends U.S.-educated talent abroad)
- Implement dual-intent for student visas (currently, students cannot signal intent to stay permanently, creating legal fiction and bureaucratic absurdity)
- Restore and expand Optional Practical Training (OPT) for STEM graduates

#### Title III: Pathway to Legal Status for Established Residents

This is the most consequential — and contested — part of comprehensive reform. It must be designed to be durable politically and effective legally.

**Sec. 3.1 — Pathway to Legal Status (The "Earned Legalization" Program)**

Eligibility requirements — all of the following must be met:
- Continuous presence in the United States for 5 or more years prior to enactment
- Not convicted of a felony or two or more serious misdemeanors
- Payment of application fee (sliding scale by income; waived for extreme hardship)
- Payment of any outstanding tax liability
- English language proficiency requirement or enrollment in an approved course
- Passing background and security check

Process:
- Phase 1: Apply for 6-year Registered Provisional Immigrant (RPI) status; work authorization and travel permission granted immediately
- Phase 2: After 6 years in RPI status with continued eligibility, apply for Lawful Permanent Residency (green card)
- Phase 3: After 3 years as LPR, eligible to apply for citizenship under standard naturalization process (total timeline: approximately 10-13 years)

Scale: Approximately 10-12 million people would be eligible. This is the vast majority of the current undocumented population — people with deep community roots who are, by any reasonable standard, members of American society.

**Political design note**: The 10-13-year timeline is deliberately substantial. It is not amnesty — it is earned legalization with a meaningful waiting period. It requires demonstrating attachment (continuous presence), good character (no serious criminal history), civic investment (taxes, English), and patience. This design reflects the polling consensus and should be defended as such: "Not amnesty. Earned. Over a decade. For people who have built their lives here."

**Sec. 3.2 — DREAM Act: Accelerated Pathway for Childhood Arrivals**
- Undocumented individuals brought to the U.S. before age 18 who have maintained continuous presence and meet educational/work requirements eligible for accelerated 3-year pathway to LPR status
- Military service, 4-year college completion, or 4 years of continuous employment all qualify
- Dream Act recipients eligible for citizenship 3 years after LPR status

**Sec. 3.3 — Temporary Protected Status and TPS Restoration**
- Codify in statute: TPS designations for countries with ongoing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or extraordinary conditions
- Restore TPS for El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, Venezuela, Haiti, and other countries whose designations were revoked by Trump
- Create pathway to LPR for individuals who have held TPS for 10+ cumulative years

**Sec. 3.4 — Farmworker Pathway**
- Accelerated 3-year pathway to LPR for agricultural workers who can demonstrate 1,000+ hours of agricultural work over 2 years
- Key support for food security, rural economies, and agricultural labor market stabilization

#### Title III-A: Immigration Court Structural Reform
*(The most important long-term institutional fix — addressing the broken model, not just adding judges)*

The 200-day adjudication target in Title I requires not just more judges, but a fundamentally reformed court system. Adding judges to a broken model produces faster bad outcomes, not better ones. The immigration court system has three structural defects that funding alone cannot fix:

**The Independence Defect:** Immigration judges are employees of the Department of Justice — the same department that prosecutes removal cases. The immigration judge works for the attorney general, who is also the prosecutor. This is a fundamental conflict of interest that produces inconsistent, politically pressured outcomes. The solution is well-established in administrative law: an independent Article I immigration court.

**Sec. 3A.1 — Article I Immigration Court Establishment Act**
- Create a new independent Article I Immigration Court system, removing immigration judges from the Executive Office for Immigration Review (DOJ) and establishing them as an independent judicial body accountable to Congress
- Modeled on the Tax Court and Court of International Trade: judges confirmed by the Senate, removable only for cause, with fixed terms
- Appellate review streamlined: create an intermediate appeals court that provides consistent national standards before cases reach the federal circuit courts
- This structural reform eliminates the attorney general's authority to overturn immigration judge decisions by "certification" — a power used by every recent attorney general to change immigration law without legislative action

**The Adversarial Model Defect:** The adversarial immigration court model, imported from criminal procedure, is poorly designed for asylum determination — which is primarily a factual inquiry (did persecution happen? is the fear credible?) not a legal dispute between opposing parties.

**Sec. 3A.2 — Inquisitorial Elements for Asylum**
- Grant immigration judges authority in asylum proceedings to actively develop the record: request documents, question witnesses, and commission country condition reports — rather than passively receiving evidence from adversarial parties who may lack equal resources
- Require the court to ensure that all relevant evidence is before it, regardless of which party presents it
- Establish a public defender model for asylum seekers: represented cases resolve 5x faster than unrepresented cases; the cost of representation is far lower than the cost of longer proceedings
- Pilot non-adversarial asylum determination for straightforward cases (clear country condition evidence, no credibility questions) with administrative law judge review

**The Backlogs-as-Leverage Defect:** The current backlog — 3+ million cases — is not just an administrative failure. It functions as a years-long de facto stay of removal for anyone who can file any claim, meritorious or not, creating an incentive structure that rewards delay and dishonesty.

**Sec. 3A.3 — Dedicated Fast-Track for Meritless Claims**
- Create a specialized fast-track docket for cases where: the country of origin has a credible fear rate below 10%; the applicant has a prior removal order; or the claim was filed after a significant enforcement action (indicating a strategic filing)
- Fast-track decisions within 60 days; single-judge panel; streamlined evidence procedures
- Applicants in the fast-track docket retain full appellate rights but expedited initial adjudication
- This is the "courts that work for everyone" reform: speeding meritless claims to resolution creates more resources for complex claims with genuine protection needs

#### Title IV: Detention Reform

**Sec. 4.1 — Detention as Last Resort**
- Statutory requirement that detention is used only when an individual presents a documented flight risk or safety threat that cannot be addressed by alternatives
- Mandatory bond hearings within 48 hours of detention for all individuals
- Prohibited: indefinite detention without periodic court review
- Prohibited: detention of individuals who are complying with check-in and court requirements

**Sec. 4.2 — Conditions of Detention**
- Mandatory minimum standards for all facilities: medical care, legal access, translation services, family notification, outdoor time
- Close all tent camps and facilities not meeting minimum standards within 18 months
- Eliminate all private for-profit immigration detention contracts (profit motive creates perverse incentives for longer detention)
- Establish independent monitoring authority with unannounced inspection rights and public reporting requirements

**Sec. 4.3 — Alternatives to Detention**
- $2 billion annually for robust alternatives-to-detention program: electronic monitoring, case management, community supervision
- Evidence base: ATD programs have >96% court appearance rates at a fraction of the cost of detention ($17/day vs. $350/day for detention)
- Expand community-based case management programs proven to produce better outcomes and higher compliance

#### Title V: Humanitarian and Due Process Protections

**Sec. 5.1 — Family Protection**
- Statutory ban on family separation as an enforcement tactic (codify in law what should never need to be in law)
- Mandatory reunification protocols for any family separated in violation of this prohibition
- Preserve and strengthen right of U.S. citizen children to petition for parents facing deportation

**Sec. 5.2 — Due Process Rights**
- Statutory right to counsel in immigration proceedings for unaccompanied minors (guaranteed); expand right to counsel to all detained individuals
- Prohibit deportation to third countries with no community ties without individual consent and judicial review
- Restore full judicial review of asylum denials; end expedited removal without hearing for individuals with 10+ years of continuous presence
- Codify birthright citizenship in statute (while the 14th Amendment is clear, a statutory codification creates additional legal protection and political clarity)

**Sec. 5.3 — Visa Overstay Modernization**
- The majority of undocumented immigrants are visa overstays, not border crossers — yet 99% of border security funding addresses the border
- Fund robust tracking and notification system for visa overstays
- Create a self-reporting voluntary departure program with protected status for individuals who self-report and meet eligibility criteria

## PART VI: ROOT CAUSES — THE FOREIGN POLICY DIMENSION

No domestic immigration policy succeeds long-term without addressing why people leave. The Trump administration's gutting of foreign aid and development assistance has made this worse.

### VI.1 What Drives Migration

The primary drivers of illegal immigration from Central America, Mexico, and the Caribbean are well-documented:
- **Violence**: Gang control, extortion, and domestic violence in Honduras, El Salvador, Guatemala, and now increasing areas of Ecuador and Colombia
- **Economic desperation**: Subsistence-level wages, lack of economic opportunity, periodic agricultural failure due to climate change
- **Climate disruption**: La Niña-related drought and flood cycles in the Dry Corridor have made subsistence farming increasingly untenable; will worsen
- **Corruption and impunity**: Governments unable or unwilling to protect citizens from violence or enforce labor protections

### VI.2 The International Investment Agenda

**Central American and Caribbean Development Compact**
- Restore and expand the Biden-era Root Causes Strategy for Central America ($4B over 4 years)
- Double the investment to $8B over 5 years, targeting: anti-gang law enforcement support, judicial reform, economic opportunity (especially agricultural alternatives), climate adaptation
- Condition aid on governance improvements, anti-corruption measures, and labor rights
- Work with the World Bank, IDB, and regional partners to leverage 3:1 private investment alongside government investment

**Restore USAID's Migration Capacity**
- Rebuild USAID's humanitarian and development capacity gutted by DOGE
- Restore funding for UNHCR ($3B+/year), WFP, and international humanitarian programs
- Evidence base: research shows that targeted development investment in source communities can reduce migration pressure by 20-40% over 5-10 year horizons

**Regional Migration Management**
- Create formal Regional Migration Management Agreement with Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Costa Rica, Colombia, and Ecuador
- Shared responsibility framework: each country contributes processing capacity, in exchange for legal work visa allocations and development assistance
- Similar to the EU-Turkey agreement but with better human rights protections
- End the "bottleneck at the U.S. border" model by distributing processing regionally

## PART VII: THE ECONOMIC CASE — GOING ON OFFENSE

Democrats have historically been defensive about immigration economics. The actual data supports going on offense.

### VII.1 The Fiscal Math

- Undocumented immigrants contribute an estimated **$100+ billion annually in federal, state, and local taxes**, including Social Security and Medicare taxes on earnings (despite being ineligible for benefits)
- A pathway to legal status for the established undocumented population would increase tax revenue by an estimated **$1.5–2.0 trillion over 10 years** (CBO analysis of 2013 Gang of Eight bill found similar revenue gains)
- The comprehensive reform package in this chapter is therefore **net fiscally positive** by approximately $1–1.5 trillion over 10 years, even accounting for increased government service usage
- Mass deportation of 1 million/year would cost an estimated **$88 billion/year** in direct enforcement costs, plus $1.1–$1.7 trillion in GDP loss — making it fiscally catastrophic

### VII.2 Labor Force Reality

- The U.S. has 10,000 Baby Boomers retiring every day
- The ratio of workers to retirees is projected to drop from 3:1 to 2:1 by 2035
- Social Security and Medicare are only solvent with a growing workforce; mass deportation accelerates insolvency
- Immigration is not a threat to the American welfare state. It is, increasingly, its financial foundation.

### VII.3 The Entrepreneurship Dividend

- Immigrants are 80% more likely to start businesses than native-born Americans
- 45% of Fortune 500 companies were founded by immigrants or their children
- Legal immigration — and legalization of established residents — unlocks entrepreneurial energy currently suppressed by precarious status

## PART VIII: POLITICAL STRATEGY — NAVIGATING THE COALITION

### VIII.1 The Internal Coalition Tension

The Democratic coalition contains real internal tensions on immigration:
- **Progressive/immigrant advocacy wing**: Opposes any enforcement measures; views E-Verify as punitive; wants immediate and unconditional relief; opposes any detention
- **Moderate/swing-district wing**: Wants credible enforcement and to avoid the "open borders" label; supports E-Verify; concerned about constituent backlash
- **Latino voter community**: Diverse — many strongly oppose mass deportation; some favor firmer enforcement than Democratic activists; a significant minority are themselves skeptical of recent arrivals; not a monolith

**The framework's approach to managing this tension:**

The legislation is designed to give each wing something essential while requiring each wing to accept something uncomfortable:
- Progressives get: massive pathway to legal status, DACA codification, TPS restoration, family protection, detention reform, due process restoration, foreign aid investment, asylum restoration
- Moderates get: mandatory E-Verify, substantial enforcement investment, 10-13 year pathway (not amnesty), asylum reform, regional management framework
- Latino voters get: community protection from terrorizing enforcement, pathways for family members, economic stability

The test of political viability is whether a Democratic senator from Arizona and a Democratic senator from Massachusetts can both vote for this bill. The comprehensive package is designed to make both votes possible.

### VIII.2 How to Handle the "Amnesty" Attack

The word "amnesty" is a political grenade and must be defused, not avoided. The answer:

> "Amnesty means forgiveness without consequence. This is not that. Under our plan, people who have been here illegally must wait up to 13 years, pay all back taxes, pass a criminal background check, demonstrate English proficiency, and pay application fees. At the end of that, they can apply for citizenship through the same process as anyone else. That's not amnesty. That's accountability. That's also what Ronald Reagan did in 1986 with bipartisan support — and it worked."

### VIII.3 The Reagan 1986 Anchor

Ronald Reagan signed the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, which granted legal status to approximately 3 million undocumented immigrants. This is a political gift that Democrats almost never use. The frame:

> "Immigration reform isn't a radical idea. Ronald Reagan signed it into law with bipartisan support. He understood that when you have millions of people contributing to your economy and your communities, you don't terrorize them — you bring them into the system. We're doing what Reagan did, with more accountability and a modern border management system."

### VIII.4 Immigration and the Anti-Oligarchy Frame

There is a direct connection between the immigration chapter and the broader anti-oligarchy framing that should be deployed:

> "The people who benefit from the current broken immigration system are not immigrants or American workers. It's the corporations who exploit undocumented workers because their vulnerability means they can't demand minimum wage, can't report safety violations, can't organize a union. The Koch brothers' industries, the agricultural conglomerates, the hotel chains — they use immigration as a cheap labor tool. We're going to end that by bringing workers into the legal system where they have rights. That's good for American workers and good for immigrant workers."

## PART IX: ENFORCEMENT PHILOSOPHY IN DETAIL

### IX.1 What Real Enforcement Looks Like

A Democratic administration must be crystal clear that it enforces immigration law — but that enforcement looks different:

**Who gets deported under our framework:**
- People who have entered recently and have no established community ties
- People with convictions for violent crimes, serious drug trafficking, or crimes against children
- People who have failed to appear for immigration hearings without good cause
- People who have committed immigration fraud

**Who does not get deported:**
- People with 10+ years of continuous presence and no serious criminal history
- Parents of U.S. citizen children, absent a serious criminal conviction
- DACA recipients
- TPS holders
- People who are complying with immigration court proceedings

**How enforcement is conducted:**
- Warrant-based operations, with judicial oversight
- Coordinated with local law enforcement only on a voluntary basis; no commandeering of local police for federal immigration enforcement (the "sanctuary city" question)
- Transparent, with public reporting on who is detained and why
- With access to legal representation
- Never at sensitive locations or in retaliation for political speech

### IX.2 The "Sanctuary Cities" Question

This is a political flashpoint that Democrats handle poorly. The honest answer:

> "We support local police focusing on local crime and public safety — not being drafted into federal immigration enforcement. When local police are seen as immigration enforcers, immigrant communities stop cooperating with police investigations. Crimes go unreported. That makes everyone less safe. That's why police chiefs in the country's biggest cities have opposed using local police for immigration enforcement. We agree with those police chiefs."

The frame: **community safety**, not "we're protecting undocumented immigrants from consequences."

### IX.3 The Fentanyl/Drug Trafficking Frame

Republicans have successfully fused immigration and fentanyl/drug trafficking. Democrats must address this directly:

**The actual facts:**
- Over 90% of fentanyl enters the U.S. through legal ports of entry, typically hidden in vehicles or shipped in packages
- The primary smugglers of fentanyl are U.S. citizens, not undocumented immigrants
- Mass deportations and border militarization do not reduce fentanyl trafficking — they redirect it through existing legal channels

**The Democratic answer:**
> "We want to stop fentanyl. That means the technology to scan every vehicle and package at ports of entry — where 90% of the drugs are coming through. We've proposed $30 billion for exactly that kind of smart border security. Instead, the Republicans spent $170 billion on tent camps for families. If you're serious about stopping fentanyl, you fund the technology. We do."

## PART X: METRICS, ACCOUNTABILITY, AND DURABILITY

### X.1 Key Performance Indicators for the Immigration System

The governance plan should include specific, measurable targets:
- **Processing time**: 90% of asylum cases decided within 200 days within 3 years of full funding
- **Legal pathway expansion**: Legal immigration capacity increased by 40% within 5 years
- **Backlog**: Employment-based and family-based backlogs reduced by 50% within 5 years; eliminated within 10 years
- **Enforcement**: 85%+ of interior enforcement arrests are of individuals with documented criminal history
- **Detention**: Average detention population reduced 75% from 2025 peak within 3 years; 100% of facilities meeting minimum standards within 18 months
- **Pathway to legal status**: 5 million applicants in provisional status within 3 years of enactment
- **Employer compliance**: 90% of large employers E-Verify compliant within 3 years
- **Fentanyl interdiction**: Advanced scanning technology deployed at all major ports of entry within 2 years

### X.2 Making Reform Durable

The 1986 Reagan reform failed at durability — legal status was granted but the enforcement and legal pathway provisions were never implemented. This framework includes:

- **Mandatory appropriations triggers**: Pathway provisions only fully activate after border technology deployment, immigration court expansion, and E-Verify rollout are certified as complete
- **Annual Congressional reporting**: DHS, DOJ, and State Department required to report annually on all metrics
- **Independent oversight commission**: Bipartisan commission monitors implementation; reports to Congress; has subpoena authority
- **Constitutional codification**: Key provisions (birthright citizenship, due process, family protection) should be defended in the courts immediately; constitutional amendment pursuit where needed
