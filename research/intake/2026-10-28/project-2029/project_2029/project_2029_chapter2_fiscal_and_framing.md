## SECTION A: THE FRAMING DEBATE
### Anti-Oligarchy vs. Moderate Populism — A Critical Analysis

---

### A.1 Why This Debate Matters Now

This is not an abstract ideological argument. The frame Democrats choose will shape:

- **Which revenue sources are politically viable** (a wealth tax requires naming wealth concentration as a problem)
- **Which voters you reach** (anti-oligarchy framing moves economically anxious non-college voters; moderated framing tries to hold suburban college-educated voters who may fear "radicalism")
- **Which governing actions flow naturally** from the mandate you claim
- **How you respond when the plan is attacked** (a vague "fairness" frame collapses under pressure; a specific populist frame has an answer)

The wrong answer is to avoid the debate by triangulating into vagueness. The Democrats who ran on "fighting for the middle class" in 2024 without naming who was fighting against them lost. Contrast is the mechanism by which any party earns a mandate.

---

### A.2 The Case for Anti-Oligarchy Framing

**The structural argument**

The concentration of economic and political power is not rhetorical — it is measurable, documented, and worsening:

- The top 0.1% of American households now hold approximately 20% of all wealth, up from 7% in the late 1970s
- The bottom 90%'s share of wealth has fallen from ~35% to ~25% in the same period
- The 400 wealthiest Americans own more wealth than all Black households and a quarter of Latino households combined
- Corporate profits as a share of GDP are at historic highs; worker wages as a share of GDP are near historic lows
- The Supreme Court's Citizens United decision in 2010, combined with the rise of dark money, has enabled a small number of billionaires to exert outsized influence over both parties — but predominantly Republicans — in ways that systematically tilt policy toward their interests

This is not a perception problem. It is a structural reality that the policy agenda must address at the root, not just the symptom.

**The political argument**

Anti-oligarchy is not a left-wing fringe position. It is a supermajority position when framed correctly:

- 2-to-1 or greater majorities of Americans — across party lines — consistently support: taxing the wealthy more, breaking up monopolies, reducing corporate political influence, capping drug prices, and protecting Social Security and Medicare from cuts
- 68% of voters (including majorities of Republicans) support a wealth tax on fortunes over $50 million
- 78% of Americans believe large corporations have too much political power
- Populist economic anger was the dominant driver of the 2024 electorate — Trump channeled it against immigrants and the "deep state"; Democrats must channel it against corporate power and the billionaire class

Anti-oligarchy framing also provides:
- A coherent villain (not abstract "the system" but specific actors and mechanisms)
- A moral grounding that does not depend on cultural identity politics
- A natural policy pipeline: every major reform has an enemy whose power it diminishes
- Cross-racial, cross-geographic appeal — the billionaire class is a universal antagonist

**What "anti-oligarchy" does NOT mean**

This frame is frequently mischaracterized. To be clear about what this framework advocates:

- It does NOT mean anti-business or anti-success. It means anti-monopoly, anti-capture of government by concentrated private wealth. Small businesses are natural allies.
- It does NOT mean class warfare rhetoric. Warren's "two cents" framing is not incendiary — it is disarming. The goal is to make the ask sound reasonable, because it is.
- It does NOT mean Bernie Sanders-style democratic socialism (though there is a Sanders constituency that can be included in the coalition). The frame is about rigged rules and fair play, not about abolishing capitalism.
- It does NOT mean ignoring cultural and social concerns. It means not leading with them. Economic populism is the through-line that allows Democrats to hold a coalition that includes people who might disagree on other things.

---

### A.3 The Case for the Moderate Frame

**The legitimate version of the moderate argument** (as opposed to the corporate-donor version) goes:

1. **Alienation risk**: "Anti-oligarchy" language can be heard as anti-success, anti-aspiration, or as class warfare — especially among voters who consider themselves middle-class and aspire to wealth. Language matters and the messenger matters. Not every candidate can authentically carry a Warren-style populist attack.

2. **Coalition breadth**: The suburban college-educated voters who swung Democratic in 2018-2022 may be deterred by what they perceive as "too far left" rhetoric. This includes upper-middle-class professionals who benefit partially from the current system and don't identify with economic grievance.

3. **Governance coalition**: Passing a wealth tax or breaking up major corporations requires winning the Senate, and winning the Senate often requires moderates in purple states. There is tension between what wins a presidential campaign and what can pass through 50 Senate votes.

4. **Credibility with fiscal hawks**: A growing number of independents are genuinely concerned about deficits and debt. An agenda that appears to promise unlimited spending may lose this cohort.

5. **Electoral geography**: The Electoral College and Senate map require winning rural and small-town voters in states like Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, Michigan, Arizona, Nevada, and Georgia. These voters may respond better to "fix what's broken" than "tax the billionaires."

---

### A.4 THE SYNTHESIS: What This Framework Recommends

**Bottom line: Anti-oligarchy framing is correct, but it must be executed with precision, warmth, and specificity. The moderate camp is right about tone and wrong about substance.**

Here is the specific synthesis:

#### Adopt: "Rigged Rules" over "Class War"

- "The game is rigged" is more powerful than "tax the rich" because:
  - It is grievance-based (who rigged it? for whom?) rather than envy-based
  - It implies a solvable structural problem, not a permanent division
  - It allows aspiration ("the rules should be fair for everyone who works hard, including you")
  - It invites Republicans who've been taken advantage of too ("farmers squeezed by monopolies, small businesses crushed by corporate chains")
  - It has been tested and wins across demographic groups

#### Adopt: Name specific villains, not abstract categories

- "Pharmaceutical companies that charge you $400 for insulin that costs $2 to make" > "the wealthy"
- "Wall Street hedge funds buying your apartment buildings and jacking up rent" > "oligarchs"
- "Elon Musk getting access to your Social Security data to help his companies" > "billionaires"
- "Companies like Amazon paying zero in federal taxes while you pay 22%" > "corporations"

#### Adopt: The "fair share" ask, not the punitive frame

- Warren's "two cents" framing is the right template — the wealth tax is not a punishment, it's asking for a reasonable contribution
- "We're not asking the wealthy to stop being wealthy. We're asking them to pitch in for the country that made them wealthy."
- This is earned populism: the ask is modest relative to the concentration of wealth; the benefits are concrete and widely shared

#### Adopt: An economic populism that includes small business and farmers

- Independent small business owners and family farmers are natural allies against monopoly power, corporate consolidation, and regulatory capture
- This is the "good capitalism vs. rigged capitalism" framing: Democrats defend competitive markets, entrepreneurship, and the opportunity to build something — against the rigged-rules capitalism that crushes competition and concentrates wealth

#### Reject: The purely defensive "not too far left" posture

- Trimming the sail to avoid being attacked doesn't prevent attacks; it just leaves you with a smaller boat
- The Obama and Biden years demonstrate that governing timidity does not earn political rewards
- Voters who are persuadable are more responsive to genuine conviction and specific plans than to carefully hedged moderation
- The risk of a watered-down agenda is real: it both fails to motivate the base AND fails to give persuadable voters a reason to switch

#### Navigate: The cultural-economic tension

- Be specific on economics; be principled but not preachy on social issues
- Don't lead with cultural issues; don't abandon them under pressure
- On trans rights, immigration, crime, and other cultural flashpoints: lead with values (dignity, fairness, safety) rather than policy categories; connect back to economic themes wherever possible
- The single biggest political error Democrats make is allowing their economic message to be drowned out by cultural controversy — either by opponents or by internal coalition dynamics

#### The "Not vs. Or" Principle

The binary choice — populist versus moderate — is itself a trap. The real question is: *can you build a governing coalition that passes bold economic reform while winning a broad enough electoral coalition to govern?* The answer is yes, but it requires:
  - **A presidential candidate who projects competence and authority** as well as populist conviction
  - **Differentiated messaging** by candidate, region, and audience (Warren-style populism in the Rust Belt; pro-competitive-markets framing in suburban Virginia; anti-corporate-capture framing in rural states)
  - **Fiscal credibility** — which is exactly what the revenue scoring in Section B is designed to provide

*This section provides the fiscal foundation for the Project 2029 governing plan. All figures are in 10-year CBO-consistent windows unless otherwise noted. All estimates use conservative independent sources (Penn Wharton Budget Model, CBO, Tax Policy Center, CRFB) rather than optimistic campaign estimates.*

### B.1 THE FISCAL INHERITANCE: What We Start With

#### B.1.1 The Baseline Disaster

Before any Democratic legislation, the incoming 2029 administration will inherit a fiscal situation that is genuinely severe — made dramatically worse by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) enacted July 4, 2025.

**The OBBBA's Fiscal Damage (official CBO scoring)**

The enacted OBBBA will, over the 2025–2034 budget window:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Total revenue reduction | -$4.5 trillion |
| Total spending reduction (largely Medicaid/SNAP/student loans) | +$1.1 trillion |
| Net primary deficit increase | +$3.4 trillion |
| Interest costs on new debt | +$718 billion |
| **Total deficit increase with interest** | **~$4.1 trillion** |
| Federal debt held by public by 2034 | 127% of GDP ($53.7T) |
| People losing health insurance (CBO) | 10+ million from Medicaid cuts; 5 million additional from ACA subsidy expiration |

If OBBBA's temporary provisions are made permanent (which Republicans intend): cost rises to approximately **$5 trillion** over the budget window.

**Key point for framing**: The OBBBA cut approximately $1.4 trillion from healthcare broadly — including $1.02 trillion specifically from Medicaid, the expiration of ACA enhanced subsidies (affecting 5 million people), and other healthcare provisions. It then handed most of the resulting fiscal space to households in the top income brackets. The CBO distributional analysis confirms that the top 20% of earners receive a disproportionate share of the tax benefits. This is the fiscal crime that creates both the moral case and the political opportunity for the Project 2029 revenue agenda.

#### B.1.2 Pre-Existing Fiscal Pressures

Even before OBBBA, the fiscal picture was structurally challenging:
- Federal debt was already projected at ~107% of GDP by 2029 (pre-OBBBA baseline)
- Interest costs were projected to reach ~$1 trillion/year by 2025 and will be dramatically higher post-OBBBA
- Social Security faces a projected 2033-2035 trust fund exhaustion (triggering automatic 20-23% benefit cuts absent legislative action)
- Medicare Part A trust fund faces similar pressure by early 2030s
- Medicaid costs will be depressed in the short run by OBBBA cuts — but costs will surge in longer run as untreated conditions worsen, rural hospitals close, and emergency costs increase

**The Democratic answer to "how do you pay for it?"** begins here: the Republican fiscal record is indefensible. The OBBBA added $4+ trillion to the deficit to cut taxes for the wealthy. The Democratic agenda reverses that, uses the revenue savings to restore healthcare and build for the future, and provides a fiscally more responsible path — even while spending more on public goods.

### B.2 REVENUE SOURCES: The Menu and the Math

#### B.2.1 Revenue Source 1: Reversing OBBBA Tax Cuts for High Earners

**What is proposed**: Reverse TCJA extensions and new OBBBA tax cuts for households with income over $400,000, while keeping all TCJA provisions for households below that threshold.

**Revenue estimate (10-year, 2029–2038)**:
- Full OBBBA/TCJA reversal for top earners and corporations: **~$2.5–3.0 trillion**
- More conservative estimate targeting only top bracket (income > $400K) and corporate changes: **~$1.5–2.0 trillion**
- Reversal of OBBBA's new corporate minimum tax rollbacks and offshore tax provisions: **~$300–400 billion** additional

*Basis*: JCT estimated TCJA extension costs $3.4 trillion over 10 years (current-law baseline). The share attributable to top earners and corporations is approximately 60-70% of that figure. OBBBA adds additional corporate and high-earner cuts on top. These estimates are consistent with Tax Policy Center and Brookings analysis.

**Political note**: The "$400K threshold" is politically critical. It was Biden's explicit promise and was respected by all Democratic legislation. It makes the frame concrete: "not one dollar of tax increase on anyone making under $400,000." This is a firewall that should not be moved.

#### B.2.2 Revenue Source 2: Corporate Tax Reform

**What is proposed**:
- Raise corporate income tax rate from 21% (TCJA) to 28%
- Enforce and expand the 15% corporate alternative minimum tax (CAMT) created in IRA, weakened in OBBBA
- Close major offshore tax haven loopholes (GILTI reform, BEAT strengthening)
- Eliminate "stepped-up basis" treatment of inherited assets above $5M
- Minimum corporate tax enforcement for companies with >$1B in profits paying <15%

**Revenue estimate (10-year)**:
- Corporate rate 21% → 28%: **~$700–900 billion** (Tax Policy Center; this estimate is well-established)
- GILTI/BEAT/offshore reform: **~$400–600 billion**
- Enforce/strengthen CAMT: **~$100–200 billion**
- Stepped-up basis reform: **~$200–300 billion**
- **Total corporate reform: ~$1.4–2.0 trillion**

*Note*: These figures are conservative. The Biden administration's original Build Back Better Act used these provisions to raise approximately $1.5T in corporate revenue. Current accumulation of avoidance since OBBBA loosening means the base is larger.

#### B.2.3 Revenue Source 3: Wealth Tax

**What is proposed**: 2% annual tax on net worth above $50 million; 4% on net worth above $1 billion. Applied to worldwide assets. Strengthened enforcement with 30% audit rate for subject households.

**Revenue estimate (10-year)**:
- Warren campaign estimate (Saez/Zucman): $3.0 trillion+
- Penn Wharton Budget Model (conservative, includes avoidance): **$2.3–2.7 trillion**
- Tax Foundation (most skeptical, dynamic effects): **$2.2 trillion**
- Summers/Sarin (very conservative, disputed): $1.0–1.4 trillion

**This framework's working estimate**: **$2.0–2.5 trillion** over 10 years, using a conservatively-adjusted version of the Penn Wharton estimate that assumes stronger enforcement than baseline avoidance models but acknowledges real-world avoidance risks.

**The avoidance problem and the enforcement answer**:

The key challenge of a wealth tax is avoidance — wealthy households restructuring assets, using trusts, moving offshore, or undervaluing holdings. The Warren plan addresses this with:
- 30% minimum audit rate for all households subject to tax
- Mandatory third-party reporting of all assets (stocks, real estate, private equity, art, offshore holdings)
- 40% exit tax on assets for those who renounce citizenship specifically to avoid the tax
- IRS capacity rebuilding (see Section B.2.7 on tax enforcement)
- Strong anti-trust mechanism for shell companies and trust structures

This framework also notes: 80%+ of the wealthiest Americans' wealth is in publicly-traded securities and real estate, both of which have clear market values and are difficult to hide. The Summers avoidance estimate implies implausibly high evasion rates on assets that are inherently transparent.

**Constitutional considerations**: Opponents argue a wealth tax is unconstitutional as a "direct tax" requiring apportionment. This is a genuine legal risk. Legal scholars are divided. The framework contemplates:
1. Pushing the constitutional argument forward (litigation is inevitable)
2. Preparing an alternative "mark-to-market" capital gains regime as fallback, which is on cleaner constitutional ground
3. Pursuing a constitutional amendment process in parallel

#### B.2.4 Revenue Source 4: Capital Gains Reform

**What is proposed**: Treat capital gains as ordinary income for households with adjusted gross income above $1 million. Eliminate the "buy, borrow, die" tax strategy by taxing unrealized gains at death for estates above $5 million.

**Revenue estimate (10-year)**:
- Capital gains rate increase (above $1M): **~$400–600 billion**
- Unrealized gains at death: **~$200–350 billion**
- **Total: ~$600–950 billion**

*Note*: These estimates are well-established and non-controversial among tax economists. The Biden budget used similar provisions. CBO has scored these measures.

#### B.2.5 Revenue Source 5: Financial Transactions Tax

**What is proposed**: 0.1% tax on stock trades, 0.1% on bond trades, 0.005% on derivatives.

**Revenue estimate (10-year)**:
- **~$700 billion–$1 trillion**

*Source*: Multiple independent estimates from CBO, Tax Policy Center, and Urban Institute. The European Commission's proposed FTT estimated similar revenue at similar rates in comparable market volumes.

**Political note**: This is a powerful populist measure — it taxes Wall Street high-frequency trading and hedge fund activity that contributes little to productive investment, and it is invisible to ordinary investors (the average retail investor would pay less than $10/year in FTT on a $10,000 portfolio).

#### B.2.6 Revenue Source 6: Healthcare System Savings

**What is proposed**: Medicare drug price negotiation (expanded beyond IRA), public option reducing commercial insurance overhead, drug price transparency and anti-gouging regime.

**Revenue/savings estimate (10-year)**:
- Expanded Medicare drug negotiation: **~$400–800 billion** in government program savings
- Healthcare system administrative savings from public option: **~$200–400 billion**
- Drug price anti-gouging: **~$100–200 billion** in program costs avoided
- **Total government savings: ~$700 billion–$1.4 trillion**

*Note*: These are system savings, not new revenue. But they offset new spending and reduce deficit impact. The CBO estimates these reasonably well; the variance is largest in the public option where market effects are uncertain.

#### B.2.7 Revenue Source 7: Tax Enforcement (IRS)

**What is proposed**: Full restoration and expansion of IRS funding gutted by OBBBA and the Trump administration, specifically for high-wealth audits. Restore IRA-funded IRS modernization capacity.

**Revenue estimate (10-year)**:
- CBO estimated the IRA IRS funding ($80B) would raise approximately $200B in additional revenue
- Full IRS modernization with focus on high-wealth audits: **~$400–600 billion** in additional collections
- Close the "tax gap" (estimated $700B/year in uncollected taxes, disproportionately from wealthy filers): even 30% improvement = **~$2 trillion** over 10 years

**This is the single highest return-on-investment item in the revenue agenda.** Every $1 in IRS funding for high-wealth enforcement yields an estimated $5–9 in recovered revenue. The OBBBA's gutting of IRS enforcement capacity was a deliberate gift to wealthy tax evaders.

#### B.2.8 Revenue Source 8: End Fossil Fuel and Other Corporate Subsidies

**What is proposed**: Eliminate direct fossil fuel subsidies ($20B+/year in accelerated depreciation, depletion allowances, and production credits); repeal targeted tax breaks for specific industries restored or expanded in OBBBA.

**Revenue estimate (10-year)**:
- Fossil fuel subsidy elimination: **~$200–300 billion**
- Miscellaneous targeted corporate subsidy repeal: **~$100–200 billion**
- **Total: ~$300–500 billion**

### B.6 THE FISCAL BOTTOM LINE: HONEST THREE-SCENARIO ANALYSIS

The fiscal picture depends critically on two uncertain variables: whether the wealth tax is enacted and survives legal challenge (Year 3-4), and whether conservative or optimistic revenue assumptions prove correct. Here is the honest three-scenario picture using the fully revised $9.6T spending estimate (updated to incorporate Chapter 14 criminal justice investment):

| | Conservative, No Wealth Tax (Years 1-2) | Conservative, Full Agenda (Years 3-4) | Optimistic, Full Agenda |
|-|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|------------------------|
| Revenue | $5.6T | $7.6T | $12.2T |
| Spending | **$9.6T** | **$9.6T** | **$9.6T** |
| **Net fiscal position** | **-$4.0T** | **-$2.0T** | **+$2.6T** |

### B.8 THE FISCAL CREDIBILITY PACKAGE: WHAT MAKES THE NUMBERS BELIEVABLE

Specific design choices that improve fiscal credibility and should be built into legislative text:

1. **Automatic stabilizers**: Phase in spending when revenue targets are met; include automatic adjustment mechanisms if revenue falls short

2. **Independent scoring**: Commit to CBO scoring of all major legislation before floor vote; no gimmicks or timing tricks (unlike OBBBA's use of "current policy" baseline)

3. **10-year budget discipline**: Establish explicit 10-year budget targets for each domain; require annual Presidential budget reports on progress

4. **Pay-for requirements**: Legislative language requiring offsets for all new spending above a baseline level — with identified revenue sources

5. **Social Security ring-fence**: Explicitly exempt Social Security from any deficit reduction discussions; fund SS solvency through the cap on payroll taxes (currently $168,600 — lift or eliminate the cap progressively)

6. **National Fiscal Commission**: Propose a bipartisan long-run fiscal sustainability commission modeled on the Debt Reduction Task Force — not to cut Social Security and Medicare, but to identify a long-run path to fiscal sustainability that is compatible with public investment
