# Education Package: Bill Analysis, Trust Fund Architecture, and 20-Year Model

Oct 19, 2026 · @espen

## What this session produced

A financing architecture for a birth-through-college education package, built out from Rep. Bonamici's Transforming Education for the Future Act: a dedicated trust fund funded by a **1.28-point uncapped payroll contribution plus a frozen estate tax exemption**, carrying roughly $300B a year in mandatory programs at maturity and solvent through 2049 with statutory borrowing authority.

Three findings matter more than the headline rate.

1. **The rate is a drafting outcome, not a policy choice.** It moved from 1.60 to 1.28 points entirely through corrections: sunsetting temporary programs properly, indexing spending to per-child costs rather than the payroll base, and freezing the estate exemption. None of those would be debated in a markup; all of them matter more than the difference between politically plausible rates.
2. **Benefits must arrive in year one.** ESEA money reached districts within a single summer in 1965; ESSER moved $122B in weeks. Formula money through existing pipes has no implementation lag. The only genuine capacity constraints are pre-K seats and new hires.
3. **Per-child formulas, not fixed dollar totals.** Demographics and immigration policy both swing the denominator hard. A program written per child with a defined standard absorbs those swings without a vote; a fixed total becomes a real cut or an unearned windfall depending on which way the cohort moves.

All figures are modeled estimates built from CBO, CRS, and CRFB anchors, not official scores. Window is 2030 to 2049, nominal dollars unless noted.

## The bill: structure and dollar amounts

The [Transforming Education for the Future Act](https://bonamici.house.gov/media/press-releases/bonamici-unveils-legislative-plan-transform-k-12-education) runs 466 pages across three titles and bundles roughly 22 existing Democratic bills, including Wilson's American Teacher Act, DeLauro's CHARTER Act, Omar's universal meals bill, Beyer's Keeping All Students Safe Act, and McBath's PARA and READ Acts. It has 13 cosponsors plus AFT and National PTA endorsements.

Money arrives two ways. Universal pre-K and school meals are **mandatory spending**. Everything else is **authorized**, so it still depends on appropriators each year. The fixed-dollar authorizations alone total about $73B annually in FY2027, excluding IDEA, the "such sums" programs, and the mandatory lines.

### Title I — a well-rounded education

Sec. 10001 carries the IDEA glidepath and the Perkins and Title IV numbers. Part A funds project-based learning grants (up to $2M over five years), writes arts education into state plans and report cards, adds Black, AANHPI, Hispanic, and American Indian history to the civics grants and NAEP, and expands community schools (planning grants from $100K; implementation at least $250K per school per year for five years). The READ Act directs literacy grants to every state in the bottom fifth on fourth-grade NAEP reading and requires states to receive NAEP results, with interstate rankings, within five days.

Part B keeps annual testing but bars states from using tests as the sole factor in grade retention, diplomas, or teacher evaluation, and from rating schools solely on scores or graduation rates. The innovative assessment pilot expands from 7 to 20 states and 5 to 7 years at $100M a year, with another $100M for professional development. Two new indicators are added — advanced coursework access and school climate — the improvement set-aside rises from 7% to 10%, subgroup reporting drops to groups of 10, and schools cannot exit improvement status until their lowest-performing subgroups improve for two consecutive years.

### Title II — climate, educators, research

Civil rights guidance covers disparate impact in discipline, police in schools, and restorative practices, and the Emergency School Aid Act desegregation program returns at $1B a year. The American Teacher Act sets a **$60,000 minimum salary** from 2027–28, CPI-indexed, through four-year state grants with 85% passthrough and a sustainability plan requirement — funded at "such sums." The PARA Act funds paraprofessional wages on the Title I formula, also at "such sums."

Safety provisions ban corporal punishment in federally funded programs and ban seclusion, mechanical and chemical restraint, and restraint restricting breathing — both with private rights of action, plus $40M a year in state grants. A new ESEA Part G requires anti-bullying policies with enumerated categories including sexual orientation and gender identity. The FACTS Act funds an HHS fentanyl pilot of up to 25 school–health partnerships. Subtitle D creates a National Center for Advanced Development in Education inside IES and an ed-tech clearinghouse rating vendors on effectiveness, privacy, and data monetization.

### Title III — socioeconomic barriers

Repeals the federal tax-credit scholarship program (Sec. 70411 of P.L. 119-21) and the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program, and bars moving ESEA, IDEA, Perkins, or ESRA functions out of the Education Department. Maintenance-of-equity rules hold the highest-poverty districts at or above FY2025 per-pupil state funding through FY31. The CHARTER Act bars for-profit management of charter schools, using a definition that reaches all of ESEA and IDEA, effective three years out.

Universal preschool runs through HHS: free for all 3- and 4-year-olds, 100% federal cost share in FY27–29 stepping to 60% by FY33, at least 1,020 hours a year, K-12 pay parity, a BA requirement for lead teachers within seven years, 4% tribal set-aside, and locality grants where states opt out. Universal meals take effect one year after enactment, eliminating the reduced-price and paid tiers, with Summer EBT rising from $40 to $60 a month.

## Drafting errors found in the text

Worth flagging to the office.

- **Missing infrastructure provisions.** Purpose (6) promises to rebuild school infrastructure; no section does it.
- **Duplicate section numbers.** Secs. 30114 and 23707 both create a new ESEA "Sec. 8549D."
- **Broken cross-references.** Parts 2–3 of Subtitle C cite Secs. 24236, 24311, and 24337, which appear to be transpositions of 23246, 23311, and 23337.
- **Probable unintended cuts.** Migrant Education drops to $100M from $374.75M; the Charter Schools Program falls to $250M from $440M. Both sit below recent appropriations.
- **Expired start dates.** The NEED Act and data systems authorizations begin in FY2026.
- **Stale meal reimbursement rates.** The bill copies 2023 rates — $4.63 lunch, $2.80 breakfast — against current 2026–27 rates of $4.76–$4.87 and $2.54–$3.05. As written this would *cut* per-meal payments to many high-poverty schools. The Senate's 2026 version uses $5.42 and $3.28.
- **Smaller glitches.** The Title II-A figure is missing its dollar sign; the IDEA amendment's "paragraph (2)" reference reads ambiguously.

Two substantive gaps beyond drafting. The Department-protection provision does not cover civil rights enforcement, which rests on separate statutes, so the June 2026 transfer of OCR functions to DOJ would survive it; and there is no staffing floor despite the summary's language. Separately, ending free and reduced-price meal applications removes the poverty count many state funding formulas rely on — the bill creates a replacement Title I measure, but state formulas are left to catch up on their own.

## The expanded package

Three additions came out of the session, on the judgment that the bill as written creates problems it does not solve.

**Teacher pipeline funding, front-loaded.** The bill's hiring demand — pre-K at scale needs 150,000 to 250,000 new lead teachers and assistants — arrives without any supply-side investment. Because preparation takes one to four years, pipeline money has to land two or three years *ahead* of the demand shock, not alongside it. The instruments exist and are underfunded: Teacher Quality Partnership residency grants (currently $70M; scaling to 50,000 residents a year costs $1–1.5B at $20–30K per candidate), registered teaching apprenticeships, Hawkins Centers, and Grow Your Own programs. Worth noting that the binding constraint on teacher supply is usually compensation rather than prep slots, so the $60K floor may do more for the pipeline than pipeline programs do; prep capacity binds mainly in special education, bilingual education, and early childhood.

**Child care.** A pre-K expansion without an under-3 expansion makes infant and toddler care worse by pulling staff toward the better-paying end — the standard critique of the Build Back Better design, and the strongest argument for treating them as one bill.

**Higher education**, on a separate track. Free community college, a larger Pell, and loan restructuring pull toward different committees and different vehicles. There is a real case for a birth-through-12 reconciliation bill in year one and an HEA reauthorization on its own track, rather than one omnibus with more failure points.

That is roughly 0.8–1.0% of GDP, or $5–7 trillion over twenty years — the largest peacetime expansion of the social state since the Great Society, comparable in size to Medicare Part A. One factor works in its favor: the school-age population is shrinking, so universal coverage is cheaper to reach than current enrollment implies. The same demographics produce stranded buildings and closure fights that land on districts in the same years.

## Projected effects, 2030–2050

### What passes

The package splits along procedural lines. Reconciliation-eligible: pre-K, child care, meals and Summer EBT, Head Start pay, repeal of the scholarship tax credit, and IDEA and Title I if written as mandatory. Not reconciliation-eligible: the corporal punishment and restraint bans, the charter definition, the subgroup-size and accountability changes, and the bar on moving programs out of the Department. A realistic outcome is most of the dollars and few of the mandates, with enactment mid-to-late 2029 at the earliest.

### 2030–2036, build-out

Universal meals reach roughly 30 million additional children under a single rule, worth $1,000–1,600 a year to households that currently pay, with measurable reductions in food insecurity among near-poor families within a year or two. Teacher pay reaches paychecks in 2030 under the bridge credit below. Pre-K scales through 2033–34.

The hiring surge is the risk. Hiring 250,000 people quickly means the marginal hire is weaker than the current average, so **average teacher quality probably dips before it rises** — and that dip lands in the same years Congress starts asking whether the money worked. Infant and toddler care loses staff to better-paying pre-K jobs unless child care subsidies expand alongside.

### 2036–2042, the test

Assume at least one Republican trifecta. Expect attempts to block-grant pre-K, add work requirements to child care subsidies, cut the discretionary half hard, and target the contribution rate itself. The entitlement structures survive with damage; Title I above the floor, pipeline programs, competitive grants, and any accountability mandates do not.

The rate is the vulnerable piece. A future Congress can cut it while leaving the program formally intact — the Highway Trust Fund outcome. That is the modal bad scenario, maybe 40% likely.

### 2042–2050, maturity

- Federal share of K-12 revenue at 15–18%, roughly double today's
- Pre-K covering 60–75% of 4-year-olds and 40–50% of 3-year-olds, with 8–12 states still out
- IDEA at or near 40%, or 30–35% if indexing was written to prices
- Teaching with a nominal median of $90–100K and a workforce whose composition actually changed — teaching turns over about 8% a year, so composition effects show up around 2042–45, not 2035

### Student outcomes

Be modest. School finance research associates sustained per-pupil increases of about 10% with real attainment and earnings gains over 10–12 years. Federal dollars are a fraction of total spending and money is partly fungible — states reallocate their own, especially with IDEA relief appearing as property tax cuts, so the real effective increase is probably 60–70% of the nominal one.

| Horizon | Expected effect |
| --- | --- |
| 2032–38 | Kindergarten readiness up; vacancies down sharply in high-poverty and rural districts; NAEP movement of 2–5 points concentrated in low-income subgroups; graduation rates +1–3 points |
| 2038–49 | For cohorts starting school around 2032: +0.3–0.5 years attainment, +5–10% adult earnings for low-income students, a few points off adult poverty incidence; college-going among low-income students +5–10 points |

Pre-K's clearest effects are kindergarten readiness and maternal employment (+2–4 points for mothers of 3s and 4s); test-score fade-out is the standard finding. Meals, Summer EBT, and child care affordability together take perhaps 1–2 points off child poverty — a quarter the size of what the 2021 child tax credit did.

### The biggest failure mode

Not fiscal. **The visible costs arrive a decade before the measurable benefits**, and the quality dip during the hiring scramble hits exactly when critics are looking for evidence. A 2036 headline reading "$2 trillion later, NAEP is flat" would be technically accurate, analytically meaningless, and extremely effective. Designing for durability means front-loading what shows results fast — meals, child care costs, maternal employment, vacancy rates — and building in independent evaluation with pre-registered expectations.

The package also does not touch housing segregation, school assignment, curriculum, phones, or post-pandemic absenteeism. Those are the constraints money does not lift.

## Macroeconomics

### The labor supply channel is real and bounded

Capping child care costs plus universal pre-K plausibly adds **1 to 1.5 million workers**, roughly 0.6–0.9% of the labor force. But the marginal workers are disproportionately mothers of young children returning at lower hours and wages than the workforce average, so the output effect is closer to **0.3–0.5% of GDP, or $110–180B a year** in mid-2030s dollars. It arrives fast — most within four or five years — because the constraint is care availability, not skills.

**Revenue feedback.** At a combined effective federal rate of 20–25% including payroll taxes, that yields $25–45B a year in federal revenue, $10–15B in state and local, plus reduced benefit spending as earnings rise. The child care and pre-K components at $55–85B a year therefore **recoup a third to a half of their cost** at maturity. Good as social programs go; not self-financing, and the K-12 and higher ed pieces have no comparable near-term feedback.

Two caveats. CBO's conventional estimates will not credit most of this, so the official price tag looks worse than the economics. And subsidy phase-outs create marginal tax rates on second earners that claw back part of the gain, depending on how the income cap is drawn.

### Where it runs the other way

The first years go backwards: demand for care rises faster than supply and wage floors raise costs, so expect prices and waitlists before access, roughly 2031–34. Some of the measured GDP gain is also accounting — shifting unpaid household care into the paid market raises measured output more than welfare.

The decisive issue is financing. Adding 15–20 points of debt-to-GDP over two decades crowds out private capital enough to reduce long-run output by roughly 1–2%. Penn Wharton's modeling of universal pre-K found essentially no long-run GDP effect once financing was included. **Deficit-financed, the long-run GDP effect is plausibly near zero; financed by taxes on high-income consumption or inheritance rather than capital, it stays positive.** The pay-fors matter as much as the programs.

The human capital channel shows up in the 2045–2060 window: if low-income students see 5–10% higher earnings and are 30–40% of a cohort, that is +0.5 to 1% of GDP by 2060, phasing in at two to five hundredths of a point a year.

### Why 7-to-1 does not survive scaling

The sign is robust; the magnitude is not. Honest scaled estimates land near 2-to-1 or 3-to-1, for identifiable reasons.

- **The counterfactual changed.** Perry Preschool ran in Ypsilanti in the early 1960s with 123 children whose control group got essentially nothing. Today about two-thirds of 4-year-olds are already in some program, so the real treatment is "public pre-K instead of existing care," not "instead of nothing."
- **Tiny samples.** Perry had 123 children, Abecedarian 111. Heckman's own reanalysis keeps the returns significant with enormous confidence intervals.
- **Intensity is not scalable.** Perry used master's-level teachers, ratios near 5-to-1, and weekly 90-minute home visits, run by the researchers who designed it.
- **Universalizing adds low-return children.** As coverage expands toward the middle class, the marginal child's counterfactual is a decent preschool their parents already paid for. Average return falls mechanically as coverage rises.
- **Crime savings dominated Perry's benefit**, measured on a 1960s cohort in a high-crime environment, using contested victim-cost assumptions. Some of it is transfer rather than output.
- **Quality degrades on scaling** — the marginal hire is weaker than the average current one.
- **Discount rates do heavy work.** Benefits arrive 30–40 years out: at 3% the ratio looks excellent, at 7% it shrinks by more than half.

The scaled evidence is what matters: Head Start's benefit-cost ratios are usually estimated at 1.5–3; Boston raised college enrollment with no lasting test-score gains; Tulsa stayed positive through high school; Tennessee's statewide program went **negative by sixth grade**; Quebec's universal child care produced worse long-run behavioral and health outcomes at low quality. Quality determines whether you land near Boston or near Tennessee, and that spread is wider than the spread between plausible funding levels.

One thing cuts the other way. Perry and Abecedarian never counted maternal employment, because they were too small to change work decisions. A universal program does, and that return arrives in five years rather than forty — which is why a modern 2-to-1 is better evidenced than the old 7-to-1, and shows up inside a single budget window.

## Financing mechanics

### Statutory PAYGO

The Statutory Pay-As-You-Go Act of 2010 has OMB keep 5-year and 10-year scorecards of new mandatory spending and revenue legislation; a debit at the end of a session triggers a January sequestration order. The sequester hits little — Social Security, most Medicaid, SNAP, and veterans' benefits are exempt and Medicare is capped at 4% — which concentrates the pain on a thin slice of non-exempt programs and makes it politically ugly.

It has never triggered. Congress wipes the scorecard whenever a large bill would set it off, as with the 2017 tax law, the American Rescue Plan, and the 2025 reconciliation bill. **The catch: the wipe needs 60 votes**, since it is ordinary legislation. The party that just passed a partisan bill must ask the minority to cancel the consequences — usually successfully, but at a price, typically in the following year's appropriations. The House PAYGO rule and the Senate pay-go point of order bite earlier.

### Converting to mandatory

Mandatory spending works for formula-driven programs with a defined per-unit cost, which covers the expensive pieces: meals (already an open-ended entitlement and the model to copy), pre-K and child care as state entitlements with per-child federal reimbursement, IDEA on the Huffman "hereby appropriated" model, Title I on the mandatory Pell precedent (a guaranteed floor with discretionary appropriations layered on top), and the teacher floor as a formula grant. Competitive and innovation programs stay discretionary — they need flexibility and buy little durability.

The durability asymmetry is the point: freezing a discretionary program requires only inaction, while cutting a mandatory one requires an affirmative vote with named losers.

### The Byrd rule

Two constraints bite. Provisions cannot increase deficits beyond the budget window — which is why BBB sunset pre-K after six years and created a cliff. And conditions whose budgetary effect is "merely incidental" get stripped, which is what kills the corporal punishment ban and the charter definition.

Three ways around the window problem: offset inside the window so the program can be permanent; set a longer window in the budget resolution, since nothing requires ten years; or accept a sunset but **align the expiration dates** so you get one high-salience fight with a broad coalition instead of three separable ones.

Spending conditions generally survive, since terms governing how money is used are budgetary. Quality standards attached to pre-K dollars should hold.

### Attaching the restraint and seclusion ban to IDEA

Byrd and the Spending Clause are separate tests. On Byrd, a condition no state would refuse produces no scoreable savings, which is what makes it look incidental — so fund it (pair the requirement with training and positive behavior support money) and write it as a use-of-funds restriction rather than a prohibition.

On the Spending Clause, **IDEA is far stronger than Title I**: restraint and seclusion fall overwhelmingly on students with disabilities, so the nexus is direct. Attach the condition to the *incremental* funds above the current 10–11% share, so a declining state loses the increase rather than its entire grant — that keeps it clear of *NFIB* coercion arguments. Keep the private right of action out of reconciliation; it is the most likely piece to be stripped and is severable.

**A standalone bill is the better path anyway.** The House passed restraint and seclusion legislation 262–153 in 2010; it died in the Senate and was deliberately left out of ESSA in 2015. The blockage is institutional rather than ideological — superintendent and principal groups oppose absolute bans on liability and staff-safety grounds. A passable version bans prone, supine, mechanical and chemical restraint and anything restricting breathing; restricts rather than abolishes seclusion (imminent danger standard, time limits, monitoring, documentation); requires 24-hour parent notification and incident reporting; funds training; and drops the private right of action. Pair it only with transparency measures — anything touching school ratings or test use reopens the ESSA settlement and loses members who would otherwise vote yes. The natural vehicle is an IDEA reauthorization, overdue since 2004.

### Which base fits

Three things matter beyond the rate.

- **Growth.** Costs are 70–80% labor and grow with wages. Payroll and broad income taxes grow with wages too. A carbon tax shrinks as the thing taxed disappears; an FTT is volatile and avoidable; estate revenue is lumpy and planning-sensitive. Anything other than a wage or consumption base rebuilds the Highway Trust Fund problem.
- **Volatility.** Capital-based taxes fall hardest in recessions, exactly when pre-K slots cannot be cut.
- **The contributory frame.** A payroll contribution is the only option producing Social Security politics — everyone pays in, everyone with children draws out. High-income surtaxes fund the program without building that constituency.

### The central tension

The base that fits the spending is the one that is politically closed. A commitment not to raise taxes below $400,000 rules out broad payroll, broad income, and VAT. What remains raises less per point, is more volatile, and severs the contributory logic. Funding $290B entirely above $400k is arithmetically possible — roughly a 5-point high-income surtax plus an FTT plus estate reform plus corporate increases — but it is four separate fights and a program whose funding falls in every recession.

### Why education rather than early childhood framing

K-12's legitimacy is settled; there is no live argument about whether government should run schools. Early childhood carries an unresolved values fight about whether public money should fund institutional care or go to parents directly, which is why it polls well in the abstract and fragments on the delivery mechanism. The lifecycle logic also works better for education: a worker pays in for forty years and draws for eighteen per child, close enough to the Social Security bargain to feel like one. The counterargument is that K-12 is traditionally state and local, so a federal dedicated tax invites a turf objection — largely neutralized by folding early childhood inside an institution people already accept ("school starts at three").

**Baby bonds are not available as a frame here.** Trump Accounts already provide a $1,000 federal seed for children born 2025–2028 with $5,000 annual contributions, so seeded child accounts are bipartisan fact rather than Democratic proposal — but they are asset transfers, not service funding, and the account frame is also the voucher frame. Once money belongs to the child, directing it to a private provider is a short step, which is the logic of education savings accounts the package repeals. Keep the trust fund pooled and never describe it in account terms.

## Trust fund architecture

Federal trust funds are accounting devices, not savings accounts — the Social Security fund holds Treasury securities, a claim on general revenue. What they provide is structure: dedicated revenue, permanent appropriation that a hostile Congress cannot defund by inaction, a visible balance that forces action, and a contributory frame that changes the politics of cutting. The Highway Trust Fund is the cautionary case: it has kept road spending going since 1956 but has needed general fund transfers since 2008, because the gas tax has not risen since 1993.

### Scope

Inside the fund: **meals, CACFP and Summer EBT; IDEA at 40%; pre-K and child care; a Title I floor; the teacher floor; PARA and school mental health staffing; and the pipeline surge.** Outside: Title I above the floor, ESEA competitive and innovation programs, and higher education, which add roughly $90–120B a year on general revenue.

Roughly 60% of the package is therefore structurally protected and 40% behaves like today's education budget — slow real erosion punctuated by fights.

### Revenue

A **1.28-point uncapped payroll contribution** at full rate from enactment, plus the estate tax at 2009 parameters with stepped-up basis repealed, about $45B in 2030.

**The estate exemption must be frozen in nominal terms.** Estate revenue growth is driven by the gap between asset growth (5–6% a year) and threshold growth. A CPI-indexed exemption produces about 3% revenue growth against wage-indexed spending at 3.2%+; a frozen $3.5M threshold produces about 5%. That difference is worth $10B a year by 2039 and $25B by 2049 — the single largest lever outside the rate itself. Wage-indexing the exemption would do the opposite, shrinking real revenue over time.

Split the streams by function: the payroll contribution funds the per-child entitlement payments, which must be paid every month; the lumpier estate revenue funds facilities, the pipeline, and the reserve.

### Indexing

The key distinction: components defined as **a percentage of actual costs are self-correcting** and can never be eroded by a bad index. IDEA at 40% of per-pupil spending and pre-K pegged to a cost model with pay parity both behave this way. Components defined as a fixed dollar figure plus an index are where erosion hides.

| Component | Index | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meals | Food-away-from-home CPI × meals served | 2.1% |
| IDEA | Self-adjusting at 40% of per-pupil spending | 3.7% |
| Pre-K + child care | Per-child cost with pay parity × cohort | 3.2% |
| Title I floor | Per-child wage cost × poverty count | 3.2% |
| PARA + mental health | Staffing cost × enrollment | 3.2% |

The structural result: **revenue grows at 4.0% and spending at about 3.2%**, because the payroll base tracks wages and employment while the number of children falls. That is the opposite of the Highway Trust Fund problem, and it means the fund becomes progressively over-funded — which creates its own political risk, addressed in the surplus ladder below.

The $60,000 teacher floor is a real cut by construction: CPI-indexed it reaches about $92K by 2049 against typical wages of $118K, so it stops binding on anyone. Since federal teacher money sunsets, that mostly does not matter, but the residual program becomes decorative by the early 2040s.

### Pre-K federal share step-down

The bill drops from 100% to 60% by FY33, an abrupt cliff. Modeled here as a seven-year glide landing in the same place: 100% through 2034, then 90, 85, 80, 75, 70, 65, reaching **60% in 2041** and holding. Only pre-K steps down; child care keeps a constant federal share.

Federal pre-K spending goes flat at $104–107B from 2035 to 2041 as the declining share offsets enrollment and wage growth. The cost transfers to states, which carry about $39B a year by 2041 and $57B by 2049. **The politically dangerous years are 2038–41**, when states pass 25% and head toward 40% — late enough that pre-K is established, at a price point where a budget-strained state might exit or freeze enrollment. Tie the locality-grant bypass to the step-down years specifically, so a state exit does not cost children seats.

### Poverty counts and the Title I floor

Title I allocations run on Census SAIPE estimates, which use the **official poverty measure** — pre-tax, pre-transfer cash income. A concurrent expanded child tax credit would cut SPM child poverty dramatically while leaving OPM formula counts almost untouched, exactly as in 2021. **Name the measure explicitly in the statute**: if a future Congress moves Title I to a post-transfer measure, an expanded CTC would cut formula counts by roughly half. This is the highest-value drafting instruction in the design.

Write the floor **per child, not as a total** — about $3,200 per formula child, indexed to per-child wage costs. A fixed total divided by a denominator that grows 20% in a recession is a 17% per-child cut arriving exactly when need spikes. Per-child framing is counter-cyclical by construction; cap the adjustment at 115% of the prior year so the claim on the fund stays bounded.

The same logic answers two scenarios that would otherwise break the design. If child poverty were eliminated, two of Title I's three rationales survive intact — poor children still need more than average, and concentrated disadvantage operates through neighborhoods and turnover rather than household cash — so the program should be written against **what it buys rather than who is poor**: a foundation amount with weights for concentration, disability, English learners, mobility, and rurality. And if immigration policy reverses the demographic decline, per-child formulas absorb that automatically; a swing of 1.5 million net annual arrivals adds 200,000–300,000 school-age children a year, moving spending growth to about 3.9% and revenue to 4.4%, with English learner demand rising fastest and landing on Title III, which sits outside the fund where it is least protected.

## The teacher floor: bridge credit into a declining grant

The problem is that state legislation, not implementation, is the binding constraint. Most legislatures convene January through May; Texas, Montana, Nevada and North Dakota meet only in odd years; and salary schedules are bargained, with contracts typically settled in spring. Waiting for schedules means a two-year gap between enactment and the first raise.

**Year one — a one-time refundable credit, tax year 2030.** Flat amounts by salary band for full-time public school teachers: $3,000 below $50K, $2,000 at $50–55K, $1,000 at $55–60K, prorated for part-time. About 1.2M teachers, roughly **$3B**, repeated once for 2031 where grants have not yet arrived.

A credit is the cleanest reconciliation instrument available — unambiguously a revenue provision, with no Byrd exposure, no coercion question, and a path through Finance and Ways and Means rather than the education committees. It also bypasses states entirely. Its three weaknesses are why it stays temporary and small:

- **Employer incidence.** If districts know a federal credit fills the gap, some will not raise salaries — the EITC capture dynamic. Non-supplanting language is nearly unenforceable against 13,000 districts.
- **It is worth far less than a raise.** Not pensionable, not in base salary, no compounding through steps and lanes, no Social Security earnings, and invisible in the salary schedule a prospective teacher sees — which is where recruitment effects come from.
- **A permanent credit inverts the conversion incentive.** If it disappears when a state legislates, every state has reason never to legislate.

**Years two through five — the grant**, structured for uptake:

1. Federal share declines on a published schedule: 100% of the gap in years one and two, 75% in year three, 50% in year four, state thereafter. Early adopters get the most money.
2. First-mover bonus: states enacting within 18 months get an extra year at 100%.
3. Only the grant covers benefits and pension costs, so conversion is strictly better for teachers and unions push for it.
4. Teachers held harmless — the grant must deliver at least the credit's value in base salary.
5. The credit expires nationally after 2032 regardless of state action. Known cliff, no free ride.
6. District bypass in year three for non-adopting states, which puts the legislature in the position of explaining why its largest cities got money the state refused.

If the credit were made permanent rather than temporary, cover 60–70% of the gap rather than all of it — filling the gap entirely creates a 100% implicit marginal rate, where a district raising someone from $52K to $55K reduces the credit dollar for dollar and leaves the teacher no better off.

**One implementation wrinkle:** the IRS cannot identify teachers or their salaries, since W-2s carry no occupation code. An employer certification or new information return means one filing season of lead time, with payment at filing in early 2031.

**Cost profile:** about $3B in credit, $14–16B in grants during the ramp, then declining — because the floor is CPI-indexed while wages grow faster, so fewer teachers fall below it each year. Federal teacher money is essentially gone after 2037, leaving the floor to 50 state legislatures with a CPI-indexed target and a roughly $4B residual for low-capacity states. That is the original bill's cliff moved rather than solved; making it permanent would cost about $8–10B a year, or 0.05 points on the contribution rate.

## The 20-year model

### Spending ramp

Benefits start immediately wherever existing pipes allow. ESEA money reached districts for the fall term in 1965, Head Start served over half a million children four months after announcement, and ESSER moved $122B in weeks. Meals go universal from January 2030, Title I lands at the full floor, IDEA starts at its 15% step rather than transferring current funding, and the pipeline is front-loaded above its steady-state level so residency cohorts complete in 2031–32 when pre-K hiring peaks. **Only pre-K seats are a genuine capacity constraint** — facilities and licensing — so that piece ramps through 2033, which is also where the Head Start precedent argues for caution: the 1965 summer launch produced quality problems that shaped criticism for decades.

About $65B of 2030 outlays is spending the general fund already makes — existing meals, IDEA baseline, and Title I up to the floor. Moving it inside the fund does not raise federal spending; it swaps the financing source and frees general revenue by the same amount. That is attackable ("a new payroll deduction paying for things income taxes already bought"), and the answer is to pair the shift with an explicit general-revenue commitment to the discretionary half rather than splitting each program between two sources, which would destroy the legibility the contributory frame depends on.

### Rate comparison

The long-run breakeven is about **1.27 points**. Below it you are financing a structural gap; above it, a timing gap. The difference is roughly $33 a year for a median earner.

### Corrections made along the way

The rate fell from 1.60 to 1.28 through four corrections, none of which would surface in a markup:

1. **Temporary programs were carried as permanent.** Teacher grants sat at $15–22B forever when the declining federal share means they peak in 2033 and end by 2037; the pipeline surge ran flat when its whole purpose is front-loading; the bridge credit was never broken out.
2. **Spending was indexed at 4%**, the growth rate of the payroll base, rather than at per-child cost growth. Compensation per worker grows 3.7% and employment 0.3%, so revenue grows 4.0% — but the school-age population falls about 0.5% a year, so most spending components grow 3.2% and meals about 2.1%. Worth roughly 15% of 2049 outlays.
3. **Program scope was inconsistent.** IDEA and Title I were run through the fund as increments in the tables but costed in full in the architecture; meals were left outside despite being the mandatory model the rest copies.
4. **Meals were costed at the bill's stale rates.** At the Senate bill's $5.42 and $3.28, meals plus CACFP, summer, Summer EBT and commodities run about $63B in 2030 against roughly $41B now — an increment of $22B, not the $13–18B the stale rates implied.

## Reserve management

### A band, not a target

Define the reserve as a share of annual outlays: **floor 25%** (about three months), target 50%, ceiling 100%. Below the floor, the general-fund backstop triggers automatically as repayable borrowing. Above the ceiling, the allocation ladder runs.

The structural problem is that revenue grows at 4.0% and spending at 3.2%, so the fund becomes progressively over-funded — a demographic windfall from a stable contributor base and a falling child population. At 1.40 points the reserve would reach about $775B by 2049, which is money collected and not spent: politically indefensible and an invitation to raids.

### The surplus ladder, in statutory order

1. **Repay outstanding general-fund borrowing.** Non-negotiable, and what makes the backstop sustainable.
2. **Non-recurring uses, above 50%:** school facilities and deferred maintenance (the gap the bill's own purposes section promises and never fills); kitchen equipment, since universal meals assume a capacity to cook that many districts lack; pre-K facility construction; prepaying state transition costs during the step-down years, which directly reduces the exit risk; lump-sum loan forgiveness for teachers, paras and early childhood staff completing a service term; and data and evaluation infrastructure, which is cheap and is what lets anyone defend the program with evidence in 2036.
3. **Recurring expansions, above 100%, in order:** restore pre-K's federal share above 60% toward 75%; raise the Title I per-child amount; extend meal coverage to a third daily meal and full-year CACFP; lower child care copays or raise the income cap.
4. **Rate reduction last, if ever.** A lowered rate is nearly impossible to raise back; better to hold it and expand the benefit.

**The gate on tier 3** is an actuarial certification that the fund stays in balance over a 25-year projection *with the expansion included* — the Social Security trustees' model. Tier 2 needs no such test, because one-time spending cannot unbalance anything. The discipline that matters more than the list: every tier 2 use carries a defined endpoint in its authorization, and no tier 2 use may create a per-child or per-staff entitlement. If a use cannot be stopped after three years without someone losing a benefit they are receiving, it is not one-time spending regardless of how it scores.

Avoid technology and curriculum purchases, which date fast and recur in practice, and be careful with construction for private child care providers — who owns the asset when the provider exits needs answering in statute.

### Countercyclical district stabilization

School budgets lag the economy by one to two years: property assessments reset slowly and state aid is set in the prior session, so the worst year of the last recession for districts was 2011–12, with teacher employment down roughly 300,000 and years to recover. A stabilizer keyed to when a recession starts pays too early and stops too soon. **Fast trigger, slow off-switch.**

- **On:** the Sahm rule — unemployment's three-month average 0.5 points above its prior twelve-month low. Monthly, never a false positive, fires within months rather than after a legislative fight.
- **Off:** when state K-12 revenue recovers to its pre-recession real level, or after twelve quarters, whichever comes first.
- **Scale:** $6–8B a year per point of unemployment above the trigger. A moderate recession draws $35–50B over three years; a severe one $90–120B.
- **Allocation:** Title I formula plus a flat per-pupil amount, weighted by each state's own revenue decline; 95% passthrough within 30 days, federal money going direct to districts if a state fails to move it.
- **Use:** personnel only — salaries, benefits, retaining positions. No capital, no debt service, no reserves.

**Against substitution**, the failure of ARRA's stabilization fund: define maintenance of effort as a *share of state revenue* rather than a dollar level, so a state whose revenue falls 15% is not forced into a waiver; make waivers cost a proportional share of the funds rather than being free; add the bill's maintenance-of-equity rule, since the deepest 2010–12 cuts landed in the poorest districts; and bar funds to any state cutting taxes during the draw period.

Two structural points. Make it **obligation authority, not an appropriation**, so districts can plan staffing a year ahead — uncertainty causes layoffs nearly as effectively as actual revenue loss. And sequence it against the ladder: if the reserve is above the ceiling when a recession hits, tier 2 releases suspend automatically until the reserve is rebuilt.

**It is a separate obligation, not a duplicate of the passive draw.** In a recession three things happen: revenue falls ($15–18B a year), mandatory costs rise automatically as poverty counts and subsidy enrollment climb ($8–12B), and districts lay off teachers. The first two are the passive draw, about $25–30B a year, and absorbing them is what the reserve is for. Stabilization addresses what they leave untouched — but the combined call is $40–45B a year, which does not fit inside a $38B trough. So: **compute each district's release net of the automatic increases it already received** (worth a quarter to a third of the cost), and make stabilization explicitly **junior** to the passive draw, firing only if the reserve clears the floor afterward, with the backstop covering the rest. That keeps the core entitlements senior, which is the right priority ordering if both cannot be funded.

## Recession stress test, 2036–38

This is the worst plausible timing, chosen deliberately: the fund is at its thinnest, pre-K is mid-step-down, and the program is established enough that failure is visible but not old enough to be untouchable. Unemployment rises 2.5 points peaking in 2037; payroll revenue falls 5% in 2037 and recovers over two years; district revenue bottoms in 2038 on the usual lag, so stabilization runs 2037–39.

At 1.35 points the reserve would bottom at $46B in 2039 and never reach zero, so the extra 0.07 points is what buys recession-proofing — about $46 a year at a median wage.

**The vulnerability is presentational, not fiscal.** Three years of a trust fund borrowing from the general fund, with the balance at zero in 2038, will be called insolvency. The substantive answer — small, self-liquidating, repaid in six years — is correct and not especially persuasive in a campaign. Two things make it defensible: **write the borrowing authority into the original statute**, so drawing on it is executing the plan rather than requesting a bailout, and **state the repayment schedule in law**, so the debt has a visible end date.

A severe recession, 4 points of unemployment, would mean $150–180B of borrowing and repayment stretching past 2047. Still solvent, but carrying fund debt for a decade — and a hostile Congress in that window could demand program cuts as the price of extending the authority. That is the scenario where the higher rate earns its cost.

## Drafting checklist

The provisions that decide whether this works, most of which would never surface in a markup.

### Formulas and indexing

- [ ] Name the **official poverty measure** explicitly for Title I allocations, so a concurrent expanded CTC cannot cut formula counts
- [ ] Write the Title I floor **per child** (\~$3,200, indexed to per-child wage costs), capped at 115% of the prior year, not as a fixed total
- [ ] Index the Title I floor to per-child wage costs, not CPI — at CPI it loses about 1.4% of real value a year
- [ ] Define IDEA and pre-K as **percentages of actual cost**, which makes them self-correcting against any indexing error
- [ ] Provide a survey-based alternative to direct certification for intra-district allocation, so a future SNAP restriction does not misallocate school money
- [ ] Write program weights (concentration, disability, English learners, mobility, rurality) so the formula survives a large fall in child poverty

### Revenue

- [ ] **Freeze the estate exemption in nominal terms** at 2009 parameters — worth $10B a year by 2039, $25B by 2049
- [ ] Guard against a future Congress quietly re-indexing it; this is the piece most likely to be amended first
- [ ] Keep the payroll contribution structurally separate from Title II of the Social Security Act, which reconciliation cannot touch
- [ ] Call it a contribution, not a tax, and name the fund for what it buys

### Fund mechanics

- [ ] **Statutory borrowing authority with the repayment schedule written in law**, so a draw is executing the plan rather than requesting a bailout
- [ ] Credit surpluses to the fund rather than scoring them as general deficit reduction, or the reserve exists only on paper
- [ ] Write the solvency test as a **ten-year projection, not an annual balance** — a fund designed to spend down through the build-out will trip any conventional annual test
- [ ] No automatic exhaustion rule: Social Security's automatic cut is tolerable on a 75-year horizon, but here it means a mid-year cut to teacher payroll
- [ ] Bar transfers out of the fund for any non-program purpose, with no deficit-reduction exception
- [ ] Publish **coverage rates alongside the reserve ratio**, so a balance that grows because states exited cannot be read as success
- [ ] Make stabilization junior to the passive draw and net it against automatic increases already received

### Program design

- [ ] Align expiration dates across pre-K, child care, and the Title I floor, so any sunset produces one fight with a broad coalition
- [ ] Tie the pre-K locality-grant bypass to the step-down years, when state exit risk peaks
- [ ] Put the restraint and seclusion condition on IDEA's **incremental** funds, not the whole grant, and keep the private right of action out of reconciliation
- [ ] Fund the restraint condition (training, positive behavior supports) so it has a scoreable cost and survives Byrd
- [ ] Every tier 2 surplus use carries a defined endpoint and creates no per-child or per-staff entitlement
- [ ] Update the meal reimbursement rates to the Senate figures; as drafted the bill cuts per-meal payments
- [ ] Fix the migrant education and charter program levels, the duplicate section numbers, and the FY2026 start dates

## Open questions and weakest assumptions

**The demographic path is a policy variable, not a forecast.** Net immigration swung from roughly 2.8 million in 2023 to negative in 2025 within two years, and a 2029 trifecta would likely move it again. Everything in the model that depends on cohort size — per-child amounts, the pace of the surplus, the step-down arithmetic — rests on an assumption that could move 10 points in either direction. Per-child formulas are the hedge; this is the input least worth betting on.

**Pre-K uptake is guessed.** State participation drives federal cost, coverage, and the fund's apparent health, and the 8–12 non-participating states in the model are an estimate with no strong basis. State exit during the step-down would make the fund look *healthier* while children lose seats, which is why coverage reporting belongs in the statute.

**Cost overruns are not modeled.** A realistic version runs 10–15% over and lands on a lower compromise rate. At 1.2 points with overruns, the fund covers 85–90% of program costs and needs general fund transfers by the early 2040s. That is survivable: the Highway Trust Fund has absorbed roughly $250B in general revenue since 2008 while continuing to build roads. What has to survive is the **structure** — mandatory spending, permanent appropriation, per-child formulas, a defined federal share — not the balance. A rate 0.15 points light is an arithmetic problem with an obvious fix, and fixing a popular program's financing is among the easier votes available; the 1983 Social Security amendments passed under worse conditions.

**The front-loaded reserve is worth defending for a narrower reason than solvency.** It is what gets the program through 2033–37 without an emergency vote, when it is least established and most vulnerable to being characterized as a failure. After 2040 the fund can run thin and it mostly does not matter.

### Questions left open

- **Federal resource standards.** Writing the program against what it buys would make it legible and demographics-proof, but any federal statement of what a school should *have* implies a view of what a school should *do*, and opponents would collapse the two. The narrower alternative — fund the weights, let the per-child amount be negotiated rather than derived from an adequacy model — gives up legibility to keep the 2015 ESSA peace. Not resolved.
- **Sequencing against an expanded CTC.** Both are defensible; combined they are 2.3–2.5 points of payroll equivalent, which is the real constraint. The CTC acts faster on poverty; the education fund is harder to restart once abandoned.
- **Whether the teacher floor should stay temporary.** Modeled as sunsetting after 2037, which leaves the floor to 50 legislatures with a CPI-indexed target that stops binding by the early 2040s. Making it permanent costs about 0.05 points.
- **The political reading of success.** If SPM child poverty falls to 5–6%, Title I will be defended against a success story told in a different measure than the one that sizes it. Worth having the answer ready before it is asked.
